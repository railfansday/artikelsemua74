2026 Build of the Year: A House for People to Love

It has been revealed that designed by Love Schack Architecture, Fine Homebuilding’s 2026 Build of the Year combines the rustic character of historic National Park Service ranger cabins with a compact, functional three-bedroom layout tailored to the couple’s lifestyle and long-term goals. Its simple footprint, custom wood siding, salvaged materials, passive solar strategies, triple-glazed windows, continuous insulation, and airtight, vapor-open building assemblies help reduce construction complexity, energy costs, and moisture risks while improving durability and comfort. Although built on a modest budget, the home demonstrates how thoughtful design, energy-efficient heat pumps, balanced ventilation, and close collaboration among the architects, builder, and homeowners can create a beautiful, healthy, and sustainable home.

Architects, builders, and the innovative manufacturers whose products enable their work genuinely impress me. I delight in the creativity and ingenuity of a wall of glass that literally disappears and removes the distinction between what is inside and what is outside a home. But when it comes to design, engineering, and construction, little surprises me anymore. We’ve proven what’s possible. Sadly, the gap between what is possible and what is probable seems to be widening.

Sources indicate that custom homes are becoming less attainable, and the rising costs of building a home are only one of the challenges. There is also the rising cost of owning and operating a home, as well as the climate impact of all building and remodeling projects, which we could only truly mitigate by not building or remodeling at all. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

Industry observers note that so while I continue to marvel at the work of architects and builders who push the limits of our design and engineering potential, I’ve become fond of a manifesto paraphrased from a former colleague: Build small and simple, when possible, and build houses that people will love. By “small” I mean right-sized, smarter instead of larger, and with economy in mind. (Related: The Reformer - Pushing the limits of language modeling...)

Recent findings suggest that by “simple” I also mean economically, and minimizing the potential for failure. And by “people will love,” I mean that by including personal details that fit the homeowners’ tastes and lifestyles, they hopefully will cherish and care for the place for a long time to come.

The intention for our Build of the Year is to give us the freedom to choose any project that inspires, for any reason. Unlike our other awards, this category is open to shipping containers and houseboats. This year, however, the house we chose checks all of the more sensible boxes described above.

According to recent reports, love Schack Architecture’s Ranger House packs three bedrooms and two baths into an efficient floor proposal and a compact, nearly rectangular footprint. It has a high-­performance enclosure and incorporates passive solar strategies in the design. And it embodies a very particular regional vernacular: the historic style of the Park Service’s ranger cabins that are scattered across the Mountain West, a style that the homeowners, Will and Angela, have long admired. Moreover, it fits into the existing landscape, a piece of land that has been in Will’s family for some time and was already home to pole barns, log cabins, and other rustic structures.

Industry observers note that “We knew we wanted to be here forever,” Angela told me. “So we wanted to build something that we’d love and that would serve us in terms of our lifestyle but also our energy bills and the changing climate. These were the kind of things that we considered before deciding to build.”.

Industry observers note that don’t be fooled by the rustic charm—this house is made for modern living. The kitchen is designed for cooking and connection, with a view to the dining and living areas and island, where family and friends can keep the cook firm. The primary bathroom has a double-sink vanity and a separate tub and walk-in shower.

If it weren’t for a small bump-out that creates space for an entry vestibule on the main floor and a bedroom closet on the lower level, this house would be a simple rectangle. Right around the size of an average home today, the house manages to comfortably fit an open great room, a primary bedroom suite, a powder room, and a generous mudroom on the main floor. Below are two more bedrooms, another full bath, the laundry, and a very special “pajama room”—an out-of-the-way space for the family to play games, watch movies, and more.

Sources indicate that the first time I saw this house was in an Instagram post—and to be honest, I was a bit confused. In the foreground of the photos is an old rancher’s fence, and in the back an old pole barn. Both looked like they’d been part of this landscape for a long time, as did the house that sat between them. Its foundation was clad in steel that wore its rusted patina well. Its green siding had an outsized exposure and more texture than is common with today’s man-made siding materials. The wraparound porch’s railing appeared to be far from code compliant. There was no landscaping to speak of. But the social media post was from the team at Love Schack Architecture, so I should have known that everything I saw was intentional.

“Out here in the West, you can rent an old Park Service cabin to stay in,” Andrea Michael told me. Andrea led the design team for the Ranger House. “They are very simple, really small buildings with a gable roof and a couple of bunk beds inside. They usually have a woodstove, but often no plumbing. Every once in a while one might have an electric range, but that’s pretty rare. So it’s basically like a camping cabin. When Will and Angela noted that the style they were after was ‘ranger-y,’ it’s these cabins that they were talking about.”.

Industry observers note that one of the key details for the design team to get right was the siding, because Park Service cabins are often built from logs, while this house is stick-framed. So they milled their own wood siding to mimic the scale, texture, and shadowlines of a log cabin. The unique siding detail has courses of 1×8 boards, each spaced with a chamfered 2×3. At the corners, the siding is mitered, and the house has no trim at the windows and doors. The scale and texture created by the siding is decidedly rustic, and yet the house maintains a clean, minimal look. Builder Mike Conner worked with the design team to refine the custom siding details, and with the homeowners to develop the green stain color they wanted.

Other details that contribute to the rustic look are the steel cladding at the foundation, which protects a layer of exterior rigid foam that insulates the concrete stem walls; the exposed rafter tails on the porch roof; and the cable-rail infill on the porch railing. You may think of cable rail as a more modern choice—and it was used in this case because it doesn’t block the view from the house down a long valley—but to me, the way it visually disappears makes the house like something that could not have been built in modern times.

Industry observers note that inside, the house is a bit more eclectic, with a serendipitous mix of salvaged and sourced materials that were thoughtfully incorporated into the design. A woodstove was a must, of course. But finding the right woodstove for a home with an energy-efficient enclosure can be a challenge. In an airtight home, a woodstove needs a source of dedicated combustion air. In a well-insulated home, the stove can’t be too big or the house will overheat. The stove they chose is from Kuru.

Sources indicate that in other places, Will and Angela were more open to material options and willing to be opportunistic when they had a chance to save some money. The pine flooring and some of the tiles were leftovers from other projects, whose owners were more than willing to give the material away provided Will and Angela were willing to pick it up swiftly.

According to recent reports, “The wood flooring in the house is actually one of the more magical pieces of this story,” Andrea told me. “We had another client at the time who had some wood flooring at his home and for whatever reason didn’t want it. It was very nice, but he decided not to install it, and he just needed it out of the property that he was renovating. He told me someone could have it if they wanted it.”.

Even the plaster detail that is found on the range-hood fan surround and on the headboard wall in the primary bedroom is the result of an opportunity the couple seized. The plaster was done for free as part of a workshop offered by Regenerative Building Solutions for people interested in learning to plaster.

It has been revealed that and like the exterior, there are some details inside that keep the look slightly modern and clean. These include drywall returns at the windows instead of trim, for example. The kitchen cabinets are frame­less and have simple Shaker-style doors and drawer fronts. On the back wall of the kitchen there are no upper cabinets. The wall is tiled from the counter up to the ceiling, with a simple floating shelf above the window.

Sources indicate that working with a tight budget, the owners were happy to source free wood and tile flooring, both left over from other people’s projects. The plaster features also added detail to the interior at no cost. The work was done as part of a plastering workshop held by a local organization that specializes in natural building materials.

Set on a hillside and looking down a long valley, the south side of the house is full of windows. That’s where the views are, and the house’s main living area—an open space that includes the kitchen and dining/living room—is oriented in that direction.

“My husband and I love food,” Angela said. “We were both raised in families that are completely all about food. We’re always talking two meals ahead, even as we’re eating a meal. So our kitchen and main living space were really key to us. And we wanted to maximize the view that we have, because we knew we’d be spending all our time in the kitchen and in that main living area.”.

The house also has a generous mudroom entry on the north side that opens into the kitchen, and a front door that opens into the living area. Though it’s not a full mudroom, the front entry has a vestibule with a space for a bench so guests can sit down and kick off their boots before entering the house. This small entry bump-out is the only irregular part of the otherwise rectangular footprint.

Also on the main floor is the primary bedroom suite. It includes a small bedroom with a headboard wall that orients the bed toward more large, south-facing windows. The bathroom has a double-sink vanity and separate tub and walk-in shower. Instead of a closet, the bedroom suite has a wall full of built-in cabinetry.

Industry observers note that it’s not quite accurate to call the lower level a basement, since the sloping site means that the south side of the lower level is all above grade. This meant that the design team could use the lower level for crucial spaces and still give them generous windows. Besides another full bath and the laundry and mechanical room, there are two additional bedrooms on the lower level. The entry bump-out helps make this work. Downstairs, it gives one of the bedrooms its closet. Also on the lower level is a room that I have never seen on a floor strategy before—and neither had the design team, until they met Angela.

“They introduced us to a term we had never heard before,” said Andrea. “They wanted what Angela’s family calls a ‘pajama room.’ It’s the room where you have your TV. It’s like a den or a family room, but it must be out of view of the front door. It’s where you can play games and just be a family. That was a clear directive in the design.”.

After the main living area upstairs, the pajama room is the largest room in the house. It too has views down the valley.

One way high-performance building assemblies conserve energy is by limiting unwanted air movement through the enclosure—preventing conditioned air from escaping and outdoor air from infiltrating, which can waste energy and make a home less comfortable.

It has been revealed that with insulation, they also stop the flow of heat, from the interior to the exterior in the winter, and the opposite in the summer, which saves heating and cooling energy. But experts have learned that assemblies with less energy flowing through them (heat and air) than the uninsulated and leaky homes of the past can be risky, because if the walls get wet, it’s not as easy for them to dry.

For this reason, many high-performance designers and builders, including the team at Love Schack that designed this home, prefer vapor-open assemblies. “Vapor-open” material allows water vapor to pass. If water vapor can pass through a wall (or roof assembly), it can dry. A material’s vapor permeance is rated in perms, and the lower the number, the less vapor that can pass. The plywood or OSB sheathing in a wall is often the lowest-perm material in the assembly, and one of the most common materials to get wet. To mitigate the risk of rot or mold, it’s a good idea if everything to the inside and outside of the sheathing is vapor-open.

Ready for the Weather: The house’s water-resistive barrier also serves as the primary air barrier on the walls while still allowing the walls to dry.

In this project, the design team chose two vapor-open insulation types—cellulose and wood fiber—and made sure to use membranes for air-sealing that allow vapor to readily pass through. On the walls, the primary water-resistive and air barrier is Pro Clima’s Mento 1000, which is vapor-open with a perm rating of 38. The ceiling air barrier is Pro Clima’s Intello Plus, which has a variable permeance. It is a class II vapor retarder in winter conditions, when it slows outward vapor drive, and it becomes vapor-open in summer when it is advantageous to allow more drying.

A simple building with simple shapes offers the potential for a more economical building, and a more durable one. Every jog in the building and every valley in the roof costs money and creates a more vulnerable area for the water- and air-control layers. High-performance enclosures are already more expensive to build and trickier to detail right, so there’s a lot to like about a simple shape and simple roof. This house has both—and on the south and east elevations, another energy-saving strategy where the deep porch roof shelters the walls.

In a recent development, “When we did the energy modeling on the house, we wanted to see how the covered deck impacted solar heat gain,” Love Schack’s Lindsay Schack mentioned. “While the house doesn’t get a ton of solar heat gain in the winter, that was less of a concern than making sure that the house was shading the sun and staying cool in the summer.”.

The house’s windows are from Fenstur. They are triple-glazed, all-wood, tilt-turn units. While Andrea stated that it is more common for them to spec aluminum-clad wood windows for their low-maintenance advantage, Fenstur provides a maintenance plan and a finish touchup kit with their windows. The windows are installed at the plane of the sheathing with exterior extension jambs to account for the 31/2 in. of exterior wood-fiberboard insulation.

It has been revealed that exterior continuous insulation has a few advantages, including minimizing potential heat loss from thermal bridging and keeping the house’s sheathing warm to prevent wetting from condensation. Wood fiber in particular is a vapor-open material, which has the additional benefit of allowing the wall to dry to the exterior should the wall get wet. For the same reason, the house also has a vapor-open water-resistive barrier and air-control layer—Pro Clima’s Mento 1000—and cellulose insulation in the stud bays.

Industry observers note that the exterior air barrier connects over the top plate to the ceiling inside, where the team chose Intello Plus for air control. They insulated the ceilings with blown-in cellulose. Below the ceiling air barrier, strapping was installed to create a chase to run cables and install light fixtures. The roof is framed with raised-heel trusses, so there was plenty of room for insulation and ventilation baffles.

Industry observers note that the foundation walls are also insulated on the exterior, in this case with 4-in. extruded polystyrene (EPS), which you’ll also find beneath the slab. The slab is thermally decoupled from the foundation walls with a 1-in. strip of EPS foam.

While these assemblies are becoming one of a few standard options that high-performance designers and builders prefer for foundations, walls, and roofs, high-performance designers and builders are still a small crowd. Mike Conner had not built a house like this before, but Andrea remarked she appreciated that he did not push back on anything, that he was happy to learn, and that it was often his experience that helped them determine the best way to execute certain details. It also helped that the clients were hands-on.

In a recent development, “They were very involved during construction,” Andrea stated. “Because they already lived on-site, they were there every day and were even willing to pick up some work on the weekends, cleaning up or doing anything else they could to help the contractor out.” Angela and Will also did all of the painting. That goes a long way when you’re working with a modest budget.

Industry observers note that the Love Schack team has tackled many passive house projects. Though they didn’t pursue certification, I doubt it would have been difficult to get this house across the finish line. Along with all the energy conservation the assemblies afford, the house is heated and cooled with heat pumps and has a balanced ventilation system to provide healthy indoor air, which makes me think I should add one more thing to my manifesto: Build houses that are as healthy for people and for the planet as possible. This project checks that box too.

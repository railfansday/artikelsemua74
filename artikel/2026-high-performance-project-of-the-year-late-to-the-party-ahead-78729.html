2026 High-Performance Project of the Year: Late to the Party, Ahead of the Code

Targa Homes took the completed plans for a code-compliant home and transformed them into Fine Homebuilding’s High-Performance Project of the Year by improving air-sealing, insulation, windows, and mechanical systems without requiring a major redesign. Key upgrades included a continuous air barrier that achieved an impressive airtightness of 0.40 ACH50, an externally insulated roof assembly to reduce thermal bridging and moisture risks, a carefully designed heat-pump, and an energy-recovery ventilation system. The project demonstrates that even when builders inherit conventional plans, thoughtful detailing, innovative construction techniques, and careful coordination can deliver exceptional energy efficiency, comfort, and durability.

Industry observers note that when Sean Conta of Targa Homes was hired to build a latest custom home in Seattle, the plans already met the building code. The engineering was complete and permits were only days from being issued. The owners had originally planned to work with another builder, but because they intended to live in the house long-term, performance and durability became more important than simply meeting code. Looking for an elevated level of construction, they turned to Targa Homes. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

The project included two structures: a 2600-sq.-ft. custom home for the owner and a detached accessory dwelling unit (DADU) to be used as a rental. When Targa came on board, the problem was that many of the decisions that normally go into designing a high-performance home had already been made. Targa looked for opportunities to improve the performance of both buildings without disrupting the plans.

In a recent development, targa Homes has two Certified Passive House Builder (CPHB) team members, and the firm is a partner in the U.S. Department of Energy’s Efficient New Homes program. Every project Targa builds is designed as a low-load, high-performance building. As Sean puts it, “I don’t do normal. This is what we do 100% of the time.” The challenge here was figuring out which improvements could still be made without sending the project back for a major redesign. Typically a home’s air barrier, insulation, structure, and mechanical systems are determined during the design phase. With this project, the team couldn’t move framing to simplify an air-barrier connection or change the structure to make room for ductwork without blowing up the plan. That’s what made the house stand out to us when we selected it as our High-Performance Project of the Year; the finished house is impressive, but many of the decisions that make it high performance weren’t part of the original design. Targa inherited a conventional, code-compliant design that was nearly ready to build and found opportunities to improve it without starting over.

The 2600-sq.-ft. Craftsman includes four bedrooms and three-and-a-half baths spread over four levels. Its tuck-under garage, sloped ceilings, kneewalls, and dormers gave the team plenty to work through. Some improvements required different products, but many came down to better planning and detailing within the existing design.

Spread across four levels, the floor plan maximizes usable living space within a complex footprint featuring sloped ceilings, kneewalls, and a tuck-under garage. To keep interior volumes open, the main living level relies on a ceiling-recessed ductless unit, while top-floor dormers are conditioned by a single wall-mounted head. On the second floor, small-diameter ductwork was threaded through existing framing and custom soffits, supplying multiple compact bedrooms without sacrificing headroom or square footage.

The original looks didn’t identify a dedicated air barrier or include details for maintaining its continuity. Instead, the project relied on Washington’s minimum code requirements, which don’t necessarily require enhanced airtightness. Developing a continuous air barrier became one of the team’s first priorities. Every Targa project targets passive house–level airtightness (0.6 ACH50 or less), which is a challenge to achieve on a project that has already been designed.

The air barrier is only as good as its weakest connection. Large sections of wall are easy to seal; it’s the transitions (where the floor meets the walls, where the walls meet the roof, at corners, and around penetrations) where most air leaks occur. On this house, the first challenge was separating the tuck-under garage from the conditioned living space.

The taped subfloor serves as the floor air barrier, which must then connect to the Zip System wall sheathing. The detail had to be simple enough that the framing crew didn’t have to stop and think about sequencing every wall. The solution was a bulb-style EPDM gasket from Conservation Technology stapled along the outside edge of the subfloor. As the walls were raised, the Zip sheathing compressed the gasket, creating a continuous seal without adding extra steps for the framers.

One inherited detail proved especially frustrating. Instead of framing the garage stair wall directly on top of the concrete foundation, the looks located the framed wall just inside the foundation wall. The arrangement may have made sense structurally, but it created an inaccessible cavity that made air-sealing extremely difficult.

The problem didn’t have an elegant solution. During preliminary blower-door testing, the team chased leaks from the interior using smoke and systematically foamed and caulked every crack they could reach. “It was just a thousand cuts,” Sean said, calling it “warfare.”.

The plywood shear wall became the primary air barrier where accessible, while the spaces around the TJI floor framing, electrical panel, rim joists, and other transitions required painstaking handwork.

Industry observers note that the large, uninterrupted wall areas were relatively straightforward. Sheathing joints were taped and rolled, while every planned penetration—including windows, doors, lighting, electrical boxes, and heat-pump line sets—received its own combination of tape, gaskets, or sealant.

Recent findings suggest that the windows were another opportunity for improvement. Rather than the builder-grade units shown on the plans, the owner chose Pivot Windows’ uPVC tilt-turn windows with a high-performance U-factor of 0.16. Most of the glazing has a solar heat-gain coefficient (SHGC) of 0.54, meaning it allows 54% of the sun’s heat through the glass. Several south-facing windows use lower-SHGC glass—0.32—to limit summer solar gain and reduce the risk of overheating.

Industry observers note that the windows’ higher price initially gave the owner pause, but Sean said that changed after he brought sample windows to the site. Once the owner saw and operated them, he was sold on the upgrade. Each unit was carefully integrated with the Zip System sheathing for a continuous air barrier around the rough opening.

Industry observers note that the most difficult areas to seal were the transitions where the walls meet the roof. Multiple dormers, intersecting rooflines, and changing ceiling slopes created dozens of potential leakage paths. An overframed roof assembly, as described in the next section, simplified those connections.

It has been revealed that mid-construction blower-door testing confirmed the air-sealing details were working. Remaining leaks—including a few around the stair enclosure—were easy to locate and seal before insulation was installed. The final test measured just 0.40 ACH50, demonstrating that exceptional airtightness results from treating every transition in the enclosure as part of one continuous air barrier.

The complicated roofline was already part of the design when Sean joined the project. Dormers, sloped ceilings, kneewalls, and multiple roof intersections needed to stay as designed, but the way the roof was built could change. The original prepares called for an unvented roof insulated with closed-cell spray foam beneath the roof sheathing. Instead of relying on spray foam, the new assembly has continuous exterior insulation on top of Monopoly framing.

Named after the small green houses from the Monopoly board game, this unvented assembly addresses several high-­performance goals at once. Continuous insulation above the structural roof deck reduces thermal bridging, while moving the air barrier to the sheathing layer simplifies one of the most difficult parts of constructing an airtight house. The roof is framed without overhangs, allowing the Zip System wall and roof sheathing to meet directly. With the sheathing exposed, every wall-to-roof transition can be taped and inspected from the exterior before the roof assembly becomes more complicated. Once the air barrier is complete, the overhangs are framed, continuous rigid insulation is installed above the structural roof deck, and a second layer of roof sheathing provides a nailbase for the finished roofing.

According to recent reports, one of the biggest construction advantages of the assembly is the sequencing. The air barrier can be completed while every connection is accessible, eliminating much of the awkward detailing that typically occurs around soffits and eaves or where walls and roofs meet. In addition, ceiling penetrations such as lights or ducts remain inside the air barrier.

For Sean, the most vital advantage is moisture control. The rigid insulation keeps the structural roof sheathing above the dew point, eliminating the cold condensing surface within a roof assembly. With closed-cell spray foam, gaps between the foam and sheathing can leave the surface vulnerable. Exterior insulation avoids that concern while maintaining a thinner, more proportional eave profile.

The main house and the DADU sit next to each other in the same climate and were built by the same team—but the constraints led to two different roof assemblies. On the main house, there was room to build outward. On the DADU, nearly everything had to fit within the depth already available.

According to recent reports, on the main house, the roof is framed without overhangs so the Zip System wall and roof sheathing can meet directly, simplifying the air-barrier connection at the eaves; this detail is sometimes called Monopoly framing. Continuous insulation is then installed above the structural roof deck, followed by the overhangs and a second roof deck. This reduces thermal bridging and keeps the structural sheathing warm.

The DADU was already close to Seattle’s maximum allowable height, leaving little room to add the same exterior insulation package used on the main house. Instead, the team used traditionally framed overhanging rafters. Fiberglass-insulated TJI cavities are covered with a membrane, followed by 2×4 furring that creates a vent channel beneath the Zip System roof sheathing. The primary air barrier is a separate Mento membrane on the interior side of the TJIs. The assembly was inspired by one presented by fellow Pacific Northwest builder and regular FHB contributor Josh Salinger (see “A Latest Take on Insulating a Roof,” FHB #299).

One of the biggest concerns when reviewing the prepares wasn’t the framing or insulation—it was something missing from the drawings. The construction documents contained almost no information about the mechanical systems: no load calculations, no duct layouts, and no plan for routing refrigerant line sets or ventilation ductwork.

According to recent reports, the project team hired Energy Vanguard, an HVAC design manufacturer based in Atlanta, to complete the load calculations, equipment selection, duct layout, and ventilation proposal. The structural design was already complete and couldn’t be changed, so routing remained a challenge. In a few locations, the structural engineer provided specifications that allowed ducts to pass through beams; otherwise, the mechanical design had to work around the existing structure.

Small-diameter (3-in.) Rheia ducting made it easier to route the heating and cooling supply ducts through the existing framing, but Sean still spent many hours drilling holes, fishing ducts, and framing soffits to make it work.

The completed system combines ducted and ductless Mitsubishi heat pumps. A ducted air handler serves the second floor, where multiple small bedrooms make individual wall-mounted units impractical. The main floor uses a ceiling-recessed ductless unit, while the third floor is conditioned by a single wall-mounted head. The combination provides efficient comfort without filling every room with its own head.

It has been revealed that ventilation is handled by a dedicated Broan ERV operating on a completely separate 4-in. duct system. Rather than tying fresh-air distribution into the heating and cooling ducts, the ERV continuously supplies and exhausts air through its own balanced network. The builder fabricated custom supply and return manifolds to simplify installation and balance the airflow.

Industry observers note that the main house’s compact, vertically organized floor proposal makes efficient use of the site, but the garage, dormers, sloped ceilings, kneewalls, and numerous wall-to-roof transitions created tough air-sealing and insulation details. The air barrier needs to move continuously from the foundation and framed garage wall through the floor assembly, up the walls, across the roof, and back to the foundation.

In a recent development, by the time Targa Homes came on board, this house was already designed, engineered, and nearly permitted. It could have been built exactly as shown on the drawings. Instead, the new project team looked for opportunities to improve performance without starting over. Many of those improvements would have been easier to incorporate had the builder been involved before the plans were finished, but good builders sometimes inherit less-than-ideal details.

“There will be compromises, but you can still achieve a very high-performance home, even if it’s not shown on the plans, and even if the client isn’t explicitly asking for it,” Sean noted. That’s the biggest lesson to learn from this project. High-performance construction doesn’t always begin with a blank sheet of paper. Sometimes it means looking at a conventional set of plans and asking one simple question: Can this be built better?

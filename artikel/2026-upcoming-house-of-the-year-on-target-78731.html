In a recent development, this custom, Phius-certified passive house in Massachusetts—Fine Homebuilding’s Fresh House of the Year—combines Scandinavian modern design with traditional New England architectural elements. Designed by ZeroEnergy Design, the home prioritizes energy efficiency, low embodied carbon, and aging in place while incorporating features such as abundant natural light, specialized hobby spaces, and an 18-meter basement archery range. Using sustainable materials, high-performance insulation, triple-pane windows, rooftop solar panels, heat pumps, and battery storage, the all-electric home achieves net-zero energy use while providing exceptional year-round comfort.

By the time I met Chip and Marie Fein, they were sitting in the living room of this house. They described their decision to relocate from Latest Jersey to Massachusetts to be closer to two of their children who were already living in the area and another who was likely headed this way as well. They were sure they wanted to be near family. The property they’d found to build on, however, gave them pause.

While the natural landscape was picturesque, the property had been for sale for six years and had two dilapidated houses on it as well as unsalvageable barns, sheds, a miscellaneous outbuilding, and a garage. It also came with an abandoned boat and a semi-trailer that would never see the open water or the open road again. But they did buy the property, and the only things that they knew had to remain were two mature sugar maples.

Recent findings suggest that no one needs to build an architect-designed custom home, so the Feins’ journey, marked by life-changing decisions and a property that gave them pause, also offered them a gift: time. In that time, they began to consider what they really wanted in a home and promptly narrowed their focus to the singular priority of energy efficiency. Recent findings suggest that you might imagine what happened next: The Feins decided to take the well-worn path to a passive house. Chip spent his career around construction, and to him, both the requirements and the process of designing and building a certified passive house made great sense. Marie also had some ideas in mind. She wanted a spacious interior in a “Scandinavian modern” style, she said, which I understand as minimal, neutral, and functional, with touches of texture and lots of daylight. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

Recent findings suggest that the final step was choosing ZeroEnergy Design, the Massachusetts architecture firm whose approach to passive house and modern design best aligned with the couple’s goals. Long-time Fine Homebuilding readers will be familiar with architect Stephanie Horowitz and engineer Jordan Goldman. We’ve published their work before, and they’ve presented at our events. The project architect on this home was a newer member of the team, Noah Fitch, who helped Chip and Marie develop and refine a complete program for the home. The plan included the ability for the couple to age gracefully in the house, and it would result not just in low operating energy but in low embodied carbon too. Chip and Marie also gave Noah an unusual request: The basement had to have room for an 18-meter archery range.

Sources indicate that the homeowners were less interested in a specific style than in creating an interior that felt spacious and light. In addition, they wanted to be able to “see the weather.” This was realized in the great room with a tall, vaulted ceiling and a featured window wall.

In the bathroom tub alcove, a generously sized window frames a specific view of one of the property’s mature maple trees. Naturally finished maple is used throughout the interior to create warmth and texture—including in the tray ceiling over the tub—to conceal the range hood in the kitchen, and as part of a lighting strategy over the kitchen island.

In a recent development, you won’t have to look too hard at the plans to guess where the archery range is located. The single-story house has two long axes. The axis that stretches west to east includes all the public spaces, beginning from the entry, through the kitchen and the dining and living area, and out onto the screened porch. The axis that runs from the southern to the northern end of the house keeps the private spaces private. These include the primary bedroom suite and a guest bedroom and bathroom. (In the basement, there’s another guest bedroom and bathroom.).

Industry observers note that “Marie is a professional archer, and so we had to figure out a way to give her the length that she needed for a regulation archery range,” Noah told me. “We didn’t want to just have a really long house, so the two linear spaces sort of ended up acting like a Venn diagram, and the shared part is what creates the space we needed.”.

Recent findings suggest that in many ways, this home’s floor initiative is ubiquitous today: a great room with an adjacent outdoor living space, and a primary suite with a neat arrangement of bedroom, bathroom, and closet. But Noah added several nuances that make this house a bit more thoughtful, and they start at the main entry.

Most houses and the people who live in them benefit from a mudroom at the most-used entry. A dedicated place where shoes, coats, umbrellas, and more can be stored out of the way makes good sense. Still, it won’t be used by everyone who visits the house, and a mudroom doesn’t always make for the most elegant of entries. Noah decided to put the mudroom immediately inside the door, but off to the left as you enter. So you don’t have to use it or even see it if it is not needed.

He also located the pantry off the short entry hall that leads to the great room. It’s immediately outside of the kitchen; however, groceries and other items headed from the entry to the pantry only need to travel a short path that avoids the kitchen altogether. The entry hall is far from squeezed, but combined with its lower, flat ceiling, it creates a dramatic reveal as you walk toward and into the great room, which has a vaulted ceiling, large windows, lots of light, and plenty of views.

Sources indicate that when I asked Chip his favorite part of the house, he joked that it was the garage. Cars are his hobby, and intentional spaces for hobbies are crucial in a home designed for aging in place and retirement. Not only is Marie an archer, but she also knits and does other crafts. Including a small craft room was an obvious choice. Its location is brilliant, buffering the great room from the bedrooms. A jog in the bedroom wing means that the hallway wraps around the craft room. In this way, the home’s private spaces are even more private than most.

This house has everything today’s homeowners want, including an open-plan great room and a primary bedroom suite on the main floor.

In a recent development, it also has all the things that make a house convenient for modern living, like an entryway mudroom with a dog-washing station, a main-floor laundry, and even a room dedicated to crafts. One surprisingly interesting detail is the two hallways.

Sources indicate that the first, at the entry, is uninterrupted by the mudroom and pantry, which are tucked discreetly to the side. This creates a clear, inviting path from the front door to the exciting reveal of the vaulted, sun-filled great room. The other hall wraps around the craft room, visually separating the public living area from the bedrooms and bathrooms—a nice buffer for the home’s private spaces.

If you imagine the exterior of this home from what you see on the floor plans, you might think of a sprawling California ranch. But the exterior tells a different story. As the house extends out over the length of each wing, it does have low-slope, standing-seam shed roofs, reminiscent of a ranch, but the exterior walls are taller around the great room, providing the vaulted ceiling and the sense of spaciousness that Marie wanted inside and a chance for Noah to reflect New England’s architectural traditions outside. This part of the house has a simple gable roof and both vertical wood siding—as found on all the old barns scattered throughout the area—and horizontal lap siding, the traditional material used on the area’s original Colonial homes. And there are lots of windows.

Recent findings suggest that “I just kept going back to gigantic windows,” Marie said as she explained her vision for the house. “I kept telling Noah that I want my house to have a warm glow. When we’ve been out for the night and we’re approaching the house, I want it to have this welcoming, warm glow. And during the day when we’re inside, I want to see the weather. I want to see if it’s sunny. I want to see when it’s raining. I want to see when it’s snowing. I want to see the animals that come on the property.”.

Recent findings suggest that not only did Noah achieve this goal in the great room, but he found opportunities to use windows to capture light and views in more subtle ways too. The window over the tub in Chip and Marie’s bathroom is a striking example. It is used to frame a view of one of the more spectacular trees on the property.

It has been revealed that in fact, this small bathroom alcove is indicative of how Noah imparted style throughout the interior. Above the tub is a modern take on a tray ceiling, with warm, natural maple slats. Next to the tub, a similar wood-slat detail extends a half-wall to the ceiling. Together they define a special space. After all, bathrooms today are more about experience than they are about hygiene.

Sources indicate that similar maple details are also found in the kitchen, where they are used above the range and above the island and sometimes incorporate lighting. While a warm glow from the outside was important to Marie, Noah wanted to create warm glows inside the house and used a variety of reflected LED lighting for that effect.

“In addition to a more functional, layered lighting strategy,” he mentioned, “I wanted to make sure that we provided some more ambient, reflective, nonrecessed lighting that would create a soft glow inside the house at night, particularly in a few key spaces.”.

Nearly every material or design detail I asked Noah about was intentional and carefully considered. The blue yakisugi (shou sugi ban) porch ceilings, for example, are an ancient Japanese material that is still novel in North America. But the wood wasn’t chosen because it is trendy, or simply for its durability. It was also a nod to traditional New England farmhouses, where porch ceilings were typically painted light blue. Another example is the well that Noah designed for the basement bedroom’s egress window. He decided to turn what is often an eyesore into an opportunity by making it large enough to be seen from above, through a first-floor window, and planted a garden inside the space. The effect, he said, is that out of this first-floor window you’d feel like you are looking down from a second story. The well meets egress requirements with a very cool custom-designed escape ladder.

The goals for the project were passive-house levels of performance, which requires nearly airtight assemblies, insulation strategies that mitigate thermal bridging, and reduced embodied carbon—achieved by favoring carbon-sequestering materials and limiting those that are carbon-intensive. Here’s a look at some of those strategies.

According to recent reports, less Portland Cement: The concrete foundation walls were engineered to minimize the amount of Portland cement used and are only a full 10 in. thick where they carry point loads to the footings.

Slabless Crawlspace: In the crawlspace, there is no concrete slab, just a vapor retarder installed over rigid-foam insulation and a floating OSB floor.

Insulated Headers: Headers were spaced with rigid-foam insulation to help mitigate thermal bridging above windows and doors.

Timber-Filled Roof: The truss-framed roofs are all insulated with blown-in wood-fiber insulation, a carbon-sequestering option.

Exterior Insulation: The walls all have rigid wood-fiber insulation outside of the sheathing to mitigate thermal bridging. Wood fiber is also vapor open, allowing these walls to dry in both directions.

Airtight Ceiling: The ceiling air barriers have utility chases created within the conditioned enclosure, so electrical and duct runs do not penetrate the air-control layer, which can require tricky air-sealing details.

Construction photos courtesy of Noah Fitch.

When I’m considering houses to publish, a thing I like about a Phius-certified passive house is that I don’t need to see any modeling or energy bills to know it is a legitimate low-load house. Phius requires modeling, but also third-party testing and commissioning. I do like to know how a design team achieved low energy use, and in this case, low embodied carbon. It turns out that it all started with the foundation, which was engineered to use less concrete.

In some places—for example, below patios—the concrete stem walls are as narrow as 6 in., although in most places they are 8 in. thick. And only where they need to carry point loads are the foundation walls 10 in. thick. Where a full-height basement wasn’t needed, Noah designed a crawlspace and skipped the concrete slab, opting for a vapor retarder, rigid-foam insulation, and a floating floor. For the concrete they did use, he specified a high percentage of fly ash, which limited the use of Portland cement.

In the finished basement, the concrete slab is insulated with rigid foam and is thermally isolated from the foundation wall. The concrete walls are insulated with rigid foam and fiberglass-batt-filled stud walls. The above-grade walls are stick-framed and filled with dense-pack wood fiber from TimberHP. Outboard of the sheathing, there is 4 in. of Steico—rigid wood-fiber insulation boards—and the siding is installed on furring strips to create a ventilated rainscreen.

“It’s a vapor-open wall assembly, which is great,” Noah said when discussing the choice to use wood fiber. “It’s a nontoxic material. It’s carbon-storing. It’s safe to install. It’s safe to live around. It’s sort of a dream assembly for a lot of reasons.”.

Noah even considered resource efficiency when designing the dimensions of the rooms. If a space could be spanned by I-joists and trusses, it would be unnecessary to use steel beams or any extra materials to build the house. The truss roofs are insulated with blown-in wood fiber.

Both the vertical and the lap siding are factory-finished, thermally modified spruce from Lunawood. All the windows and doors are triple-panes from Makrowin. They are aluminum clad on the exterior and clear-finished European spruce on the interior.

There’s more to like about passive houses and low-load houses in general, including that they make it easy to achieve zero-energy use with minimal power generation, which this house has done with rooftop solar. Chip and Marie are thrilled that they are producing enough electricity to cover their all-electric house, which has ducted air-source heat pumps, a heat-pump water heater, a Zehnder ERV, and a couple of whole-house batteries to store emergency power. What they like even more is how comfortable the house is. This is the only house they’ve ever lived in where they can walk around barefoot year-round, even by the windows, and not feel cold.

Sources indicate that I end interviews about projects like this with the same question, and I usually get the same answer. And I’m often skeptical of that answer. But when I asked Chip and Marie if, in hindsight, they would have done anything differently, and Chip replied confidently and rapidly, “Not a thing,” I believed him.

It has been revealed that while the screened porch was designed to be a three-season space, the screen panels can be removed and replaced with glass panels. The homeowners report using this porch year-round and say that if the sun is shining, the porch remains warm and comfortable. The blue-colored yakisugi used for the porch ceilings is a nod to traditional farmhouse porches, which were often decorated with beadboard painted light blue.

Recent findings suggest that the house has two custom-designed ladders. In the craft room, a ladder leads to a loft that overlooks the great room through interior windows, a space created specifically for the homeowners’ grandchildren. Another ladder was designed and built to offer an emergency escape route from the window well outside the basement bedroom’s egress window, an otherwise utilitarian space that the designer turned into a visual feature.

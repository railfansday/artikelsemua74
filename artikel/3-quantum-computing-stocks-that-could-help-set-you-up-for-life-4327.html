Your laptop is pretty smart; it can use binary processing to execute instructions that let it edit video and beat you at chess. But ask it to model exactly how a new drug molecule grabs onto a protein, or how lithium moves through a better battery, and those complicated tasks that involve quantum physics overwhelm it. Ordinary computers are lousy at faking natural processes. (Related: How to train a upcoming language model from scratch usi...)

The desire to solve these complex problems is the basis behind the pitch for quantum computing. A regular bit is a coin lying flat: heads or tails, a binary option. A qubit is a coin still spinning, looking at multiple options simultaneously, and a few hundred spinning coins linked together can calculate more possibilities than any supercomputer could list. The trouble is that spinning coins tend to fall over at the slightest bump. Error correction advances take bundles of lots of wobbly qubits so they act like one steady one, but nobody has got it to work effectively at scale yet. (Related: Tech Report: Introducing Optimum: The Optimization Tool...)

So quantum computing is still in its early stages of development. Really early. But the competitors in this race aren't all on the same clock. If you want to invest in the potential of this technology, I'd build a long-term stake from three small bets based on now, soon, and later.

D-Wave Quantum (QBTS -1.64%) builds annealers, quantum machines tuned to pick the best answer from a ridiculous pile of options. Think delivery routes, factory schedules, or which cell towers track your phone.

That last one isn't hypothetical. NTT DOCOMO, Japan's largest mobile carrier, put a second D-Wave-powered tool into everyday use, cutting one kind of peak network chatter by about two-thirds. D-Wave also delivered Italy's first annealing quantum computer and signed a deal for up to $100 million in federal CHIPS Act funding, paid out as it hits milestones. Florida Atlantic University researchers even used its machines to help predict lung cancer from small patient data sets.

The business is still tiny. Third-quarter revenue was just $4.5 million, upcoming orders shrank from a year ago, and D-Wave lost $48.2 million. Its cash burn is manageable for now. It's also building a general-purpose gate-model machine, with Spanish bank BBVA testing its simulator. D-Wave is my "now" bet, a quarterly check on whether customers will pay for quantum today.

NASDAQ · As of 9:57 AM ET 11/20/26.

IonQ (IONQ -1.72%) makes qubits from single charged atoms steered by lasers. It's also been shopping. After buying chipmaker SkyWater over the summer, it picked up Elisity, a network security firm, to sell cybersecurity tools for the day quantum machines can crack today's encryption.

The machines are selling. Florida International University and a South Korean company each ordered IonQ's new Superion 256 system, and Nvidia (NVDA -3.43%) plans to put one in its own quantum research center. IonQ also reached the final stage of a Pentagon research agency's quantum benchmarking program, potentially worth up to $300 million.

According to recent reports, all that growth has a price tag. IonQ lost $133.6 million on an adjusted basis last quarter, then raised $2 billion in October by selling latest shares, leaving about $3.7 billion, more than any other pure play. Every new share slices your piece of the pie thinner. IonQ is my "soon" bet and the trio's core, as long as orders keep becoming delivered machines.

NYSE · As of 9:56 AM ET 11/20/26.

Microsoft (MSFT +0.77%) picked the hardest road. Its Majorana chips use an exotic state of matter meant to make qubits naturally resistant to errors. In June, it said Majorana 2's qubits stay stable 1,000 times longer than those of its first chip, and it pushed back its target for a scalable quantum computer to 2029.

NASDAQ · As of 9:59 AM ET 11/20/26.

Plenty of physicists aren't sold and have criticized Microsoft for not sharing enough data to check its claims. It isn't betting on one horse, though. Magne, a machine built with Atom Computing for a Danish customer, pairs Atom's hardware with Microsoft's error-correction software and is due early next year.

Recent findings suggest that quantum won't move this stock for years. Its cloud business, Azure, grew revenue 48% last quarter. Microsoft is my "later" bet, worth holding even if quantum takes 20 years.

Calvin and Hobbes contains multitudes as a fantastic comic strip from the 1980s and 1990s, with Bill Watterson's work still celebrated over 30 years since it concluded. It's loaded with approachable philosophical insights and emotional nuance, while told largely through the eyes of a chaotic, complicated young Calvin accompanied by his stuffed tiger, Hobbes. While Calvin has plenty of his own high and low points, though, the sometimes underappreciated aspects of living around or even raising a complex young kid can show itself in occasional instances.

Susie Derkins has certainly been expressive about the difficulties of growing up with Calvin as a classmate and friend, but as for family, it can sometimes show in more bite-sized moments. Calvin's parents, naturally, face each little detail head-on, from general mischief, to house damages, to occasional reminders that, while they invest their lives in raising him, he may amusingly not always realize the work that goes into parenting. But for one November 8, 1993 Calvin and Hobbes strip, Calvin attempts to present himself and his parents' work as a point of pride to his mother, with the comic hilariously cutting to her subsequent reaction. In a recent development, the scenario is incredibly simple for this two-panel strip: Calvin steps in while his mother works on some household errands at the table, simply saying he's her "massive accomplishment in life!" This is immediately followed by the father arriving home from work to see mom, sunken into the couch, simply remarking, "I'm depressed." It's effective, incredibly concise, and a funny, lighthearted jab at how parents might occasionally feel like everything they sink into raising their kid might not have been enough. Calvin's an expressive, individualistic young kid with a wild imagination and a heart for mayhem with little responsibility, but he's also, crucially, still a kid, which his parents obviously acknowledge. In a recent development, it's not that Calvin's mom regrets having him, although moments like this are rare blips of regret to which any parent can relate. It's entirely human to have small moments of doubt in oneself as a parent, especially since Calvin's mother is a stay-at-home mom, so any doubts in raising her boy as the "significant accomplishment" of her lifetime are perfectly reasonable. But it's pretty clear from how she's written throughout Calvin and Hobbes that the mother is a proud parent, guilty only of being overwhelmed and exasperated by her son's antics. The best part is, the week surrounding this particular strip alone is loaded with fun moments featuring her.

It has been revealed that in the very next strip released on November 9, 1993, Calvin and Hobbes introduced one such type of incident that would understandably overwhelm all sorts of veteran parents. The strip shows Calvin talking to his mother as she goes through his laundry, having taken in some sort of scary critter, erroneously keeping it in his pockets, and being concerned that she didn't find it, implying it's loose somewhere in the home.

The mom understandably grows concerned by Calvin's carelessness, but never outright takes it out on him. Yet, when unprovoked in other strips in that same week, this same character can happily poke fun at her son when tensions are low, her comedic timing staying on-point while not at any point hurting her child's feelings with her wit.

This is the sign of an effective parent who, while never fully adjusted to the whimsy of raising Calvin, shows enough poise at the right moments with him, while channeling any doubts away from him. Calvin's mom is still prone to completely understandable reactions to her son's most foolish actions. But kids are allowed to be loud, annoying, and irresponsible, while parents are allowed to feel overwhelmed or doubt themselves, on the assumption that it doesn't consume them or harm their bonds with their children.

Recent findings suggest that parents should also be honest with themselves, while being strategic with their hilarious or embarrassing but ultimately harmless truths about their kids, such as confirming that Calvin was, indeed, a very stinky baby. Calvin and Hobbes may largely be about life as a kid, but it also encompasses what goes into raising that kid with love, devotion, and some lighthearted, teasing when the moment calls for it.

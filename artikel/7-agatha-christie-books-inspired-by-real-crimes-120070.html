Agatha Christie did not write true crime. Instead, her talent was imagining complex and compelling puzzles surrounding a murder. Several of them were inspired by experiences from her own life, such as working as a nurse during WWI or traveling through the Middle East, accompanying her archeologist second husband to excavation sites. But not even the Queen of Crime could avoid the coverage of real crimes in newspapers and media. Although the stories and characters are always her own, some of Christie's most famous murder mysteries take inspiration from real-life cases. It has been revealed that many real crimes and criminals are explicitly mentioned in Agatha Christie's novels and short stories. "Remember the long-continued successes of Jack the Ripper," Hercule Poirot tells Captain Arthur Hastings in the 1936 novel The ABC Murders. It is often believed the story of the Victorian serial killer inspired the plot of the book. Christie also held a deep fascination for the 1892 Lizzie Borden case, and the rhyme inspired by it is recited in After the Funeral. Other times, Christie did more than just borrow the general idea of a crime, and incorporated specific details related to a murder for her stories. Here are some of the most fascinating cases.

Recent findings suggest that in 1916, during World War I, Agatha Christie was working in a hospital dispensary and was bored. Christie decided to use this time to write a detective novel. "Since I was surrounded by poisons, perhaps it was natural that death by poisoning should be the method I selected," Christie herself wrote in her autobiography. Inspired by the detective book tradition, she created her own iconic investigator. He would be Belgian, like many of the refugees she was interacting with. His name would be Hercule Poirot.

The Mysterious Affair at Styles was inspired by not one but two real murder cases. The fictional death of wealthy Emily Inglethorp at her Essex country house, Styles Court, has many parallels to that of Frances Garnett Orme. The 49-year-old spiritualist was found dead from poison in her locked room in the Savoy Hotel in Mussoorie, India, in 1911. Orme's companion was the main suspect. The book also echoes the story of William Palmer, an English doctor convicted in 1856 of poisoning his friend, John Cook, with strychnine, the same poison Agatha Christie uses in the book. It was suspected that Palmer had also killed several members of his family, including four of his children. In addition to the murder method, Agatha Christie's novel also includes several key details from the Palmer case, such as coffee left in a hallway, an inquest at the village inn and a pharmacist who sold the poison thinking it would be used to kill a dog. Billie Carleton was a British musical comedy actress in WWI London. By 18, she was a star. Four years later, and days after the end of the war, Carleton was found dead of an apparent drug overdose. The night before, she had attended the Victory Ball at the Royal Albert Hall dressed in a scandalous semi-transparent dress designed by her friend Reggie de Veulle. An investigation found De Veulle had provided Carleton with the substances and revealed he had been part of a secret underground London cocaine trafficking ring. He was sentenced to eight months in prison.

Agatha Christie's The Affair at the Victory Ball, one of the first Hercule Poirot short stories, takes readers to the inside of British high society. It is set during this luxurious celebration of the end of the war, which a group of people attended dressed in the costumes of the Italian commedia dell’arte. During the ball, Lord Cronshaw, dressed as Harlequin, is stabbed to death. The morning after the ball, his fiancée, actress Coco Courtnay, is found dead in her bed from a cocaine overdose. Christie never remarked the story was based on Billie Carleton’s death, but the details speak for themselves.

According to recent reports, on the evening of 31 January 1910, Cora Crippen, an extravagant music hall singer, disappeared from her London home. Her husband, the quiet American homeopath Hawley Harvey Crippen, claimed she had left him and moved to the U.S. Soon after, his mistress, Ethel Le Neve, moved into his house and began wearing Mrs Crippen's clothes and jewelry. When the case caught the police's attention, Dr. Crippen and Le Neve fled on a ship to Canada, with Le Neve disguised as a boy. However, the ship's captain recognized the pair and notified his superiors using wireless telegraphy. It was the first time this technology was used to track a criminal. In their absence, the police had searched the home and found human remains. Police officers then jumped onto a faster ship and chased the doctor and his mistress. They were arrested, and Dr. Crippen was found guilty of the murder. Le Neve was acquitted and later changed her name to distance herself from the case.

The story of the Crippen murder made international headlines, and even caught Agatha Christie's eye. She borrowed many details from the crime for her witty novel Mrs. McGinty's Dead, in which James Bentley is convicted of killing the elderly Mrs. McGinty. Hercule Poirot is asked to take on the case. The trail leads him to a newspaper clipping Mrs. McGinty kept that profiles women connected to notorious murder cases. One of them is Eva Kane, the mistress in the fictional "Craig case". Like Le Neve, Kane was found not guilty of the murder of her lover's wife. In the book, Kane changes her name and leaves the country. Her story is the key to the case.

It has been revealed that the Mousetrap still holds the record for the longest-running play in the world. The production, which first opened in 1952, is the same that can be seen today in London's West End. It is based on a radio play written by Agatha Christie as a birthday present for Queen Mary, titled Three Blind Mice. Christie then expanded the plot into a short story, but she requested that it not be published until the play had closed. Since The Mousetrap is still running, this has not happened yet in the UK, although the story has been published in the U.S.

In a recent development, both The Mousetrap and Three Blind Mice are based on the same case, the tragic death of 12-year-old Dennis O'Neill in 1945. Little Dennis and his younger brother, Terence (Terry) O'Neill, aged 9, were placed in foster care and sent to live with Reginald and Esther Gough on a remote English farm. Under their care, the children were beaten and barely fed. Less than a year later, Dennis died after being struck on the chest and beaten. The couple was charged with manslaughter and neglect in a grueling case made worse when young Terry was asked to act as a witness in the couple's trial. Terry's testimony angered the whole country and helped bring about the Children Act of 1948. The case became a clear inspiration for Agatha Christie's story, in which three fictional foster care children are sent to live in Longridge Farm, where one of them dies. Years later, one of their siblings takes revenge.

The second Poirot story Agatha Christie wrote takes the detective to France. When he and Captain Hastings arrive, they are met with the news that the man they came to see, Paul Renauld, has died. His widow, Eloise Renauld, claims a group of masked men broke into her villa in the middle of the night, tied her up to the bed, and dragged her husband to the golf course, where they stabbed him to death. The investigation takes a turn when a second body is discovered, killed in a nearly identical way.

In a recent development, the setup of Murder on the Links follows that of one of France's most notorious murder trials. In May 1908, Marguerite Steinheil was found gagged and bound to a bed. Like Mrs. Renauld, Marguerite claimed she was attacked by intruders who killed her mother and husband, the painter Adolphe Steinheil. Marguerite was already infamous in Paris. Years before, she had been the mistress of French President Félix Faure, who had died in her arms after suffering a stroke. Marguerite tried to frame several people for the murder of her family members, but she was found out. It was widely believed that she herself had staged the intrusion to murder her husband. With no proof to back the accusation, a French jury acquitted Marguerite Stenheil in 1909 after an extremely sensationalistic trial.

Just as with a good murder-mystery, a list can take a turn. In this case, it is worth highlighting The Pale Horse as the only Agatha Christie book that inspired a crime, helped solve another, and saved several lives. In the novel, Mrs Davis gives her last confession to Father Gorman along with a list of names. Shortly after, the priest is murdered, and the people on the list begin dying from what seem to be natural causes.

At the heart of this book is a poison. It is made from an odorless heavy metal called thallium, which, if ingested in small doses, can cause symptoms such as hair loss and gastrointestinal distress that doctors routinely misdiagnose. The poison is said to have inspired Graham Young, known as "The Teacup Poisoner". In 1972, Young was accused of lacing his coworkers' tea and coffee with thallium and antimony, killing two colleagues. Some accounts say Young was caught thanks to a doctor who had read Agatha Christie's novel and became suspicious. Thallium was also the method used by George Trepal to poison his neighbors, the Carr family, killing Peggy Carr. An undercover detective who had been in Trepal's home recalled seeing a copy of The Pale Horse.

Recent findings suggest that agatha Christie's book also saved lives. In 1975, more than a decade after the novel was published, Christie received a letter from a reader in South America who had noticed a friend suffering from unexplained hair loss and pain. The reader remembered the plot of The Pale Horse and alerted the woman's physicians, who confirmed she had suffered thallium poisoning. Two years later, a one-year-old Qatari girl was flown to London. She was in a coma caused by an illness no doctors could diagnose. It was Marsha Maitland, a nurse in the hospital who had been reading The Pale Horse, who suggested thallium poisoning. The test came back positive, allowing doctors to find the treatment that saved the toddler's life.

It has been revealed that in the book, Agatha Christie aims to give a sense of justice to a real-life tragic crime. In 1932, 20-month-old Charles Lindbergh Jr, was taken from his crib in his New Jersey home. He was the son of the most famous aviator in the world, whose name he shared. The authors of the crime requested a $50,000 ransom. The parents paid. Soon after, the baby's body was found in the woods near the house. Weeks later, a young maid, Violet Sharpe, killed herself after being questioned by the police as a suspect. The crime was so famous it led to the passing of the Federal Kidnapping Act (the "Little Lindbergh Law"). Agatha Christie's fictional Armstrong case reproduces the Lindbergh case almost point for point. Except, of course, real crimes are rarely clear-cut. In 1934, a carpenter named Bruno Richard Hauptmann was arrested for the crime. Hauptmann was executed in 1936, although his guilt continues to be called into question even today.

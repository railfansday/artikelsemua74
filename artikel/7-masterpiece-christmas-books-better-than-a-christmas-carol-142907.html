Every Christmas, we turn to the same stories. The same movies the grandparents like, and the song that plays every single Christmas Eve so that everyone in the family can sing along. It is comforting to have stories to return to. Many of the traditions we return to were actually cemented by none other than Charles Dickens. A Christmas Carol is the most popular, having been adapted hundreds of times. Dickens cemented Christmas as we know it today, but that doesn't mean there aren't other Christmas stories worth knowing.

During this time of year, it is normal to gravitate toward stories with deeper meaning that reflect on what one is grateful for. Some books have achieved this with incredible mastery. Here are the best.

In a recent development, claire Keegan's novella Small Things like These is quietly heartbreaking. Set in Ireland in 1985, it follows Bill Furlong, a coal and fuel merchant. The week before Christmas, he goes about his routine while thinking about what presents to get his wife and daughters. One day, he visits a local convent and is surprised to find a young woman named Sarah locked in a nearby shed and asking about a baby. He frees her and leaves her with the Mother Superior, who says she was playing a game. The girl reminds Bill of his mother, who was an unmarried teenager when she got pregnant with him. Over the following days, Bill cannot shake the suspicion that something terrible is going on in the convent.

It has been revealed that this story shows that Christmas stories do not need to be wrapped in a pretty bow. It is also possible to read something themed to the season that deals with ethical questions. In the case of Small Things like These, Keegan discusses the difficult topic of Ireland's Magdalene laundries, where young women were imprisoned and forced to do labor. The subject is treated with the dignity and care it deserves, showing how a single person can choose to do what is right in the face of larger injustices, even when the cost is high.

Truman Capote's "A Christmas Memory" is set in rural Alabama in the 1930s. It was inspired by Capote's own life and his experience of the holiday season in a family with financial difficulties. The short story is beautiful, warm, funny, and deep. It will also absolutely break your heart.

“It's fruitcake weather!” Buddy's elderly cousin reveals, declaring the start of winter. The relationship between the seven-year-old boy and his cousin is the heart of Capote's story. They are two childlike, wide-eyed people in a stern and strict family. During the story, they gather the ingredients to make fruitcakes and share them with friends. Years later, Buddy looks back at the simple magic of those shared Christmases. The prose is as beautiful as the story, and it is worth reading, if only for the last few lines. The cousin character is mentioned to have been inspired by Sook, a relative the author lived with as a boy, who may have had intellectual disabilities. Capote called her "a genius.".

A Bigger Business Isn’t Automatically a Better One. Here’s What to Ask Before Scaling.

I’ve spent 13 years building a business. It’s long enough to figure out what works, and definitely long enough to get a lot of things wrong. When we were five people in a 300-square-foot office, our team tripled in size in a year. Our client base and revenue grew just as promptly. At some point, I genuinely started to believe we were the gods of consulting. As it turned out, we were simply very good at running a small manufacturer. In a recent development, if a team of five can become 10 by working harder and talking more, it’s tempting to assume that 10 can become 50 the same way. But growth is not multiplication. A organization of eight people and a company of 30 are not the same business at different sizes. They are two different organizations that require two different ways of managing.

In its early days, a small firm can get by on instinct, proximity and a good deal of improvisation just because, as a founder, you are involved in nearly everything. You know the clients, notice the work, answer questions and make decisions often before anyone has had time to schedule a meeting about them.

Industry observers note that for me, the hardest part wasn’t letting go of tasks. It was realizing that the quality of my business now depended on decisions I wasn’t making, and sometimes didn’t even know were being made.

This is a transition I didn’t handle perfectly. One of my wrong calls was appointing people to management roles because they were excellent specialists. It seemed reasonable at the time, but expertise in consulting doesn’t automatically equip someone to lead a team or resolve a conflict. The difference becomes significant when the business depends on managers to preserve quality without the founder standing nearby.

I am convinced that a company must become less dependent on its founder at precisely the moment the founder may feel most protective of it. That requires more than delegating tasks. I’d say develop leaders who can exercise judgment, establish standards that don’t live solely in the founder’s memory and distribute authority with enough clarity. Without it, founders often remain the final decision-makers, even after building several layers of management.

Industry observers note that if you recognize your business in this stage of growth, Ichak Adizes’s model of the firm life cycle offers a useful way to understand this transition. The entrepreneurial “Go-Go” stage, with its energy and instinct for opportunity, eventually gives way to “Adolescence,” when informal habits must yield to professional management and clearer authority.

These growing pains don’t necessarily mean you’re running a bad business. On the contrary, they may simply mean you’ve outgrown the way you used to run it. This model gives founders a way to understand why the habits that helped a young manufacturer move promptly may no longer serve the organization as it grows.

One of the easiest traps to fall into when scaling is that you can become so busy building the company that you forget who you’re building it for. You can look at your dashboard, see rising revenue and new sign-ups, and think you’re winning. Meanwhile, right under your nose, the quality your loyal clients are getting is quietly tanking.

It has been revealed that it’s incredibly easy to lose sight of the customer when your calendar is consumed by recruitment and endless alignments. You end up stuck in back-to-back meetings talking about structure, while your actual clients are dealing with junior staff who don’t fully understand their business yet.

Ask yourself, when was the last time you personally spoke to a client not to sell them something, but to understand whether your company is still delivering the value they expect?

As a business grows, the cost of complexity grows with it. More employees mean more coordination, more management responsibilities and more opportunities for processes to break down. Before committing to another expansion push, it’s worth looking at the reality of the market.

OECD figures show that among SMEs with 10 to 249 employees, 8% to 14% qualified as employment scalers in 2020, while 12% to 24% qualified as turnover scalers. The OECD also found that scalers were, on average, 20% more productive than other SMEs before their high-growth phase, with that advantage rising to 35% after three years.

What this reinforces for me is that expansion should never be an automatic next step. I try to keep that question front and center; otherwise, you can become so absorbed in perfecting the manufacturer’s internal structure that you stop thinking about the actual person using your product.

Not every business needs to become bigger. Sometimes scaling creates value. Sometimes it simply creates overhead. More employees do not automatically mean more success. More markets do not automatically mean more opportunities. And higher revenue does not necessarily mean a healthier business.

It has been revealed that in specialist consulting, a focused team of 10 can be more profitable and resilient than an organization of 50. In fintech, entering another country may be strategically sound, or it may add cost and complexity without improving the customer proposition.

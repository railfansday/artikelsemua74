Sources indicate that metabolic disorders such as diabetes mellitus are global health crises driven by metabolic imbalance, chronic inflammation and dysfunction of the neuroimmune axis1,2,3. Electroceuticals targeting the autonomic nervous system have shown anti-inflammatory effects, yet existing systems lack the metabolic interoceptive feedback to fully restore immune–metabolic homeostasis4,5,6,7,8,9. Here we report an artificial neuroimmune axis (NeuroAxis) as a closed-loop immunomodulatory therapy for treating metabolic diseases. Analogous to its biological counterpart, NeuroAxis is solely powered by endogenous glucose, operating without batteries or wireless power transfer. It emulates the vagal reflex through closed-loop neuromodulation with metabolic interoceptive feedback. In a rat model of type 1 diabetes, NeuroAxis promotes the recovery of insulin-producing pancreatic β-cells and reduces inflammatory immune responses; in a rat model of type 2 diabetes, it drives macrophage remodelling and reduces systemic chronic inflammation. In both models, NeuroAxis restores glucose tolerance, improves insulin sensitivity and normalizes glycaemic control, with therapeutic effects persisting after treatment cessation. Our artificial neuroimmune axis promotes substantial advances in treating metabolic disorders and represents a general approach for autonomously regulating interorgan communication, central to homeostasis and pathophysiology.

According to recent reports, diabetes mellitus and associated metabolic disorders are major public health challenges, affecting more than 14% of the total adult population worldwide10,11. Beyond hyperglycaemia, these conditions are often accompanied by chronic inflammation and impaired coordination between the nervous, immune and metabolic systems, resulting in maladaptive autonomic responses and an impaired neuroimmune axis3,10,11,12. In particular, the vagus nerve, a bidirectional conduit linking afferent metabolic signals to efferent autonomic output, critically governs metabolic tone12. Under hyperglycaemic conditions, parasympathetic vagal activation reduces hepatic glucose output and increases pancreatic insulin secretion6,13. Moreover, vagal signalling modulates immune function through the inflammatory reflex, whereby efferent vagus nerve activity can suppress inflammation by attenuating pro-inflammatory cytokine release across diverse conditions2,14,15,16. Preclinical and clinical evidence indicates that electrical stimulation of the vagus nerve can suppress systemic inflammation, improve insulin sensitivity and augment pancreatic β-cell activity6,12,13. Despite the neuroimmune axis being a promising therapeutic target for diabetes mellitus17, developing a therapeutic strategy to restore its impaired function remains a daunting challenge. The challenge arises from the lack of afferent vagal nerve functions in real-time sensing of metabolic cues (for example, glucose concentration). Recent advances in artificial nerves have led to the restoration of the impaired sensorimotor loop. However, the artificial afferent nerves are designed to sense physical input (for example, pressure) from the environment, rather than metabolic signals within the body18,19. Similarly, feedback with indirect surrogate biomarkers, such as heart rate variability, gastric motility or electroencephalographic activity, is unable to restore the neuroimmune axis4,5,20,21,22, resulting in suboptimal clinical outcomes and adverse effects such as bradycardia or gastrointestinal symptoms5,23,24. Moreover, continuous electrochemical sensing in implantable electronics introduces additional system-level challenges, including sustained electrical biasing, low-noise signal amplification and wireless data transmission25,26, which collectively increase energy demand and system complexity, thereby limiting the development of compact, long-term and self-sustained closed-loop neuromodulation systems. Recent findings suggest that to address these challenges, we developed NeuroAxis, which uses glucose as both an endogenous energy source and an interoceptive metabolic signal to restore the impaired neuroimmune axis. Inspired by how nerve cells metabolize glucose to generate the energy-carrying molecule adenosine triphosphate (ATP)27, NeuroAxis incorporates a glucose fuel cell (GFC) that oxidizes endogenous glucose to generate electrical energy for powering the entire system. Meanwhile, the glucose oxidation process also serves as metabolic interoceptive feedback to periodically infer metabolic states. The metabolic feedback is relayed to a logic circuit, which then autonomously regulates neuroeffector output through vagus nerve stimulation (VNS). The activation of this immunomodulatory circuit subsequently re-establishes metabolic homeostasis and ameliorates diabetes mellitus. Functionally, the GFC, logic circuit and morphing electrode recapitulate the afferent vagal nerve, brainstem and efferent vagal nerve, respectively, within the neuroimmune axis (Fig. 1a). In a type 2 diabetic rat model, the glucose-responsive NeuroAxis substantially improved glycaemic control, body weight regulation and immune–metabolic homeostasis. In a type 1 diabetic rat model, NeuroAxis alleviated the inflammatory immune response and enhanced β-cell activity, thereby facilitating the re-establishment of normal glycaemic homeostasis.

The bioinspired NeuroAxis consists of three core components: the GFC for metabolic feedback and energy input, a flexible printed circuit board (FPCB) for logic control and a soft morphing electrode for neural stimulation28 (Fig. 1b,c and Supplementary Fig. 1). At the system level, the self-regulating logic circuit continuously monitors glucose concentration through the terminal voltage of the GFC (Fig. 1d). The power management integrated circuit (PMIC) uses maximum power point tracking to optimize energy extraction from the GFC. Its integrated boost charger harvests and stores the energy in a capacitor. The on-chip buck converter provides a stable power supply for the microcontroller and peripherals when sufficient harvested power is available. Based on the glucose concentration, the logic circuit outputs a specific electrical stimulation pattern to the vagus nerve on the oesophagus through the morphing electrode. NeuroAxis was implanted subcutaneously into the rat abdominal region, with the GFC and FPCB placed into the abdominal subcutaneous space, while the morphing electrode was interfaced with the oesophageal vagus nerve (Fig. 1e).

Industry observers note that to realize the dual functions of sensing and powering, we designed a GFC in which the electrode was a porous, water-wicking carbon nanotube (CNT) electrode (Supplementary Fig. 2). The anode was decorated with Au nanoparticles (average diameter, 9.33 nm) on the CNT electrode (CNT-AuNP). These small-diameter Au nanoparticles exhibit glucose oxidase-like nanozyme activity, enabling catalytic oxidation of glucose to gluconate and generating an electrochemical current to power the control module29 (Extended Data Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3). At the cathode, a platinum-nanoparticle-coated CNT foam electrode (CNT-PtNP) can catalyse the reduction of oxygen and complete the redox cycle30,31. When immersed in glucose solution, CNT-AuNP and CNT-PtNP electrodes revealed distinct redox peaks, and the CNT-AuNP electrode generated a substantially larger current response to glucose than to the tested potential interferents (Fig. 2a–c and Supplementary Fig. 4). Both electrodes had b values (slope of log–log analysis of cyclic voltammetry) close to 0.5, indicating diffusion-controlled kinetics (Fig. 2d). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy showed a reduced transfer resistance after AuNP and PtNP modification, suggesting enhanced interfacial electron transfer (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Sources indicate that across the physiological and pathological glucose range (3–30 mM), the power density of the GFC exhibited a nearly linear increase with glucose concentration (Fig. 2e,f). The open-circuit voltage also revealed a higher response to glucose than to the tested potential interferents (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 6). The large electrochemical surface area of the porous structure yielded higher power density and higher open-circuit voltage compared with that of non-porous structure (Extended Data Fig. 2). Enzyme-mediated GFCs are costly and unstable in vivo32,33, retaining only around 0.5% of baseline power density after 30 day immersion in glucose solution. By contrast, our nanozyme-based GFC retained 79.7% of baseline power density after 31 days in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing glucose and retained 64.5% of baseline power density after 90 days in PBS containing glucose and 1% bovine serum albumin (Fig. 2h, Extended Data Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 7a). Moreover, accelerated ageing tests further indicated that the nanozyme-based GFC was estimated to retain approximately 50% of its initial power density after nearly 6 months (180 days) of incubation in PBS/glucose/bovine serum albumin solution (Supplementary Fig. 7b–e), indicating good stability of the nanozyme-based GFC.

To interface with the soft, deformable and dynamically moving oesophageal vagus nerve, we designed a low-impedance morphing electrode with a tissue-like Young’s modulus, high stretchability and intraoperative reconfigurability28 (Fig. 2i,j and Supplementary Fig. 8). The electrode comprises poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrene sulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS)/glycerol as a conductive layer and poly(dimethylsiloxane)-isophorone bisurea (PDMS-IU) and PDMS-isophorone bisurea-4,4′-methylenebis(phenyl urea) (PDMS-IU-MPU) as encapsulation layers34 (Supplementary Fig. 9). Its robust self-healing property (Supplementary Fig. 10) allowed the device to transform from a two-dimensional (2D) film into an enclosed loop around the oesophagus. Owing to its non-Newtonian behaviour, the morphing electrode can form a seamless interface with target tissues without previous knowledge of their size and morphology. Moreover, the morphing electrode maintained stable electrical performance over 1,000 expansion cycles (Fig. 2k,l and Supplementary Video 1) and 8 weeks of soaking in PBS (Supplementary Fig. 11).

According to recent reports, the biocompatibility of NeuroAxis was further evaluated in vitro. After 7 days of incubation, cell viability was comparable to that of the control (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). Moreover, haemolysis tests revealed a low haemolysis ratio after 12 h of incubation with rat red blood cells (Supplementary Fig. 14).

It has been revealed that neuroAxis monitors interstitial glucose concentration and initiates neuromodulatory stimulation only under sustained hyperglycaemia35. Every 30 min, the GFC terminal voltage is sampled by NeuroAxis and can be converted to estimated blood glucose concentration through a voltage-to-glucose calibration curve (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 15 and Supplementary Video 2). We use a glucose level of 11.1 mM (corresponding to an input voltage of 0.6 V) as a threshold for hyperglycaemia in diabetic rats36,37. To avoid activation by transient postprandial glucose fluctuation in healthy animals, which typically return to baseline in 2 h, electrical stimulation is triggered only when glucose levels are consistently above the threshold for five consecutive readings (Fig. 3b). When triggered, the system delivers 3 min of biphasic VNS before returning to the sensing state (Fig. 3c,d and Supplementary Video 3). At a glucose concentration of 7 mM, which corresponds to normoglycaemia in healthy rats, the GFC can achieve a glucose-to-energy conversion efficiency of approximately 3.43% and generate sufficient power that exceeds the FPCB’s power consumption, enabling sustained, battery-less operation for NeuroAxis (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17).

In a recent development, in vivo VNS elicited evoked compound action potentials with stereotyped waveforms after NeuroAxis implantation, which remained stable 1 month after implantation (amplitude of around 0.44 mV; Fig. 3e and Supplementary Figs. 18–20). Moreover, the evoked compound action potentials recorded from the cervical and abdominal vagus nerves, the increased FOS expression in the nodose ganglia and the elevated serum acetylcholine (ACh) levels collectively support that NeuroAxis stimulation simultaneously engages both the afferent and efferent vagal pathways (Supplementary Figs. 21 and 22). Open-field testing under stimulated and non-stimulated conditions highlighted comparable exploration without freezing, thigmotaxis or abnormal movements, and the total travel distance did not differ significantly between groups (Extended Data Fig. 4). Furthermore, X-ray and micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) confirmed stable placement and a conformal neural interface (Fig. 3f,g). NeuroAxis produced no measurable temperature increase during operation and caused no observable behavioural abnormalities, inflammatory responses or substantial alterations in serum biochemical and haematological parameters over 8 weeks of implantation (Fig. 3h,i, Extended Data Fig. 5 and Supplementary Figs. 23–25). Moreover, once the treatment was completed and the animals recovered to a stable condition, NeuroAxis could be readily explanted (Supplementary Fig. 26). These results suggest that NeuroAxis did not cause obvious adverse effects and was well tolerated in vivo.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is closely associated with insulin resistance, chronic obesity, low-grade inflammation and neuroendocrine dysfunction3,11. We first assessed the six-week therapeutic effects of NeuroAxis in an obesity-associated T2DM rat model12,13, assigning animals to four groups: healthy controls (group 1, G1); diabetic controls without implantation (G2); diabetic rats implanted with inactive NeuroAxis (G3); and diabetic rats implanted with a fully functional NeuroAxis (G4). Compared with rats in G2 and G3, rats in G4 showed sustained reductions in food intake and body weight (Fig. 4a,b), with visibly lower adiposity (Fig. 4c). Besides, blood glucose levels in G4 fell to near-healthy levels after treatment for 2 weeks, whereas hyperglycaemia persisted in G2 and G3 throughout the 6-week period (Fig. 4d,e). As further evidence of progressive glycaemic normalization, the GFC output voltage from NeuroAxis, which is closely associated with blood glucose concentration, gradually decreased in G4 during treatment (Extended Data Fig. 6).

Compared with G2 and G3, G4 had significantly lower fasting glucose and circulating insulin levels, resulting in lower Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) scores (Fig. 4f–h). Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance tests (GTTs) and insulin tolerance tests (ITTs) further corroborated metabolic restoration. At week 6, G4 exhibited substantially faster glucose clearance and enhanced insulin responsiveness compared with G2 and G3, with decreased glycaemic excursions and reduced areas under the curve (AUCs) for glucose response (Fig. 4i–l). Importantly, although NeuroAxis entered standby mode as glycaemia was restored by week 2, the therapeutic benefits persisted beyond 6 weeks. Moreover, NeuroAxis treatment significantly reduced adipocyte hypertrophy in inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT), epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) and interscapular brown adipose tissue (BAT), and alleviated the vacuole-like changes and hepatic lipid accumulation in the liver (Fig. 4m–p and Supplementary Fig. 27). Furthermore, immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining of pancreatic sections revealed enhanced insulin synthesis in G4 compared with G2, approaching the level observed in G1 (Extended Data Fig. 7). Collectively, our data suggest that NeuroAxis increases insulin sensitivity, normalizes glucose homeostasis, restores β-cell function and broadly mitigates metabolic dysfunction in obesity-associated T2DM with a persistent therapeutic effect.

It has been revealed that we also evaluated the long-term operational stability of NeuroAxis in vivo. In NeuroAxis-treated diabetic rats (G4), the proportion of operational devices after several weeks was mainly attributed to the normalization of blood glucose after effective treatment, together with the gradual reduction in GFC output. By contrast, because rats in the inactive NeuroAxis group (G3) remained hyperglycaemic throughout the study period, most devices in this group remained powered after long-term implantation for up to 90 days (Supplementary Fig. 28). Explanted G4 devices showed no obvious structural or circuit damage and could effectively deliver electrical stimulation output when exposed to higher glucose concentrations after 90 days of implantation (Supplementary Fig. 29). These results suggest that the NeuroAxis can operate effectively under hyperglycaemic conditions after 3 months of implantation. Longer-term applications will require further improvements in biofuel cell stability and adaptive threshold setting.

According to recent reports, recent advances in neuroimmunology have revealed that stimulation of the vagus nerve triggers the release of ACh, which binds to α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (α7nAChR) expressed on immune cells. The cholinergic signalling pathway can suppress obesity-associated inflammation by inhibiting the production of proinflammatory cytokines9,12,38,39. We therefore hypothesize that NeuroAxis can ameliorate T2DM by engaging α7nAChR signalling. To test our hypothesis, we included a fifth cohort (G5): diabetic rats implanted with an active NeuroAxis and given methyllycaconitine citrate (MLA), a selective α7nAChR antagonist, to pharmacologically block cholinergic anti-inflammatory signalling40,41 (Fig. 5a). G5 exhibited substantially poorer glycaemic control compared with G4, supporting the role of the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway in mediating the therapeutic benefit of NeuroAxis (Supplementary Fig. 30).

To investigate NeuroAxis-mediated immunoregulation, we evaluated the concentration of inflammatory cytokines and the infiltration of macrophages in adipose tissues. ACh levels were elevated in both G4 and G5, supporting the activation of the cholinergic pathway during NeuroAxis stimulation (Fig. 5b). However, only G4 had increased concentrations of the anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-10 (IL-10) in adipose tissues and decreased concentrations of the proinflammatory cytokines interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumour necrosis factor (TNF) in adipose tissues and serum (Fig. 5c–e and Supplementary Fig. 31). By contrast, the cytokine levels in G5 were comparable to G2, with a negligible immunoregulatory effect. Moreover, the flow cytometry analysis suggested that G4 had lower total macrophage infiltration and proinflammatory macrophage (M1-like macrophage) infiltration, and higher anti-inflammatory macrophage (M2-like macrophage) infiltration, compared with G2. Furthermore, the infiltration of lipid-associated macrophages was reduced only in G4 and not in G5 (Fig. 5f–j and Supplementary Fig. 32). Moreover, G5 had lower CD163 expression and higher CD86 expression in adipose tissue compared with G4 (Fig. 5k–m). The time-course analysis of macrophage phenotypes and inflammatory cytokines revealed that active NeuroAxis treatment induced an early shift from pro-inflammatory to anti-inflammatory macrophage profiles, accompanied by reduced local and systemic TNF and IL-1β levels from day 7 to day 21 (Supplementary Fig. 33). These effects were not observed in G2, G3 and G5, supporting the involvement of α7nAChR-mediated cholinergic signalling in inflammatory attenuation. These results suggest that inflammatory attenuation occurs early during NeuroAxis treatment and may contribute to the subsequent metabolic improvements. Furthermore, NeuroAxis treatment (G4) improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity in diabetic rats, as shown by reduced blood glucose excursions and lower AUC in both the GTT and ITT compared with G2 and G3 (Supplementary Fig. 34). These beneficial effects were substantially attenuated by MLA administration (G5), supporting the involvement of α7nAChR-mediated cholinergic signalling in NeuroAxis-induced metabolic regulation.

We next investigated the relative contributions of vagal afferent and efferent pathways to the therapeutic effects of NeuroAxis. NeuroAxis stimulation activates afferent vagal pathway projecting to central metabolic circuits, as evidenced by increased FOS expression in the hypothalamic regions involved in feeding regulation42 (Supplementary Fig. 35). Consistently, NeuroAxis-mediated VNS increased circulating concentrations of the satiety- and glucose-regulatory hormones cholecystokinin octapeptide-8 (CCK-8) and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), whereas vagotomy attenuated these effects, suggesting that these hormonal responses depend on intact vagal–brainstem connectivity42 (Supplementary Fig. 36). These results suggest that NeuroAxis engages a vagal afferent–brainstem axis to reduce food intake and regulate body weight. However, reduced food intake and body weight loss alone do not fully explain the metabolic benefits of NeuroAxis, because pair-feeding resulted in only modest improvements in glucose control (Supplementary Fig. 37). Moreover, NeuroAxis-treated rats that underwent vagotomy maintained better glycaemic control than those treated with MLA, despite showing less body weight reduction (Extended Data Fig. 8). These findings suggest that, relative to the vagal afferent pathway, the efferent α7nAChR-mediated cholinergic pathway is likely to be more closely linked to inflammation suppression, improved insulin sensitivity and improved glycaemic control. Moreover, metabolic cage analyses further indicated that NeuroAxis treatment restored the average respiratory exchange ratio towards healthy control levels, suggesting improved glucose utilization and metabolic flexibility in G4 rats43 (Supplementary Fig. 38). Collectively, vagal afferent signalling primarily regulates feeding and body weight, whereas α7nAChR-mediated cholinergic signalling mainly attenuates inflammation and promotes insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis.

In a recent development, to further assess the therapeutic effect on systemic metabolic dysfunction, we performed integrated transcriptomic–metabolomic profiling of liver tissue after 6 weeks of NeuroAxis treatment in T2DM rats. We observed broad reprogramming of hepatocellular functions in response to NeuroAxis therapy. Specifically, NeuroAxis treatment was associated with coordinated downregulation of pro-inflammatory genes (such as Nfkb1, Tnf, Il1b and Il6) and upregulation of insulin-signalling-related genes (such as Insr, Irs1, Pik3ca and Akt1). Consistent with these transcriptional changes, metabolomic analysis revealed alterations in glucose metabolism and mitochondrial function, including changes in glucose-6-phosphate, lactate, citrate and other tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates44. Circulating 1,5-anhydroglucitol, a marker inversely correlated with glucosuria due to reduced renal reabsorption, also increased after NeuroAxis stimulation, suggesting an overall improvement in glycaemic control45. Lipidomic profiling further revealed a shift from saturated and monohydroxy fatty acids (FAs) toward monounsaturated and polyunsaturated FAs. This shift is generally indicative of enhanced lipid use and potentially reduced lipotoxic stress13,46. Moreover, bile acid turnover and vitamin metabolism were modulated in the NeuroAxis group, consistent with partial restoration of hepatic metabolic homeostasis47. The integrated pathway mapping suggested that NeuroAxis treatment alleviates systemic chronic inflammation, restores PI3K–AKT pathway function to improve glucose uptake and metabolism, and promotes hepatic glycogenesis and mitochondrial activity, both of which are downstream targets of vagus nerve-mediated anti-inflammatory circuits12,48,49 (Fig. 5n,o and Extended Data Fig. 9a–e). These molecular reprogramming events are accompanied by systemic improvements in metabolic and inflammatory status in the NeuroAxis-treated group (G4), as evidenced by reduced circulating TNF, IL-1β, leptin, cholesterol, triglycerides and haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), alongside increased levels of ghrelin and adiponectin (Extended Data Fig. 9f–m).

NeuroAxis treatment attenuated inflammatory immune cell infiltration and activation associated with β-cell attack. It increased regulatory T (Treg) cells while reducing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in both spleen and pancreas, promoted M2-like macrophage infiltration in the pancreas and decreased CD45RA+ B cells and antigen-presenting dendritic cells (DCs) in the spleen (Fig. 6d–f and Extended Data Fig. 10). These changes were accompanied by elevated anti-inflammatory cytokines (TGF-β and IL-10) in the pancreas and reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF, IFNγ, IL-2 and IL-1β) in the pancreas and blood, together with lower pancreatic CD8 and CD86 expression (Fig. 6g,h and Supplementary Fig. 41), indicating suppressed inflammatory immune responses in the pancreatic microenvironment.

Moreover, representative immunofluorescence images showed direct evidence of increased BrdU+ β cells and restored β-cell mass in the pancreas in G4 compared with in G2 and G3. These data suggest that the proliferative activity of β cells is enhanced after NeuroAxis treatment (Fig. 6i,j), consistent with previous studies supporting a role for vagal nerve signalling in the regulation of β-cell proliferation6,51,52. Time-course insulin staining over a broader pancreatic area further showed that NeuroAxis treatment (G4) progressively restored insulin-positive β-cell mass in diabetic rats, with detectable improvement after 2 weeks and a substantial increase after 8 weeks compared with diabetic (G2) and inactive NeuroAxis controls (G3) (Supplementary Fig. 42). Consistently, histological analysis by haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining demonstrated improved islet structural integrity and cellular organization in G4 (Fig. 6k). The GTT results further showed that G4 exhibited a metabolic profile close to healthy controls (G1) (Fig. 6l,m). Together, these results indicate that NeuroAxis reshapes the streptozotocin-induced inflammatory pancreatic microenvironment, suppresses immune-cell infiltration and activation, and promotes β-cell proliferative recovery, thereby restoring glycaemic control.

By mimicking energy metabolism and signal transduction in biological systems, we conceived a fully autonomous artificial neuroimmune axis that harvests energy from endogenous glucose while transducing metabolic cues into vagal output. In contrast to existing vagus nerve stimulators4,20, NeuroAxis directly couples real-time fluctuations in blood glucose to self-regulated vagal stimulation, restoring the cholinergic anti-inflammatory reflex. The GFC simultaneously harvests metabolic energy and senses biochemical signals, enabling chemically responsive neuromodulation without battery or wireless power transfer.

By functionally recapitulating the autonomic interoceptive system, NeuroAxis exerts systemic effects on peripheral targets, including attenuating chronic inflammation, reducing inflammatory immune response, enhancing pancreatic β-cell activity and insulin sensitivity, normalizing glycaemic control and, ultimately, reestablishing metabolic and immune homeostasis. NeuroAxis remained functional under hyperglycaemic conditions after 3 months of implantation in rats, although further optimization of the GFC will be required to improve long-term operational stability for translational applications. By restoring the impaired neuroimmune axis, NeuroAxis provides a complementary strategy to conventional pharmacotherapies and opens a path towards personalized, metabolism-driven neuromodulation for immunometabolic disorders. More broadly, this intelligent bioelectronic system establishes a general framework for engineering artificial interoceptive circuits that restore interorgan communication and treat complex multisystem diseases.

The circuit integrates a BLE module (BC840, Fanstel), an energy harvester/power management integrated circuit (BQ25570, TI), two low-power digital-to-analogue converters (DACs) (MCP4725, Microchip Technology) and supporting passive components. The BC840 module, based on the Nordic nRF52840 system-on-chip (SoC), integrates a 64 MHz, 32 bit Arm Cortex M4F MCU, a 2.4 GHz multiprotocol transceiver, and an integrated PCB trace antenna, enabling compact wireless operation. It acquires GFC signals through its built-in successive approximation analogue-to-digital converter (SAADC) and transmits data to a smartphone through BLE, with the SAADC shut down when idle to reduce power consumption. The BQ25570 harvests energy from the GFC, boosts the input voltage and stores the harvested energy in a capacitor, then provides a regulated power supply for the whole system. Two MCP4725 DACs generate a 10 Hz biphasic stimulation waveform (0.5 ms pulse width, ±2 V amplitude) for stimulation. Both DACs communicate through I2C and support power-down mode which can be controlled by the BC840. The FPCB was fabricated by JLCPCB using EasyEDA design tools and encapsulated in a silicone shell for better biocompatibility and water resistance (Supplementary Fig. 43 and Supplementary Table 1).

Recent findings suggest that the glucose-responsive logic control of NeuroAxis received input from periodic sampling terminal voltage of GFC, which can represent glucose concentration. The output of the GFC was directly connected to the input of SAADC port of BC840 and power-harvest module. Specifically, the terminal voltage of the GFC was sampled once every 30 min. When the detected voltage exceeded 0.6 V, the high-voltage count was incremented; otherwise, the counter was reset to zero. Once the high-voltage count reached five, the system output electrical stimulation for 3 min. As the output voltage of the GFC exhibits a linear correlation with glucose concentration, the terminal voltage of GFC can be directly used to estimate blood glucose levels. This strategy ensured that stimulation was selectively initiated under sustained hyperglycaemic conditions, thereby reducing the likelihood of false triggering caused by transient voltage fluctuations. To experimentally evaluate the logical control function, the GFC was immersed in glucose solutions of varying concentrations. During the testing, the sampling interval was set to 10 s. The terminal voltage of the GFC was continuously acquired and transmitted to cell phones through a BC840 BLE module, while the corresponding output signals of NeuroAxis were simultaneously recorded using an oscilloscope.

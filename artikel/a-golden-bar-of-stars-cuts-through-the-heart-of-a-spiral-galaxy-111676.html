Hubble’s portrait of NGC 4394 shows how a bar of stars fits into a spiral galaxy and offers a rare perspective on the structure of our own Milky Way.

Today’s Image of the Day features a nearly face-on view of the spiral galaxy NGC 4394.

The European Space Agency released this Hubble Space Telescope image on November 24.

Industry observers note that the galaxy lies about 55 million light-years away in the constellation Coma Berenices. Its light was already on its way here long before humans appeared on Earth.

Industry observers note that on a clear, dark night, the Milky Way looks like a hazy band across the sky. That band is part of our galaxy’s disk seen from within.

When we look along the disk, stars at many different distances crowd into the same field of view, and dust obscures much of the light coming from the center.

Astronomers have used infrared telescopes such as Spitzer to look through some of that dust and trace the distribution of stars.

Industry observers note that those observations helped establish that our galaxy has a central bar. A face-on view of NGC 4394 makes it easier to picture how such a feature fits into a spiral galaxy.

The angle also makes it easier to follow the dust. In Hubble’s image, narrow dark filaments arc around the core and wind through the disk.

They stand out where they block light from the stars behind them. Farther along the arms, blue and pink knots punctuate the softer glow of the surrounding stars.

A bar may look like a solid feature in a still image, but the stars that make it up are moving around the galaxy.

Recent findings suggest that one way a bar can form is when nearly circular stellar orbits become stretched. If enough of those elongated orbits line up, the combined light from the stars takes on the shape of a bar.

In a recent development, A passing galaxy can disturb a disk through gravity and help set that process in motion. A disk can also become unstable through gravitational interactions among its own stars.

Either way, a feature that spans a large part of the galaxy can emerge from changes in the paths of individual stars.

Recent findings suggest that once a bar forms, its gravity can help channel gas inward. That matters because gas supplies the raw material for upcoming stars.

A bar can therefore influence where a galaxy builds its next generation of stars, even though the bright feature we see in the image is mostly the light of stars already there.

Hubble first captured NGC 4394 while studying a different galaxy. One instrument was trained on nearby Messier 85 and its star clusters, while another recorded an adjacent patch of sky containing NGC 4394.

Hubble’s team released that earlier image about a decade ago.

The latest portrait combines those observations with data from a program studying 40 galaxies. NGC 4394 belongs to the Virgo Cluster, a gathering of more than 1,000 galaxies.

The researchers want to understand how star formation winds down in crowded environments and whether it follows a different course in galaxies with fewer neighbors.

Sources indicate that star clusters give researchers a way to look backward. By comparing how brightly a cluster shines at different wavelengths, astronomers can estimate its age.

It has been revealed that A young cluster marks a place where stars formed relatively recently. Older clusters preserve evidence of earlier activity.

Taken together, their ages and locations can help researchers reconstruct where star formation persisted and where it faded.

That gives the specks along the spiral arms a second role in the image. The bar is the feature you can pick out at a glance, and it offers an immediate point of comparison with the Milky Way.

Sources indicate that the star clusters are harder to spot. Each one carries a piece of the galaxy’s history, and Hubble can pick them out even though their light has crossed 55 million years of space to reach us.

A morning dose of blue light may help you sleep better at night

🌐 Source: Earth • 📅 25 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 5 min read (950 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

Blue light has become the villain of bedtime. Phones get dimmed at night and glasses that block it sell as sleep aids. (Related: Block Sparse Matrices for Smaller and Faster Language M...)

Yet the same light, delivered for half an hour after waking, made a group of office workers sleep longer and better that night. The gain carried into the next day’s work.

The effect was modest, about 11 minutes more sleep a night and a small lift in how well people mentioned they slept. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

Participants who reported sleeping better also reported more alertness and less mental fatigue the next day. Greater alertness was linked to more engagement at work.

Kathleen Keeler, who studies management and human resources at Ohio State University’s Fisher College of Business, led the study.

Christopher Barnes of the University of Washington’s business school and Brian Gunia of Johns Hopkins University’s Carey Business School were among her co-authors.

The glasses came from a manufacturer called Ayo, which donated half the pairs used.

Sources indicate that barnes stated the mechanism is not a morning jolt from the blue light itself. “It’s that the blue light helps them sleep better later that night, which helps them the following day,” he noted.

The body runs on a roughly 24-hour cycle that decides when a person feels sleepy and when they feel awake.

Light is the strongest signal that keeps that internal clock on time, and blue light, in particular, is what the clock responds to most.

Blue light at night pushes the clock later and holds back melatonin, the hormone that brings sleep on.

That is the part everyone has heard. The same light in the morning pulls the clock earlier, which, in principle, should make sleep come sooner and run smoother that night.

Workplace researchers had tested the night half of that idea with blue-blocking glasses and found better sleep and better work. Workplace studies had not tested the morning half.

The team invited 200 full-time employees at a large Midwestern university, all working roughly nine to five, and 182 finished the study.

Sources indicate that nearly three in four were women, and the typical participant was in their early forties. They had been with the university for about a decade on average.

Recent findings suggest that for 10 workdays, each wore glasses that shine blue-turquoise light toward the eyes for 30 minutes right after waking, between 5 and 10 a.m. On average they started just before 7 a.m.

Sources indicate that for one of the two weeks the blue glasses ran at full power. For the other they ran at 1%, a glow so faint that in earlier tests people couldn’t tell the two settings apart.

Which week came first was decided at random, so every person served as their own comparison.

Participants filled in a survey each morning about the night’s sleep and their energy, and another each afternoon about their work, from engagement to helping coworkers to slacking off.

The team collected 1,259 daily surveys and registered its predictions in advance.

Each participant kept the glasses and a year of the companion app afterward.

After a full-power morning, people reported sleeping about 11 minutes longer that night, and rated the night’s sleep about 3.5% higher. Neither number is large. The team then traced the effect through the next day.

Longer and better sleep predicted more alertness the next day, and better sleep predicted less of the feeling that one’s mental energy is running low.

Sources indicate that alertness, in turn, predicted stronger work engagement and more helping behavior. Less depletion predicted better task performance and fewer behaviors like taking an overlong break.

Each link in that chain was small, and the team said so.

“This is a pretty small, cheap, easy intervention, and we’re getting meaningful movement on work outcomes that matter,” Barnes mentioned.

The morning blue light didn’t increase alertness that same day.

Alertness that same day was no higher, which is why the authors read the whole effect as running through sleep.

The benefit also built across the week rather than appearing in full on day one.

According to recent reports, two groups gained more than the rest. People who arrived with existing sleep problems saw a bigger improvement in sleep quality.

Younger employees saw a bigger gain in sleep length, with effects on engagement and helping that the older workers did not show.

The authors tied the age gap to the eye’s declining sensitivity to light with age, and called both findings exploratory.

It has been revealed that barnes had a cheaper suggestion. “The best thing you could do in the morning is get outside,” he remarked.

Every measure here was self-reported, including how well people said they performed, so the findings wait on a replication with objective records.

According to recent reports, everyone studied worked daytime hours at one university, and most were women. The authors noted shift workers, who often miss morning light altogether, might gain more, but no one in this study worked nights.

The measure of workplace misbehavior was also short and noisy, which the authors stated may have hidden a larger effect rather than created a false one.

What the glasses add on top of a sunny walk is the open question, since the study compared bright glasses with dim ones, not glasses with daylight.

The authors said the next test belongs with people whose schedules fight their body clocks.

The full study was published in the journal Journal of Applied Psychology.

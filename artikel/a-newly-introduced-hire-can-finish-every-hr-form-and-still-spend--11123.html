Sources indicate that picture this. Your fresh employee joins the welcome call on time. Their manager has planned a simple first assignment. Then someone realizes the employee can open email but can’t access the tool they need to do the work. (Related: Latest Update: Simple considerations for simple people ...)

Recent findings suggest that by lunch, three people have forwarded the same ticket. The employee is still waiting and wondering whom to ask.

Recent findings suggest that A good employee onboarding checklist catches that gap before day one. It tells each person what they own, when it’s due, and how to tell whether it’s done. It also gives the new hire a clear path from meeting the team to doing their first piece of work.

Here’s a checklist you can adapt from the accepted offer through day 90.

Before Day One | Get the Essentials Ready.

The days between offer acceptance and the start date give HR, IT, and the manager time to do work that the new hire can’t do for themselves. Give each task an owner and a due date so everyone knows where to follow up.

According to recent reports, HR sends a welcome message. Include the start time and time zone, location or meeting link, first-day agenda, and a named contact for questions. (Related: Welcome spaCy to the Hugging Face Hub...)

In a recent development, HR shares approved paperwork. Tell the employee what they can complete early, what’s due later, and who can help if a document needs a correction.

IT prepares equipment and accounts. Confirm delivery or pickup, share sign-in instructions, and arrange a way for the employee to reach support before they have tech giant access.

The manager approves role-specific access. List the tools needed for the first assignment and check when each approval must happen.

The manager schedules introductions. Book a kickoff, name an onboarding buddy, and invite the latest hire to any team meetings that will help them settle in.

In a recent development, do a short readiness check before the start date. If a laptop is delayed or an approval is still pending, tell the employee what to expect and adjust the first-day proposal. A clear update beats an hour of waiting for instructions.

Day One | Help the Employee Find Their Feet.

The employee should finish day one knowing who can help, where to find information, and what they’ll do next. Give them time to ask questions and try the tools they’ll use tomorrow.

Industry observers note that start with the manager welcome. Explain what the team does, what the role contributes, and what the first week will look like.

Verify access with the employee. Ask them to sign in, open the relevant workspace, and complete a simple action. An account can exist without the permissions the employee needs.

Industry observers note that introduce the onboarding buddy. Set aside time for an informal conversation about team routines, communication channels, and everyday questions.

In a recent development, share one place for onboarding information. Include the schedule, contacts, key documents, and outstanding tasks.

In a recent development, close with a quick check-in. Ask what’s still unclear and confirm the first thing the employee should do the next morning.

According to recent reports, one question can surface a gap quickly. “If you got stuck tomorrow morning, who would you contact?” If the employee hesitates, help them identify the right person before the day ends.

Week One | Give the New Hire a First Win.

By the end of week one, the employee should have tried a task that resembles the work they were hired to do. Choose something small enough to finish and meaningful enough to prompt useful feedback.

Set a first assignment. Explain the outcome, show a good example, and agree on when someone will review it.

Recent findings suggest that schedule a shadowing session. Let the employee observe a teammate and ask why they make certain decisions.

In a recent development, prioritize essential training. Make time for the policies, tools, and workflows the employee needs to start working safely and confidently.

Introduce key partners. Explain when the employee will work with each person and what those teams can help with.

Review the week together. Ask where they lost time, what helped them learn, and which process they’d like to practice again.

Recent findings suggest that A support hire might draft a reply to a sample customer ticket, then review it with a buddy. That exercise can reveal questions about tone, escalation, or where to find an approved answer. The manager can use those questions to strategy week two.

Days 30, 60, and 90 | Review Progress and Adjust the Plan.

Recent findings suggest that the checklist should carry past the first week. As the employee takes on more work, use each milestone to talk about what they can do independently and where they need more practice.

Recent findings suggest that day 30. Review what the employee can already do on their own. Ask which tools or processes still slow them down. Agree on one or two outcomes for the next month and identify who can help.

Sources indicate that day 60. Give the employee ownership of a larger task with a clear review point. Look at how they prioritize work, ask for help, and handle handoffs. If they keep running into the same problem, clarify the process or arrange another practice session.

Day 90. Review progress against the expectations discussed in week one. Ask the employee which onboarding steps helped and which information arrived too late. Agree on what they’ll focus on next and when you’ll check in again.

According to recent reports, A 90-day review can also uncover a problem earlier in the checklist. If several hires say they spent their first month guessing who approves requests, explain that process during week one and give them a chance to try it.

Give Each Task an Owner and a Completion Check.

A checklist entry like “Set up IT” leaves two questions unanswered. Who does the work, and what counts as done?

Try something more specific. “By Thursday, IT confirms the laptop is ready. On day one, the employee signs in and opens the support workspace with their buddy.” This gives IT and the buddy separate actions and tells the manager what to check before assigning work.

Use the same structure for tasks that cross departments. A manager can approve software access, IT can grant it, and the employee can verify it. If the request stalls, HR can see which step needs attention and follow up with the right person.

What to Measure After Each Hiring Group.

Start with a few measures your team can act on.

Recent findings suggest that day-one readiness. How many employees could access the equipment and systems they needed when they started?

Time to first assignment. When did the employee complete a meaningful task and receive feedback?

Recurring blockers. Which tasks were late or reopened across multiple hires?

Upcoming-hire feedback. Could employees find their schedule, understand what was expected, and get help when they needed it?

Industry observers note that read the numbers alongside what employees tell you. A task might show as complete because someone sent an invitation. The employee might still have been unable to open it. That feedback tells you to add an access check before marking the task done.

Keep one shared checklist and name someone to maintain it. After each hiring group, ask the owners which steps repeatedly needed a manual follow-up. If the same problem appears twice, make the fix part of the checklist for the next hire.

Start with a recent hire and walk through their first week. Where did they wait? What did they have to ask twice? Which introduction or practice task made the biggest difference?

Recent findings suggest that use those answers to improve your employee onboarding checklist. The next hire should know what to do, who can help, and what a good first week looks like.

Ancient hunters in Australia had more on the menu than archaeologists expected

A study spanning roughly 50,000 years found that dietary variety could reflect plentiful food and skilled hunting.

In a recent development, the research was led by Tiina Manne from the University of Queensland. Her team examined ancient animal remains from Riwi rockshelter and Boodie Cave on Barrow Island.

The upcoming research challenges that reading of dietary diversity. At both Australian sites, people ate many kinds of animals from early in the occupation records.

“First Nations peoples in Australia were much more sophisticated and strategic in the animals they chose to hunt,” Manne noted.

“Flexibility, not fixation on a few species of prey, was the smartest way to live in an unpredictable climate.”.

Riwi stands on Gooniyandi traditional lands near the Great Sandy Desert. People began using the rockshelter about 45,000 years ago.

Its deposits contain remains of wallabies, echidnas, possums, flying foxes, lizards, and snakes. Small wallabies, lizards, and snakes dominate the earliest part of the analyzed sequence.

Boodie Cave’s first occupation dates to between about 51,100 and 46,200 years ago. Barrow Island was then part of the mainland, with its surroundings changing as sea levels fell and rose.

The team identified 1,167 specimens from Riwi and 2,083 from Boodie Cave by animal group or body size. The varied diets persisted across substantial environmental changes.

Industry observers note that burned bones and impact marks at Riwi helped link the remains to human food preparation. Some fractures also occurred while the bones were fresh.

“In a land of unpredictable rainfall, a variety of foods signal ‘plenty’, and flexibility was the best strategy,” Manne explained.

Some of the most revealing findings were bones still joined together, known as articulated remains. Their condition suggested that people had discarded body parts with little nutritional value.

“On Barrow Island, which was then connected to the mainland, the diet included wallabies, turtles and reef fish, and articulated bones suggest only the most nutritious parts were eaten and the rest were discarded,” Manne said.

“This isn’t the behavior of people short of food – they could afford to be choosy, so a broad diet reflected choice, not scarcity.”.

According to recent reports, the paper dates these articulated remains to around 7,200–6,800 years ago, long after the earliest occupation. Around this time, rising seas were cutting Barrow Island off from the mainland.

The researchers documented 24 groups of joined bones, including fish spines and parts of turtle limbs.

They interpret these discarded portions as evidence that people could select the more rewarding parts of their catches.

An animal’s value depended on more than its size. Weather affected both its condition and the effort needed to catch it.

“When water and vegetation were plentiful, large kangaroos were spread thin across the landscape, but in arid conditions they became easier to ambush as they stayed near waterholes,” Manne said.

“Small animals like bandicoots were also a good option when it got dry, because they stayed fat by switching to eating insects.”.

“Hunters who kept their options open could shift between prey as conditions changed.”.

According to recent reports, at Riwi, large kangaroo remains became prominent after about 34,000 years ago, as conditions grew drier. The authors suggest hunting them became less risky when water drew the animals into more predictable locations.

Recent findings suggest that fat mattered, too. Opened leg bones at both sites point to the extraction of marrow, while smaller insect-eating animals could retain valuable fat during drought.

Many Australian animals hide in burrows, vegetation, or rock crevices during the day. Finding them requires knowing which traces to follow.

Sources indicate that the paper draws on First Nations ecological knowledge, including signs such as fresh tracks, disturbed soil, and scratches on bark. These can reveal prey that a hunter cannot see directly.

“A tremendous amount of ecological knowledge is passed on in oral histories, including subtle signs of not only how to find prey, but the best prey depending on the season and circumstances,” Manne said.

Where hidden animals were abundant and diverse, investigating those signs could reward hunters with many different foods. A smaller animal could be a dependable catch even when a larger one promised more meat.

The authors argue that archaeological diets need to be understood through local animal behavior, food availability, and hunting costs.

A fixed ranking of desirable prey can miss how those factors change with rainfall.

Animal remains also reveal only part of a meal. Plant foods, documented at other northern Australian sites, would have supplied important carbohydrates alongside meat and fat.

“Importantly, our work suggests archaeologists around the world should re-consider how they have interpreted diet choice and diversity,” Manne concluded.

The study is published in the journal Proceedings of the Natural Academy of Sciences.

Guitarist Andy Taylor, whose tenure in England’s glammy, pop-savvy rock band Duran Duran coincided with the group’s commercial pinnacle of the early ‘80s, died of prostate cancer at his home on Monday while surrounded by family. He was 65.

Recent findings suggest that in a statement shared with Variety, Taylor’s wife Tracey stated on behalf of their family, “To the world, Andy was a brilliant guitarist, but to us, he was the most wonderful husband, a loving dad and the proudest grandfather. He was the rock of our family. For all the incredible experiences music brought him, family always came first. Taylor, who was diagnosed with stage 4 metastatic prostate cancer in 2018, had opted out on appearing at the group’s November 2022 induction into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame, citing a decline in his health in a statement.

According to recent reports, he said, “Although my current condition is not immediately life-threatening there is no cure…. Recently I was doing OK after some very sophisticated life-extending treatment, that was until a week or so ago when I suffered a setback, and despite the exceptional efforts of my team, I had to be honest in that both physically and mentally, I would be pushing my boundaries.”.

Duran Duran had been in existence for two years in the Midlands city of Birmingham when Taylor — then a 19-year-old veteran seeking upcoming opportunities after successfully touring British army bases in Europe with a cover band — spotted a April 1980 Melody Maker classified ad seeking a “Live Wire Guitarist.”.

He wrote in “Wild Boy,” his candid and frequently sensational 2008 autobiography, “[The ad] cited Mick Ronson of the Spiders From Mars and Steve Jones of the Sex Pistols as musical influences so I thought it looked worth a call.”.

After a successful audition, Taylor became a member of the quintet, which became a popular fixture at the Birmingham club the Rum Runner. Sporting extravagant coiffs and makeup — “We’d end up discussing cosmetics with the girls,” Taylor later wrote — the band was lumped in with Britain’s Newly introduced Romantic movement of the early ‘80s. After only eight months with Taylor in the lineup, the act was signed by EMI.

Duran Duran — whose early compositions were credited to all five group members — became a nearly instant success in the U.K. Its third single “Girls on Film” (No. 5) helped push its self-titled debut album to No. 3. The 1982 follow-up album “Rio” spawned the hits “Hungry Like the Wolf” (No. 5) and the eponymous single (No. 9). Its first No. 1 single “Is There Something I Should Know” was issued in 1983.

The band was among the first beneficiaries of Stateside exposure on MTV, the then-upcoming cable network that broke through with wall-to-wall airing of videos; their lavish clips, handsomely directly by former 10cc members Kevin Godley and Lol Creme and the prolific Russell Mulcahy, captured eyes with their lush cinematography, scantily clad models and expensive location shoots in exotic locales like Sri Lanka and Antigua. Three Duran Duran singles reached the U.S. top five in 1982-83.

Taylor noted in his memoir that those videos often found the band members risking life and limb: “They were enormous fun to make — even if it nearly killed us at times. I’m not joking! Several of us risked being drowned, we narrowly escaped being trampled by a crazed elephant, and we were forced to flee one shoot after being threatened by four thousand angry monks.”Unsurprisingly, Duran Duran’s only Grammy Awards, both presented in 1986, were for best short form music video and (for “Duran Duran,” a compilation of their first 12 clips) best long form music video.

By 1983, the act was big enough in England to headline a London charity benefit attended by Prince Charles and Princess Diana, who told its members, “I don’t need any introductions to these boys. I know exactly who they are.” Their album of that year, Seven and the Ragged Tiger,” shot to No. 1 in the U.K. and No. 8 in the U.S.

However, by 1984, when the singles “The Reflex” and “The Wild Boys” reached No. 1 and No. 2 respectively in both the U.K. and the U.S., the band’s hard-partying, notoriously debauched lifestyle was beginning to shred their reputation; a story in the Fleet Street tabloid the Sun detailed stories of extreme cocaine abuse — later verified by Taylor, who, with bassist John Taylor (no relation), was the drug’s most enthusiastic user. Additional turmoil came into the guitarist’s life that year after his wife Tracey suffered a postpartum psychotic episode following the birth of their son Andrew.

Wrung out from a grueling recording and touring schedule and increasingly debilitated by their drug habits, Andy and John Taylor made a lateral move into a fresh quartet, the Power Station, which also featured white-soul vocalist Robert Palmer and Chic’s drummer Tony Thompson. Produced by Thompson’s band mate Bernard Edwards, its self-titled 1985 debut reached No. 6 in the U.S. and No. 12 in the U.K. (A long-delayed 1996 sequel, with Edwards standing in on bass for John Taylor, then in rehab, failed to chart.).

By the time Duran Duran appeared at the U.S. incarnation of the transatlantic, internationally televised benefit concert Live Aid in July 1985, the original lineup of the group was reaching the end of its tether. Taylor wrote in his memoir, “John and I were in a dark place….[W]e were dying inside. We were tired of twenty-four-hour attention, tired of drinking and taking drugs, haggard from the lifestyle we had once aspired to.”.

Exiting the band in 1986, Taylor attempted to clean up his life in Los Angeles and began working with Steve Jones, the former guitarist for the Sex Pistols and himself then newly in recovery. The fruits of their collaboration included “Take It Easy,” a No. 24 pop hit heard on the soundtrack for the film “American Anthem,” and the tough “I Might Lie,” which rose to No. 17 on Billboard’s mainstream rock chart. His 1987 solo set “Thunder” reached No. 46 domestically.

Following occasional work behind Rod Stewart, Taylor rejoined Duran Duran, after an absence of 14 years, for a full reunion of the original band. A successful world tour and a new album, 2004’s “Astronaut” (No. 3 in the U.K., No. 17 in the U.S.), followed. But the guitarist soon became discontent again, and exited a second time in 2006; material he had worked on for a new album was shelved.

“I was tired of going over and over the same old creative difficulties and financial tensions,” he later wrote, “and I was fed up with having blazing rows with our management over what I perceived to be our lack of clear direction….We’d ceased to function as a band; it was seriously dysfunctional. We’d returned to being five individuals pulling in different directions, just as we’d become in the eighties. When it came to sorting out our problems, just like before, we failed to connect.”.

In 2007-08, Taylor was briefly partnered in RockAffairs.com, a site devoted to the sale and promotion of music by unsigned independent artists.

He was born Feb. 16, 1961, in Tynemouth, Northumberland, and raised nearby Cullercoats.. At 11, he received his first guitar as a Christmas present, and in his teens played with local acts like the Gigolos before touring Europe.

He is survived by wife Tracey and his children Andrew, Isabelle, Bethany and Georgina.

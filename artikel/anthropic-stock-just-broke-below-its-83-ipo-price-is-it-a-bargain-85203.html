Before we look at Anthropic itself, it's useful to look at what the historical record claims about how stocks perform after an IPO.

Sources indicate that university of Florida professor emeritus Jay Ritter -- known as "Mr. IPO" for his research -- looked at more than 9,000 U.S. listings from 1980 through 2024. He found that the average stock gained about 19% on its first day. Investors who got in at the offer price trailed the broader market by 3.3 percentage points over the study's three-year window, while those who bought at the first-day close trailed it by 20.5 points.

Sources indicate that OK, but that's a pretty wide data set. A narrower one with companies that are at least somewhat comparable -- admittedly hard when dealing with a company as unique as Anthropic -- will tell us more.

Companies already doing at least $1 billion in annual sales fared better, though they still finished 2.1 points behind the market when measured from the first-day close.

Sources indicate that at about $80 a share, Anthropic is worth roughly $1.5 trillion on a fully diluted basis that includes shares reserved for future issuance. The organization's trailing 12-month revenue comes in at $35.3 billion, meaning the stock is trading at about 41 times sales. That is expensive.

Bulls argue that the company is at the very forefront of a technological revolution and its product is so valuable that it will grow into this valuation. It's a fair argument, especially given the pace of that growth -- revenue jumped from $1.4 billion to $17 billion year over year in Q3. That's basically unheard of at this scale.

Industry observers note that if we assume last quarter's sales pace holds, you're paying about 21.5 times annualized sales at today's price, or about 16 times based on shares outstanding. That's still expensive -- Nvidia (NVDA -5.86%) trades at about 8 times annualized sales on that same outstanding-share basis -- but more in the realm of reason.

My concern is twofold -- one, spending, and two, moat (or lack thereof).

In a recent development, anthropic's compute and infrastructure spending ate up 58% of sales in Q3, up from 48% in Q2, and Q2's $974 million operating profit turned back into a loss of $567 million. I'm still not convinced that this business can reliably turn a profit over time.

Industry observers note that that's especially true if the company is forced to compete on price with other model providers in order to defend its market share. That's the problem with having, in my view, very little moat. The company's current advantage comes from having the best models.

It has been revealed that that makes for a fairly shallow moat. Not only will it need to innovate relentlessly and spend incredible amounts training new models, but it may find itself with fewer and fewer customers willing to pay premium prices for those latest models when cheaper open-weight models can do the job.

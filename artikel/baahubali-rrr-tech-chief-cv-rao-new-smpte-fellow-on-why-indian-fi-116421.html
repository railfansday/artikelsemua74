According to recent reports, C.V. Rao, who helped shape the production and post-production pipelines behind S.S. Rajamouli‘s “Baahubali” films and “RRR,” has become the first technologist from South India to be named a Fellow of the Society of Motion Picture and Television Engineers (SMPTE).

In a recent development, rao, chief technology officer at Hyderabad-based Annapurna Studios, describes the honor as one that comes with added responsibility, along with a chance to learn from senior figures across the global SMPTE community. “It also reflects the tremendous technical talent available in India,” Rao tells Variety. “We have developed strong capabilities in adopting and implementing advanced filmmaking technologies, and we believe the next opportunity is to contribute more actively to innovation, technology development and international standards.”.

According to recent reports, “Baahubali” brought challenges well beyond what the studio had expected. The scale of the production demanded major upgrades in grading, processing power, storage, bandwidth and technical staff. For “Baahubali 2,” the team set up a dedicated peer-to-peer link between Hyderabad and Los Angeles to send large volumes of material straight to Imax. The experience also led to the launch of ANR Sound & Vision, a one-stop post facility that takes projects from data management and editing through VFX, sound, grading and final mastering. Recent findings suggest that the Dolby Cinema version of “RRR” had its roots in Las Vegas, where a Dolby Cinema demo at NAB led to talks with cinematographer K.K. Senthil Kumar, who pitched the idea to Rajamouli.

In a recent development, “We had to travel to Germany for the Dolby Cinema processing of ‘RRR,’ which raised an crucial question: why should Indian filmmakers depend on overseas facilities for these capabilities?” Rao says. “That experience became a major motivation for establishing Dolby Cinema processing capabilities at Annapurna Studios.”.

Rao points out that India had skilled VFX artists and capable post houses well before either film. What Rajamouli’s productions brought was a upcoming order of scale and complexity, which pushed studios to grow their capabilities, strengthen their teams and get departments working together more closely. Both films also relied on international partners. Attempting projects of that size entirely within India 10 years earlier would have been far tougher, he says, given the gaps in specialized infrastructure, processing capacity and experience at the time.

“The most significant impact was the confidence these projects created across the industry,” Rao notes. “They demonstrated that Indian studios could handle increasingly complex work and encouraged greater investment in our technical ecosystem.”.

Rao started out as a graphic designer and 3D artist. From there he worked his way through documentary and feature editing, scanning, lab work, visual effects and color grading. That time spent across departments taught him where crews run into trouble during the shoot and in post, and at Annapurna he has used it to find weak spots in workflows and build infrastructure around what productions actually need.

“We have always believed that understanding the filmmaker’s requirements is just as vital as understanding the technology itself,” he indicates.

Sources indicate that annapurna has put money into virtual production and one of India’s largest motion-capture stages. Interest is growing, but uptake has lagged expectations. During COVID, the studio saw virtual production as a way for filmmakers to stay inside controlled environments and cut down on location work and travel. The format, though, demands heavy preparation: digital sets have to be built, and camera moves and lighting mapped out, before anyone shoots a frame.

“In our industry, approvals and shooting schedules often get finalized very late,” Rao notes. “Even when filmmakers are interested, there may not be enough time to prepare everything properly. Under these circumstances, conventional shooting becomes the easier option.”.

Motion capture is gaining ground on complex action scenes, digital characters and performance-driven VFX. Rao expects both technologies to become standard tools once more productions have hands-on experience and learn where they genuinely save time.

On infrastructure, he maintains that Indian facilities hold their own against established international hubs and can access most of the same advanced technology. Several major Indian productions now finish large chunks of their post work at home. Where India trails is research and development. Mature markets, he notes, benefit from close collaboration among studios, technology companies, research institutions and creatives, while India needs to spend more on homegrown technology and on testing upcoming workflows.

According to recent reports, “We strongly believe infrastructure alone cannot deliver quality,” Rao notes. “The knowledge, experience and creativity of the professionals behind it make the real difference.”.

Annapurna has also set up Dolby Vision and Dolby Atmos mastering facilities. Rao finds big-budget productions increasingly sold on premium formats, while mid-budget filmmakers are still weighing whether they pay off commercially. With streaming and high-end TVs giving audiences quality viewing at home, he sees premium theatrical formats as a reason to get people back into cinemas.

It has been revealed that “However, there needs to be better alignment between mastering capabilities and theatrical exhibition infrastructure,” Rao says. As more premium-format screens open across India, he expects producers to commit to these formats earlier in their release plans.

AI is already proving useful in concept development, storyboarding, previsualization, restoration and repetitive VFX tasks, letting teams test ideas faster and automate slow work.

“However, storytelling, performances, emotional expression and final artistic decisions must remain under human creative control,” he notes.

Industry observers note that he sees AI working alongside existing tools rather than replacing them. Complex digital characters driven by performance may still need precise motion capture, with AI handling supporting jobs.

Whether any of this lowers costs depends on the project. Virtual production can trim location, travel and scheduling expenses, but it requires investment in infrastructure, digital assets and prep, and without proper planning it can end up costing more. AI is getting easier for smaller filmmakers to access, yet consistent professional results still call for skilled teams, computing power and quality control.

“We believe smaller productions can benefit from shared facilities and specialized technology services rather than investing in expensive infrastructure independently,” Rao claims.

Recent findings suggest that having overseen technical pipelines for more than 500 feature-film deliveries, Rao traces most post-production trouble back to poor planning. The most frequent error is bringing post teams in too late, when VFX, sound, grading, data management and delivery specs should ideally be on the table in pre-production. Late creative changes are the other big problem. Changes come with the territory, he acknowledges, but major revisions near delivery put artists and technicians under enormous strain.

In a recent development, “Teams may need to work extended hours, repeat completed tasks and rush important processes to meet deadlines,” Rao states. “This creates unnecessary stress, increases costs and can sometimes compromise the quality of the final product.”.

For India to land complete high-end international productions, Rao wants a friendlier business climate, consistent government policy, competitive incentives and simpler approvals, along with deeper partnerships with global studios and more spending on research, training and integrated facilities.

“The opportunity is to position India not merely as a cost-effective outsourcing destination, but as a reliable creative and technical partner capable of handling complete productions to international standards,” he says.

According to recent reports, training is part of that equation. Rao wants studios to offer structured internships and mentorship, educational institutions to rework curricula alongside working professionals, and government to back specialized training centers and research.

As an SMPTE Fellow, he plans to take part in technical discussions where India’s experience can help. An industry that works across many languages and production scales, under demanding workflows, has lessons to offer in digital cinema, post-production and data management, he says. He frames the fellowship as a two-way exchange, bringing ideas from global engineers and researchers back home in return.

Looking five years out, Rao expects AI, real-time rendering, virtual production and motion capture to reshape filmmaking as they increasingly converge. Annapurna will keep upgrading its infrastructure, exploring latest workflows and training teams, judging each new technology on whether it solves real production problems rather than on novelty.

“Ultimately, we believe technology investments should be driven by the value they bring to filmmaking, not simply by technological advancement itself,” Rao claims.

Ballerina Farm Is Opening a Restaurant, Farm Store, and Cooking School—Here’s a First Look

It has been revealed that it’s a glorious late-September morning in the Kamas Valley, and dozens of cows with handwritten name tags are circling me. A soft breeze hints at the seasonal shift that will soon blanket this bucolic corner of Utah in frost. I’m standing beside two of Daniel Neeleman’s daughters and Daniel himself, his head full of curly hair and his feet tucked into work boots. He exudes the easy familiarity of an old friend, despite a family name—and now a social media presence, thanks to Ballerina Farm’s 20 million–plus combined followers—that has taken on a life of its own.

Industry observers note that still, the eloquence and enthusiasm with which he discusses the economics of small-scale agriculture surprise me. “We love small agriculture and the diversification of it,” he states. “I think that we can make an impact in helping people see that small agriculture can work. Whether you’re doing an agritourism aspect or not, we still think it should and can be profitable.”. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

In a recent development, he speaks about each of the farm’s 120 animals with a fondness that belies his otherwise pragmatic approach to building a modern agricultural enterprise in America. Daniel did not grow up with animals and still remembers how mesmerized he was the first time he saw a cow. “I tell people on the farm tours all the time, if your endorphins are going off watching these animals, watch out. You might be a farmer. It doesn’t go away—it might come back to you later in life.” From the very day that Daniel and his now famous wife, Hannah, bought their first farm in 2017, he knew this was where they were meant to be. (Related: Building the next generation of multimodal AI experienc...)

According to recent reports, earlier that morning, I was sitting at their kitchen dining table over a brunch of quiche, greens, yogurt, and tea made from just-picked mint, the spread illuminated by beeswax spindle candles. “It’s our responsibility, and our love and passion too, to build this,” Hannah tells me, wearing a white dress with three daughters by her side. She’s sitting against a backdrop that’s become familiar to millions who have watched her cook and feed her growing family on a farm less than an hour outside Salt Lake City.

Recent findings suggest that despite their sincere intentions, the Neelemans’ endeavor has brought moments of global controversy. The internet has scrutinized their approach to homeschooling their nine children, their family wealth, and how they balance their roles as parents and cofounders. “We don’t take the platform that we’ve been given lightly,” Hannah says. Agriculture, she explains, has been at the heart of the project from the beginning, even as its reach has expanded far beyond the Kamas Valley. “It has worn a lot of different hats as it’s grown. We were young city kids, and we craved something real and knowing where our food comes from.”.

According to recent reports, now, what began as a story told online is becoming a universe people can actually step into. On a stretch of land that spreads out beyond the window like something from “America the Beautiful,” they’re planning—and here announcing—one of the country’s most ambitious fresh farm hospitality destinations: a cookery school, farm-to-table restaurant, farm store, and spaces for agricultural education and events, all set within a working farm encompassing livestock, organic gardens, a dairy, and a forthcoming creamery.

According to recent reports, at the heart of the vision is a cookery school, scheduled to open in 2029, that is as much about learning to cook from the farm as it is about taking those lessons home. The goal is to teach people how to cook seasonally and adapt when certain ingredients aren’t available, wherever they might live. (Daniel describes this as an education in life skills rather than formal chef training.) “I want students to have time with the dairy, the market garden, the creamery, so they’re seeing where the food comes from,” Hannah notes.

The cookery school will be guided by a philosophy similar to that of the legendary Ballymaloe Cookery School, where Hannah and Daniel spent 12 weeks in 2025—and first met Michael “Tiffin” Griffin, a beloved longtime instructor who has since joined Ballerina Farm as the director of the culinary experience. (A more formal, fine-dining restaurant may eventually complement the school.).

But visitors can step into the new Ballerina Farm world as soon as fall 2027, when a Dutch barn is slated to open at the site of the current Kamas market. Inside will be a restaurant and café, which the couple collectively calls an “eatery,” serving meals featuring the meat, dairy, and produce grown on the surrounding farm—a considerable expansion of the current menus at the Midway store and food truck.

Across a breezeway, a market will offer fresh produce straight from the garden and meat and dairy from the livestock—think onions, pea shoots, beets, rib-eye steaks, smoked paprika goat cheese, and gallons of A2/A2 cream-top whole milk—as well as a growing selection of prepared sides, sauces, and meals, from pickled torpedo onions to just-juiced turmeric ginger shots to chicken salad. The latter is Hannah’s particular favorite. “We got so attached to the chicken salad that we had this summer, because we grew meat chickens. We harvested them. And they made the most delicious chicken salad. Then Tiffin told us this week, ‘One more week of the chicken salad—I’m out of pickles. You’ll see it next spring.’ And I went, ‘Nooo.’” She laughs. “I think people are getting back to how grandma used to cook. She cooked from the garden, and it was amazing food.”.

Just-cut spinach and radishes will sit next to Ballerina Farm’s signature products, initially available only online, as well as Ballerina Farm’s home goods like oven mitts, aprons, and wooden measuring spoons, with the name appearing only discreetly. They also launched their first skin-care product just this month: Tallow Tin body butter—made with tallow from the cattle, naturally.

Sources indicate that the restaurant, farm store, and connecting courtyard will be more than five times the size of the existing Ballerina Farm store and café that opened in 2025, 25 minutes down the road in Midway. “There’s an awful lot of hullabaloo about Hannah doing it, but for me that’s what I saw when I was a kid,” Tiffin, the culinary director, tells me. “That’s how my grandmother lived her life.”.

Over a two-hour meal of Ballerina Farm roasted chicken, garden carrots, pickled onions, and homemade yogurt, I sat enraptured by Tiffin’s story and the depth of knowledge and experience that had brought him to this particular farm in Utah. While guests can expect a diversity of dishes—Tiffin is especially passionate about Moroccan flavors—the land is the final decision maker. “We’ll ask Tiffin what’s on the menu for an event,” Daniel explains, “and the first thing he’ll say is, ‘Let me call Madison [Goodman], the head grower.’”.

In a recent development, goodman is responsible for a vegetable operation that will eventually expand to five acres. The kitchen will draw on its harvest but supplement it with neighboring producers when necessary—a philosophy similar to that which guided Tiffin at Ballymaloe: “If we can’t grow it, we get it locally. If we can’t get it locally, we’ll get the best.”.

It has been revealed that the design and architecture of the project, meanwhile, will be led by Patrick Williams of Berdoulat, the British design house known for its richly textured, historically informed interiors. At Berdoulat, the mantra is “the house rules.” But as Williams puts it, “In this instance, where the buildings are yet to exist, the land rules.” His designs draw inspiration from 19th-century settlers’ homes and agricultural buildings in the Kamas Valley (and, yes, the family’s famous farmstead).

The Ballerina Farm team has worked closely with Kamas officials for years now to define what can be developed on its 14-acre property, prompting the city to introduce upcoming agritourism zoning in 2024. The looks also protect the property’s conserved wetlands. “We are constantly in touch with the city,” says Brad Roberts, the brand’s director of marketing. If done well, Kamas can become a role model for the many other small towns grappling with their own decisions around the opportunities made possible through agritourism.

Sources indicate that yet the Kamas Valley is not the most obvious place to craft such an ambitious project that’s certain to draw travelers not only from the coasts but also from overseas. (Eighty percent of Ballerina Farm’s 10 million–plus Instagram following is based outside of the US.) A high water table can flood the gardens, frosts set in as early as October, and voles relentlessly try to work their way into the harvest. But the valley has a certain quality of light, giant skies, and an openness that feels rarer than ever in today’s world.

In a recent development, walking through three high tunnels filled to the brim with produce, I eventually met Goodman, a fourth-generation farmer who ran his own successful community-supported agriculture program in the Kamas Valley and was courted for years by the Neelemans before joining the Ballerina Farm team. He speaks passionately about the farm’s recently earned USDA organic certification and the decades of advocacy that brought those standards into existence. “You can make me cry, because there are a lot of people who fought hard for this,” Goodman states, describing the certification’s baseline standards as the floor while emphasizing that he’s reaching for the ceiling. “The label helps us bridge this gap that we, as a society, have created between our reality.”.

He continues: “Everything here has a story. I can tell you the story of the winter leek. I can tell you the story of these girls.” (The girls are chickens.) He then spends 10 minutes explaining to me exactly why these chickens—Freedom Rangers rather than the more commonly raised Cornish Cross—are so special and how they’re raised from chicks to harvest. “It’s hard for me to do this and not believe in a creator,” Goodman says.

One of the challenges and opportunities of an agritourism project is that, in a four-season destination like Utah, the experience will be remarkably different depending on when visitors arrive: tomatoes in the summer, spinach in the winter, chicks in one season, and a mature flock in another. “There’s something beautiful about eating what’s in season and retraining us as eaters to appreciate that,” Hannah reflects. “I have started to notice that I’m craving spinach, pumpkin, and squash because your body wants the things that are growing.”.

The culture side of agriculture has probably played a larger role in the Neelemans’ agricultural pursuits than they expected, however. While Hannah started sharing on social media as a way to kick-start business for their nascent pork operation, it snowballed into a public inquisition over what many perceived as an idealized, regressive performance of rural domesticity. But what the public wanted to know most was how it was possible. What was real? And who funded it?

Industry observers note that when I ask them about balancing their ambitious vision with the realities of building a farm and business, Hannah claims, “That’s a true founder of a firm. They are the one who has to hold those two worlds together. We have to keep the dreams alive and the fire burning, but we’re also camping over the farm stand or dealing with broken pipes and trying to put up hoop houses.”.

Industry observers note that daniel adds: “We’re the ones that decide whenever there’s money left over at the end of the month from the business.” He says that every individual part of the business is profitable. “We don’t have any investors, we don’t have any partners—it’s just us. So then it’s just a question of, What projects are we going to conquer first? We are a for-profit company, and we have to be good stewards of the finances of all the things. It has to be sustainable.”.

For years, people have been asking to visit, Hannah explains, sending her messages asking to meet the animals, help with chores, or simply spend a day on the farm. At the time, the Neelemans weren’t equipped to receive this audience of millions who had developed a relationship with a place they’d never visited. Now, they’re preparing to welcome both admirers and curious outsiders, as well as farmers and cooks, into the world that has generated so much conversation. “We have a community that wants to be a part of it,” Hannah recalls thinking. “So how do we build it?”.

This new expression of Ballerina Farm places Hannah and Daniel among a lineage of visionaries in farm hospitality who have transformed working farms into gathering places—Ballymaloe, yes, but also Daylesford Organic in the English Cotswolds and Babylonstoren in South Africa. But of course, one crucial factor sets Ballerina Farm’s journey apart. Rather than building a hospitality destination and then finding an audience, the Neelemans are working the other way around. The relationship is already shaped by years of watching the family’s life unfold online—and by the very different ideas people have formed about what Ballerina Farm represents.

Sources indicate that it’s easy to understand the appeal: Rather than watch a 60-second video on their phones, visitors can pull sourdough or slip on a Ballerina Farm oven mitt and slide a pork chop out of the oven. They can greet a just-born calf. Their children can run the fields, breathe the fresh Utah air, and taste the milk from Daniel’s cows. As I pulled through the gates of Ballerina Farm in my rental car and watched six Sebastopol geese waddle in front of me, even I felt that very modern feeling of disorienting familiarity upon experiencing a person or a place in the flesh that I had only ever seen on a screen.

In a recent development, right now, the Kamas property has already begun hosting farm dinners, seasonal events, and agricultural experiences alongside the market and food truck. Each offering showcases how the Ballerina Farm world will eventually come together: market, meals, events, education, animals, and gardens all in one place.

It has been revealed that and for all the ambition of what Hannah and Daniel are building, the idea at its heart remains deeply personal. “The greatest work that we can do is in our homes and our own families and our communities,” Hannah tells me, a conviction that has remained constant even as Ballerina Farm’s audience has rapidly grown.

Adds Daniel, “We’ve accomplished some things that we’re proud of, but we’re far from being done.”.

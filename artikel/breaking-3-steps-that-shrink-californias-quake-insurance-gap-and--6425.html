Breaking: 3 steps that shrink California's quake insurance gap, and where each falls short

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Nearly 90% of California homeowners had no quake coverage in 2018. Three steps from a USGS geophysicist cut the risk but leave the gap open.

Recent findings suggest that californians tend to treat earthquakes as background, a risk they know about and seldom plan for. Experts don't treat that risk as remote. Over the next 30 years, the Hayward Fault by itself has a 33% chance of producing a large quake, according to U.S. Geological Survey figures reported by BLS. That window matches the term of a standard home mortgage, and a family that buys a house near that fault faces real odds of living through the quake before the loan is paid off. Each household decides on its own whether to insure against that risk, and the sum of those private choices is the state's insurance gap. Many homeowners who skip coverage expect government help to cover the damage when the quake comes. The scale of a bad quake makes that a weak plan. Estimates of insured and uninsured damages from a magnitude 6.9 quake on the Hayward Fault run as high as $210‒$235 billion, according to BLS. At the high end of that range, a great many households across the region would need help at the same moment. For an uninsured homeowner, the useful question is what the family can do before the ground moves to make the loss smaller and the days after it survivable. NPR reported on steps that Ken Hudnut, a geophysicist with the U.S. Geological Survey, says Californians should take to build earthquake resilience, from the strength of a home's foundation to the food and water in its cupboards. The need for those steps was plain in 2018, when nearly 90% of California homeowners had no quake coverage and federal disaster aid to an individual was capped at about $33,000. That cap is far below what a major quake can destroy, so most of an uninsured family's loss would stay with the family. "There's a lot you need to do to insure resilience for yourself, your family, your home, your workplace," Hudnut said. A policy is only one piece of that work, and the rest of it falls to the household.

Industry observers note that preparing a home and buying quake coverage work together, even though many uninsured homeowners treat preparation as a substitute for a policy. Damage to buildings alone is a large bill. Estimates for a magnitude 6.9 quake on the Hayward Fault vary widely, and even the lowest puts building-related losses at $23 billion, according to BLS. Some preparation also lowers the cost of coverage, since insurers price policies around a structure’s strength.

According to recent reports, homeowners who skip coverage often assume a major quake will strike on some other fault. U.S. Geological Survey figures give the San Andreas Fault a 22% chance of a major rupture within 30 years, lower than the Hayward Fault’s, according to BLS. Yet a magnitude 7.2 quake on the San Andreas would bring moderate to heavy shaking to places that pay 17.0% of Bay Area wages, BLS estimates. Lower odds don’t mean a smaller loss.

Preparation can make a quake less costly, but it doesn’t replace a policy.

A foundation retrofit lets an owner change how much a quake can take from the house itself. Well-designed, well-built buildings take only negligible damage at intensity VII on the Modified Mercalli scale, according to BLS, and construction quality is one of the few things an owner can improve ahead of time. Hudnut counts a foundation check among his key steps. In 2018, a completed upgrade could also earn up to 20% off California Earthquake Authority coverage, according to NPR, but only for owners who buy a policy. For the uninsured majority, the payoff is a sturdier house, not a smaller premium.

According to recent reports, public money for retrofits goes mainly to owners whom a city has already ordered to act. A grant for apartment buildings is open only to owners who have received a city notice and order to retrofit, the California Residential Mitigation Program reports, and it runs in 14 California cities with mandatory ordinances. “Buildings that qualify for this grant are already required by law to retrofit under their city’s ordinance,” noted Janiele Maffei, chief mitigation officer at the California Earthquake Authority.

Even an owner who qualifies still pays part of the bill. Qualifying owners get 70% of their construction costs paid, up to $4,260 per unit, according to the program, and cover the rest themselves.

A strengthened foundation limits the damage to a house, but most owners pay for it themselves, and it leaves the insurance gap open.

The first hardship after a major quake is getting through days without power, running water or open stores, and it arrives long before any insurance claim or aid check is settled. Hudnut advises Californians to keep emergency packs stocked with food and water for several days so they can survive outages and displacement, according to NPR. Federal health officials also measure readiness in days, and the CDC advises keeping earthquake supplies for at least three days. A stocked kit helps a family on the first night, well before any payout could, and a household with no policy can still rely on it.

According to recent reports, A useful quake kit holds more than food and water. The CDC recommends adding sturdy shoes, heavy gloves, a fire extinguisher and a whistle to a home kit, so people can walk over broken glass, put out a small fire and signal for help. Checking a kit means checking that gear as well as the dates on the food.

The three-day standard is a floor, and households need to initiative past it. The CDC also calls for a supply kit in the car, because supplies are useful only if they go with the family. A second kit means the household has supplies wherever it ends up.

With a full kit, a family can get through the first few days after a quake, but it needs a plan of its own for the weeks that follow.

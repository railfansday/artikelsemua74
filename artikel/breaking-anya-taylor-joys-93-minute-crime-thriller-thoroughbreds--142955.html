According to recent reports, one of the most unsung releases of the last decade was 2017’s Thoroughbreds, a thriller deep in the film noir mode with three great actors going toe to toe. With Anya Taylor-Joy, Olivia Cooke, and Anton Yelchin as the architects and participants of a murder plot, it’s less a crime movie and more of a character study, with director Cory Finley mining both pathos and extremely dark comedy from the premise. In an era where minimal-location movies about the sociopathy of the rich were frequently successful discourse machines (including Taylor-Joy’s 2022 vehicle The Menu), Thoroughbreds was a classically amoral story of kids whose wealth insulates them from consequences, yet you still root for them. The movie was unfortunately ahead of the curve with its note-perfect plotting and criminal ideation, as well as its cast.

While the late Anton Yelchin had found major success in movies like Green Room or even the modern Star Trek films, Cooke and Taylor-Joy were just getting started, some years away from the blockbuster extravaganzas of Dune and House of the Dragon. Coming some years before that, Thoroughbreds suggested great depth from all three actors, playing them against each other as they all fight for their own agendas, while also giving viewers the chance to identify with potentially evil teens. The movie is leaving Starz on November 30, but its study of youthful sociopathy and the tenuous nature of female friendship means it should remain a cult classic for years to come. Recent findings suggest that thoroughbreds is a movie of interiors, specializing in the rich, old money homes of its protagonists Amanda (Olivia Cooke) and Lily (Anya Taylor-Joy), who live in suburban Connecticut. From the very beginning, when Amanda mercy-kills her crippled horse (leading to the title) in an unnerving moment, the movie keeps its distance from the characters, with director Cory Finley’s camera holding on scenes and moments for long stretches. Rather than the aspirational gleeful immorality of the classic teenage murderer movie Heathers, Thoroughbreds is methodical and ironic. The fancy decor, the emotional repression, and the basement tanning beds all go hand in hand with Amanda’s claims of an inability to feel anything. You might wonder if she’s just being an edgy teenager, especially after the horse euthanizing moment. But the movie’s visual language backs her up. It has been revealed that in a twist directly out of Alfred Hitchcock’s Strangers on a Train, the movie’s narrative begins to hinge on a murderous favor. Lily, effectively tasked by Amanda’s mom to befriend her troubled daughter, has an abusive stepfather named Mark (Paul Sparks), and Amanda, in her emotionless wisdom, presents a solution: kill him. The girls have a means of laundering the murder in the form of drug dealer Tim (Yelchin). Olivia Cooke’s portrayal of Amanda veers beyond creating an empathetic version of somebody suffering from an undisclosed mental illness, but it never goes into villain territory either. The movie’s cold and heavily adorned visual sensibility might have Yorgos Lanthimos-esque ironic distance from what you see, but it means that the relationship between the two girls becomes increasingly complex, in a way that it wouldn’t be if viewers were directly keyed into how the girls see each other.

Industry observers note that cory Finley had reportedly envisioned the movie as a play originally, and that feeling lingers in the film. The minimal cast and locations, as well as the long takes and reliance on dialogue, have the potential to feel theatrical, but the movie is sophisticated on the level of cinematic storytelling, commenting on its cast with its staging and using its widescreen compositions for both comedy and terror as chickens come home to roost. As he told IndieWire, the story began with the idea of a character who couldn’t feel anything. As the friend seeking to take advantage of that to help her own family situation, Anya Taylor-Joy’s Lily is effectively not much different.

The movie’s cinematic lineage is rich, drawing from Alfred Hitchcock (besides Strangers on a Train, his highly theatrical one-take experiment Rope, with its homoerotic murder conspiracy, has a clear line to Thoroughbreds) as well as ‘90s evil rich kid movies like Cruel Intentions, but with a much less moral stance. Peter Jackson’s true-crime classic Heavenly Creatures also feels like an influence, with the fantastical sequences substituted for cold stylization. When Amanda and Lily’s prepares go awry, we see the truth of what the girls are willing to go through with, how calculating they can be with the other’s emotional headspace. A different movie about teenage murder conspirators might let them off the hook, but Amanda’s dreams of wild thoroughbred horses suggest that a cruel end is coming to everybody.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Paying cash for upcoming equipment can leave your business short for payroll and inventory. Before you buy, get quotes on a lease, a used model with a dealer warranty, and a repair of what you have. Then compare each one’s total cost over the years you’ll use it, after taxes.

Recent findings suggest that buying outright looks thrifty, and it usually costs less over an asset’s life than leasing, the U.S. Small Business Administration notes. But that saving builds over years, while the whole price leaves your account the day you buy.

In a recent development, so each option comes down to two numbers, the cash it takes up front and its total cost over the years you’ll use it. With new equipment, lowering one raises the other. A lease takes little cash up front but usually costs more in total than paying cash, and so does an equipment loan, once you add interest and fees. Buying the same model used lowers both, as long as it has plenty of working years left. (Related: How to train a newly introduced language model from scr...)

Recent findings suggest that at tax time, lease payments are deductible as rent, while Section 179 of the tax code lets you deduct a purchase’s full price the year you start using it, up to yearly limits. A lease you can buy out for a token sum, such as $1, generally counts as a purchase to the IRS, so have a tax pro run each option’s after-tax cost. In a recent development, lease what you’d trade for a newer model within a few years, like computers. Before you sign, check how the lease ends: the buyout price, who pays to ship the equipment back, and how much notice stops it from renewing on its own.

Buy used from a dealer that inspects or refurbishes the equipment and puts a warranty in writing. A restaurant that pays $9,000 for a two-year-old, dealer-certified range, when the same model costs $22,000 upcoming, keeps $13,000 as a cushion for its first slow season.

Repair what you have when the repair costs less per year of use than a replacement. Say a $1,500 repair keeps an old range going three more years, and a $9,000 used one would last 10 years. The repair costs $500 a year, the replacement $900.

Then pick the lowest total among the options that leave enough cash to cover your slowest month’s payroll, rent, and supplier bills.

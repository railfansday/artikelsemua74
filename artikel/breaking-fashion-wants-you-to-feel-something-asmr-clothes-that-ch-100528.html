📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

At Yuima Nakazato’s spring/summer 2026 haute couture show in January, the clothing was the soundtrack. Models paraded down the runway in slow motion while the chimes of hulking hand-sculpted ceramic beads rang out throughout the old church venue. The ASMR effect has slowly but surely started to make its way down the runways. In 2024, Paloma Wool had models showcase their clothing against microphones for the ultimate ASMR experience. Fast forward, and Simone Rocha has presented shoes topped with bells (an allusion to the classic English nursery rhyme); Celine’s embellished supersized paillette dresses gently rustled down the catwalk (begging to be touched) and Jean Paul Gaultier’s latest collections are covered in bells that softly jingle as they’re worn. Earlier this year, Maison Margiela showcased a shattered porcelain dress that emitted the sound of broken glass as the model wore it down the runway.

Recent findings suggest that consider it a manifestation of the gentle brain massage we’re all craving amidst the chaos, even if just for a moment. Soothing sounds and clothing you can hear take a stab at promoting relaxation. At Nakazato’s show, the silence and the clinking of the clothing were a jolt. You could hear camera shutters and others’ breathing in between the footsteps of the models and the swaying of the heavy gowns: something typically impossible at most fashion demonstrates. Industry observers note that “I chose not to add music to this show,” Nakazato wrote in his show notes. “Today, we live surrounded by electronic sounds streaming through headphones and by the glow of screens, constantly absorbing information. That is why, during the fifteen minutes of this fashion show, I invite you to direct your attention to the unstable, subtle sounds created by the garments before you, and to the breath of the bodies wearing them.”. Sources indicate that even if it’s trending now, clothing has a long history with ASMR qualities. Fashion has perpetually engaged beyond the visual sense, leveraging sound, texture, and movement to create a holistic tactile experience. Take, for instance, the time Viktor & Rolf famously covered garments in sonorous bells for their fall/winter 2000 couture collection, turning the runway into a symphony of sound. Yet even long before modern couture, women wore small bells stitched onto their garments in medieval Europe to signify presence and status, while in the 1850s, the distinct rustling sound of a big, full, and colorful skirt was widely regarded as an audible signifier of fashionable wealth and social standing.

Likewise, Jean Paul Gaultier’s bell-covered dress from spring/summer 2027 was inspired by a JPG glove from the 1989 archive, completely covered in tassels and bells. “When we started adding bells on things, it made an amazing sound,” creative director Duran Lantink said immediately following the show. “So we started thinking about that woman, or that person who is quite loud—she’s there, and she’s present.”.

Sources indicate that fashion’s ASMR love affair isn’t merely limited to sound either. Just look at fashion’s bags of the moment: intricately woven or designed to look like antique tapestries or grandma’s couch. Or Monse’s kinetic fringe, Bottega Veneta’s gasp-worthy fiberglass jackets, and Valentino’s awe-inspiring mermaid tail gowns resplendent in pleats and wrapped up with a black bow.

Noir Kei Ninomiya’s exploding garden dresses were blooming with dynamic flowers (both real and synthetic) with out-of-this-world silhouettes so tactile you get the feeling you might ascend to a higher place just by touching one.

Recent findings suggest that maybe the urge to put our phones down and experience the real world (and all of its sounds, smells, and feels) is the most compelling thing about what’s coming down the runway right now. It’s fashion that wants to make you feel alive.

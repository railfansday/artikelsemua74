From Social Security numbers to FBI agents' home addresses, here's what 2026's government hacks exposed and how to protect yourself.

It has been revealed that in September, a criminal hacking group called ShinyHunters claimed it had taken data on nearly every FBI agent and job applicant, and knocked the bureau's jobs portal offline. "We have compromised the FBI. We hold very sensitive data on almost ALL FBI Agents and individuals who filed an application with the FBI for a job," the group's message mentioned, according to PBS News. The FBI said it was investigating the claim, but that it hadn't yet determined the "point of breach.". (Related: Rockstar explains why GTA 6 added its Criminal Profile ...)

In a recent development, the stakes go beyond identity theft. A Latest York Times analysis found that the stolen data may include home addresses, Social Security numbers and job assignments. Reuters reported that the hackers claimed to have 2 to 3 terabytes of data, and some of what it reviewed highlighted which agents work in specific field offices and in units handling intelligence and counterespionage. In November, the FBI began investigating an apparent hack of the National Crime Information Center, a person familiar with the matter told The Associated Press. Local, state and federal police use the database to look up fugitives, missing persons, criminal histories and stolen property. The FBI declined to comment, and the scope of the problem wasn't immediately clear.

This exposure reaches people who never worked for the government: anyone named in a police record. "We just heard about it, and I will say that it's not good," President Donald Trump stated in a Fox News Channel interview. Administration officials have blamed a separate run of recent outages on routine system upgrades, and there was no immediate sign the two were linked.

According to recent reports, the exposures this year haven't been limited to federal agencies. Before the FBI's jobs portal was hit, a separate breach may have exposed more than 153 million driver's licenses across the U.S. and Canada, TIME reports. Sherman's warning about not dismissing "just another data breach" was written about that leak.

Combined with Social Security numbers and job details from other breaches, a license record helps a scammer build a fuller profile of one person.

Each breach adds a piece; together they can complete the picture.

Government hacks tend to be covered as stories about secrets and spies. The breaches that have piled up through 2026 are mostly about something closer to home: the personal records of the people who serve, work for or deal with federal agencies. Social Security numbers, home addresses, job assignments and criminal records have all been in the path of intruders this year.

According to recent reports, "It can be tempting to dismiss the next hack of a database as 'just another data breach.'... But breaches do not exist in a vacuum," Justin Sherman, a senior associate at the Center for Strategic and International Studies, wrote, as TIME reported. Each exposed record can make the next scam or intrusion easier. Here's what has been exposed so far, and what people caught up in it can do.

Credit: Kevin “KevDoy” Doyle / Unsplash.

The largest confirmed exposure this year came from the Defense Manpower Data Center, the Pentagon office that keeps personnel records for pay, healthcare and retirement. A Pentagon official confirmed to TIME that about 2.76 million living people and 294,000 deceased people were affected. The exposed files included Social Security numbers and job details, along with names, birthdates and contact information.

According to recent reports, the center holds records on more than 60 million people, including troops, civilian staff, contractors, family members, retirees and veterans. So the breach reached well beyond people presently in uniform. Intruders had access to a file-sharing server from October 2025 until the center found the weakness on July 16, 2026. Notification letters went out in September, and it's still unclear which files were opened or who did it.

For anyone linked to the military, the most sensitive number they have may already be in someone else's hands.

When the targets are investigators, a leaked home address can be a safety risk, not just a privacy one.

Credit: Jakub Żerdzicki / Unsplash.

ShinyHunters states this attack wasn't about money. It gave the FBI one week to retract a May public advisory that called the group a "cyber criminal group specializing in large-scale data breaches and extortion," and said it was "offended" by the description. "This is not a ransom, coercion, or extortion. Your federal policies do not apply here. This PSA is NOT financially motivated," the message said.

According to recent reports, the FBI's own advisory had warned that the group often uses "real or exaggerated claims of access" to pressure victims. That means its claims about how much it took can't be taken at face value. The group didn't say what it would do if the deadline passed.

Whether the motive is money or pride, the data is the same once it's out.

A breach here would put ordinary Americans' records at risk, not just federal staff's.

Credit: Michał Jakubowski / Unsplash.

Sources indicate that the FBI's troubles started earlier in the year. In March, the bureau said it was investigating "suspicious activities" on an internal system holding sensitive information about surveillance operations and investigations. That same month, a pro-Iranian hacking group claimed to have hacked an account belonging to FBI Director Kash Patel and posted what appeared to be old photos, a resume and personal documents more than a decade old.

The FBI said the Patel material was "historical in nature" and involved "no government information." Together, the incidents show how often the bureau itself has become a target this year.

Even old personal documents give attackers something to work with.

Sources indicate that the Pentagon is offering a year of free credit monitoring and identity-restoration services through IDX, a private firm it hired. People with questions can contact IDX at response.idx.us/DMDC or 1-855-744-4556, a defense official told TIME.

Anyone can also place a free credit freeze with each of the three major credit bureaus (Equifax, Experian and TransUnion), according to the Federal Trade Commission. A freeze blocks upcoming accounts from being opened in a person's name. Active-duty troops and National Guard members can also get free electronic credit monitoring. Experts also warn that exposed records make phishing easier, so unexpected messages that cite personal details deserve extra suspicion.

A credit freeze costs nothing and takes minutes, and it's the simplest protection after a breach.

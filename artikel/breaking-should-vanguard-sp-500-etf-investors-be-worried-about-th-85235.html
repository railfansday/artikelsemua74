Once the largest firm in the world, with an over-$5 trillion market cap, Nvidia (NVDA -5.86%) had been one of the biggest driving forces in the market. However, the stock closed out the week ending Nov. 20 on a seven-day losing streak, losing 27.5% of its market value in this span. But then there was a strong rebound on Monday. As I write this, the stock is down 32% from the $243.37 high it set less than two months ago, on Oct. 6. (Related: Ex-Rockstar artist explains main difference between Red...)

Nvidia's troubles stemmed from the surprise resignation of its longtime co-founder and CEO Jensen Huang following revelations of marital infidelity, allegedly including with subordinates and a potential Chinese spy. Even a strong quarterly report and the company raising its fiscal 2028 (ending January 2028) revenue guidance to 75% growth were not enough to stop the stock's slide. However, a surprise return to the CEO role by Huang on Monday led to a huge rebound in the stock. (Related: Nvidia reinstates Jensen Huang as CEO amid ongoing fore...)

In a recent development, nvidia's woes spread to other semiconductor stocks, with the entire artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure segment under intense pressure to close out last week. Cloud computing and software-as-a-service (SaaS) stocks also generally performed poorly this past week, although they also joined in on the rally.

Entering November, Nvidia was the second-largest holding in the S&P 500 (^GSPC -0.50%), with a nearly 7.6% weighting. That was just behind Apple (AAPL +1.43%), which represented around 7.7% of the index. As such, the stock's recent performance has weighed on S&P 500 exchange-traded funds (ETFs), like the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO -0.48%), which lost 7.8% the week ending Nov. 20.

The Nasdaq Composite(^IXIC -1.48%)was also down. Even with the Monday rally, the question is whether investors should worry that Nvidia and other AI stocks will push the benchmark indexes into a bear market.

The S&P 500 has become less diversified over the past decade, as mega-cap tech companies have come to dominate the index. At the start of the month, over 37% of the stocks in the index were classified as tech stocks, and that doesn't include tech-oriented companies like Amazon (AMZN -1.21%) and Tesla (TSLA -0.20%), which get placed in the consumer discretionary category. Meanwhile, 10 of its top 15 holdings were AI stocks.

Industry observers note that as such, Nvidia and the tech sector are going to have a significant influence on the performance of the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF. However, the great thing about the S&P 500 is that it is self-correcting. As Nvidia and other AI stocks fall, they become less and less of the index, while stocks from other sectors become a larger percentage.

It is this survival-of-the-fittest structure the index has created that has made it such a strong performer over the years. It lets its cream rise to the top, its losers fade, and fresh stock leaders emerge. That's one of the big reasons why, over the past decade, only about 14% of actively managed funds have been able to outperform the S&P 500.

Ultimately, Nvidia doesn't need to be the leader of the S&P 500 going forward. There are plenty of other leaders that can step up, including fellow "Magnificent Seven" names like Alphabet (GOOGL -1.08%) (GOOG -1.05%), Amazon (AMZN -1.21%), or Meta Platforms (META -1.30%), or perhaps even a stock not yet in the index like SpaceX (SPCX -5.65%).

There is also a possibility that what the market is seeing is just a normal correction, and AI infrastructure stocks will bounce back quickly. Regardless, history teaches us that it's time in the market, not timing the market, that wins in the long run.

According to recent reports, as such, I would not be rushing to sell my Vanguard S&P 500 ETF on fears of a further drop in AI stocks, and instead would be using this opportunity to continue to dollar-cost average into the ETF. That's a proven strategy that works again and again over the long term.

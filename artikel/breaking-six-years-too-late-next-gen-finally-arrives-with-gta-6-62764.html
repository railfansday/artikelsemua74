You've probably had this experience in the last few days: That feeling you get, playing a video game, when you can't quite believe what you're looking at. The visuals look too real, the world is too immersive, the game technology is doing things you didn't think possible. Grand Theft Auto 6 gives me that feeling.

This used to be a relatively regular experience for a video game fan. It would come along once — at least once — every seven years or so. Every upcoming console generation would seem like an unfathomable leap forward in game technology from the last. But diminishing returns kicked in with the PlayStation 3/Xbox 360 generation, as exponential increases in processing horsepower were increasingly focused on higher screen resolutions, and as the distance to photorealism got smaller and smaller. By the time PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X arrived, those returns had diminished almost to nothing, under pressure from a range of factors: the huge processing cost of 4K resolution, slow takeup for newly introduced consoles lengthening the cross-gen phase of game development, and most significantly, the cost of developing at this level of fidelity escalating to unsustainable levels. (Related: Latest Update: Razer's MMO mouse hits all-time low pric...)

Sources indicate that there were other tangible advances, of course: backward compatibility as standard, persistent friends lists and game libraries, quick resume and fast loading. But GTA 6 is arguably the first game that makes a persuasive case for why you need a console as powerful as a PS5 or a Series X, the first game that seems like it could never have run on the prior generation. (That said, the game does run on Xbox’s less-powerful hardware, the Series S, albeit with some visual compromises.) There's likely a large cohort of players out there who are finally upgrading for this game after holding out for six whole years.

Sources indicate that but is it really the PS5 and Series X's processing power that gives GTA 6 this wow factor? Is it the generation's-worth of advances in gaming technology that have transpired since Rockstar's last game, Red Dead Redemption 2, in 2018? Or is it the time the game has had in the oven — 13 years since conception, eight years in full production? Is it the skilled work of many thousands of people that has been poured into it? Is it the hundreds of millions, possibly billions of dollars that it cost to make?

Industry observers note that maybe it's all the above. But what takes my breath away about this game is less the way sunlight reflects off Jason's sweaty pores, or the way Lucia's hair waves in the wind, and more the sheer density of its world. Some of that is technological: the engine's ability to handle all those cars, all those pedestrians, all that overlapping audio. But much of it is just hard graft. It's the remarkable, meticulous interiors, so full of handcrafted, lived-in detail, that make this the most visually credible game world I've ever seen.

Sources indicate that as we look toward the next generation of game consoles and what they can possibly bring (not to mention how much they can possibly cost), it's worth remembering that the technological ceiling on video games is now so high that it can only be meaningfully raised by expending gargantuan amounts of money and unfathomable lifetimes of labor. That doesn't mean it isn't worth doing; GTA 6 is incredible. But it does mean we should start looking for upcoming ways to measure progress in this artform.

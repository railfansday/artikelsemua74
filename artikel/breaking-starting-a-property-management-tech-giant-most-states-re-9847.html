According to recent reports, before you take on a single client, get the real estate broker’s or property management license your state requires for managing other owners’ rentals. Then form a limited liability organization, insure the business, open a trust account for owners’ rents and tenants’ deposits, and keep adding homes to manage until the fees pay you a salary.

Rental owners pay a manager to fill vacancies, collect rent, and handle repairs, and a management manufacturer can start with a single owner’s house.

It has been revealed that in most states, managing rentals for other owners takes a real estate broker’s license or a property management license, the Bureau of Labor Statistics states. Maine’s real estate commission, for one, says rental management needs no real estate license there, so check with your own state’s real estate commission before you advertise.

According to recent reports, managers typically charge 8% to 12% of the rent they collect, the National Association of Residential Property Managers indicates. Most also charge a leasing fee each time they place a upcoming tenant, and many charge another when a lease renews. Say you manage 40 single-family homes that stay rented all year at an average rent of $1,800, and you charge 9%. Each home pays you $162 a month, or about $77,800 a year for all 40, before leasing fees and the costs of running the business. (Related: Senate panel vote on National Science Foundation nomine...)

Recent findings suggest that ask real estate agents who don’t manage rentals, and rental investors you know, to refer owners to you. Each owner hires you to screen tenants, collect rent, hire repair crews, inspect the home, and file evictions, all under fair housing law and your state’s security deposit rules.

Sources indicate that put your monthly fee, your leasing fee, and the most you’ll spend on a repair without the owner’s approval in a written management agreement. Then have a real estate attorney in your state review it before your first owner signs.

Breaking: Vertex’s executive chairman: Here’s how we got our drug-trial success rate over 70% when the industry’s is 10% to 20%

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Corporate America is locked in an innovation arms race – a race that is turning out very few winners. Companies are spending billions on R&D, acquisitions and upcoming technologies, operating on the seemingly intuitive premise that making more bets is the best way to approach the uncertainty of the future.

But more bets don’t necessarily produce more successes.

It has been revealed that there’s no more extreme example of this problem than pharmaceuticals and biotech. It takes an average of 10 to 15 years for a drug to go from the lab to regulatory approval. And more than 90% of drugs that enter clinical development fail. Just look at the headlines: one of the world’s largest pharmaceutical companies recently suffered three drug trial failures in a single week despite an enormous R&D budget, many talented scientists and a deep pipeline. Industry observers note that but this problem is far from unique to healthcare. In consumer tech, many startups flame out after a single hit; in automotive, EV pioneers are routinely outpaced by fast followers; digital investments in retail often fail to translate into customer growth. (Related: “Giant superatoms” could finally solve quantum computin...)

In a recent development, the instinctive response when innovation fails is to do more of it: fund more projects, pursue more ideas, make more acquisitions and place more bets. If innovation is inherently unpredictable, the thinking goes, the best way to improve your chances of success is to increase the number of shots you take.

This kind of thinking is completely backward. It’s not about the shots. It’s about the target.

According to recent reports, imagine entering a darts tournament and throwing five consecutive bull’s-eyes, only to discover that you have been throwing at the wrong board. Your accuracy doesn’t matter. You’ve lost.

Sources indicate that more activity creates the appearance of innovation without improving its effectiveness. I learned this the hard way.

In a recent development, when I joined Vertex Pharmaceuticals in 2009, the manufacturer had spent two decades investing heavily in R&D across 20 different programs. Yet, it produced only a single breakthrough product, which was rendered obsolete just two and a half years later with the approval of a better and more tolerable treatment discovered by another company.

Industry observers note that when we examined why drug development failed so frequently, the problem wasn’t that scientists had failed to create a drug that hit its intended biological target. The problem was that they had chosen the wrong target in the first place.

The solution was to take away choices, constraining ourselves to a “sandbox” of diseases with genetically- or pharmacologically-validated targets where we believed we had a much higher probability of success.

Most importantly, both R&D and business development had to stay inside the same sandbox. A fascinating scientific discovery or enticing acquisition wasn’t enough. If it didn’t fit our strategy or if we didn’t have a validated target to address, we walked away.

According to recent reports, that last part may be the most difficult lesson for any leader. Companies often celebrate the ability to say yes to innovation. Sustainable innovators also become very good at saying no. Focus and strategy don’t limit innovation, they accelerate it.

A thriving innovation program requires many parts of an organization working in harmony. Yet companies unintentionally divide innovation into functional silos, with each group incentivized differently and no one focused on the major picture.

It has been revealed that researchers discover something and hand it to developers. Developers hand it to manufacturing. Eventually, commercial teams are asked to sell it. Business development may operate on a separate track entirely, hunting for external opportunities.

But a brilliant invention that cannot be efficiently developed, manufactured or commercialized isn’t a successful innovation.

Sources indicate that to break down internal silos, leaders from research, development, regulatory, manufacturing, commercial and other key business units should be included in decisions about new product development from the earliest stages of the innovation process. And internal and external innovation must be tightly integrated into the same strategy. Business development can’t be given permission to roam the universe looking for any interesting assets; partnerships and acquisitions must reinforce the same strategy, diseases, and targets where a company believes it has a high probability of success.

According to recent reports, innovation shouldn’t be measured by how many ideas an organization generates. It should be judged by how effectively the entire organization converts its best ideas into products that meet people’s needs and create meaningful value.

Industry observers note that once innovation succeeds, companies tend to become complacent and stop innovating to create better and better medicines for the same disease. Our experience seeing one of our breakthrough drugs quickly made obsolete by an innovative competitor created a sense of urgency, and even some paranoia, which drove our organization to continuously try to “out-innovate ourselves.”.

In a recent development, being first doesn’t guarantee long term success. Competitors will learn from your breakthroughs and improve on the foundations you laid. We decided that if someone was going to build a better second-generation product, it should be us.

Industry observers note that the results were dramatic. Vertex went on to develop multiple transformative medicines, including 5 medicines that alter the course of cystic fibrosis for 95% of patients and, with CRISPR Therapeutics, the first FDA-approved human gene-editing therapy for patients with severe Sickle Cell Disease and transfusion-dependent B-thalassemia. More recently we created a completely upcoming class of non-opioid medicines for moderate to severe acute pain.

Even more important to me is what happened to the odds. Our R&D success rate, measured by positive Phase II proof of concept, exceeded 70% across six diseases — far above the 10% to 20% industry average. We didn’t eliminate risk. Innovation can’t exist without risk. But we became much more deliberate about which risks were worth taking and therefore more successful.

Before approving the next project, acquisition or moonshot, leadership teams should ask themselves an important set of questions: Do we understand why innovation has failed here before? Are we pursuing opportunities where we possess a genuine advantage? What evidence tells us that the underlying problem is solvable? Does this opportunity reinforce our existing capabilities – or merely distract from them? And, perhaps hardest of all, what are we willing to stop doing so that our best ideas have the resources to succeed?

The innovation economy has conditioned leaders to admire the size of the pipeline, the number of experiments and the volume of bets. Those are measures of activity, not success.

The companies that sustainably innovate will not be the ones willing to make the most bets. They’ll be the ones that understand their odds better than anyone else and have the discipline to walk away when those odds aren’t good enough.

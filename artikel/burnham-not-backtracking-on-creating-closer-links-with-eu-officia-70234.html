Government wants to let trade deal ‘breathe a bit’, insiders say, after speculation No 10 is dialling back ambitions.

Andy Burnham is not backtracking or delaying his ambitions to create even closer links with the EU in the wake of a landmark summit in Brussels, the Guardian has been told, after signs of an apparent slowdown in the timetable.

The prime minister has promised to set out options for the UK’s long-term relationship with the EU, including the possibility of rejoining, saying last week that these would be outlined later in the year.

But speaking at Friday’s UK-EU summit after finalising a deal on reducing food and drink checks, emissions trading and a mutual youth mobility scheme, Burnham talked instead about “brainstorming” ideas with the EU for what comes next, with a timetable given of some months.

The apparent slowdown prompted speculation No 10 wants to dial back the political pressure and prevent his agenda being swamped by arguments about the EU, with campaign groups being formed before a possible debate about rejoining.

But after Ed Miliband, the foreign secretary, talked warmly on Sunday about the benefits of more integration, government officials mentioned this was still the aim after a “really positive” summit in which the EU side, they said, promised to be as helpful as possible with whatever comes next.

“There is no backtracking or pumping the brakes,” one said. “The government just wants to let this deal breathe a bit, and point to the benefits.”.

Recent findings suggest that another official noted: “There hasn’t been a timetable shift since the summit. It’s being considered alongside the domestic agenda and the 10-year plan.”.

Burnham will seek to keep up the initiative from the summit with a visit on Monday to a branch of Morrisons, in part to see the supermarket chain’s efforts in employing young people, but also to push home his message that the deal on food checks will lower retail prices. Also on Monday the prime minister is expected to brief the Commons on the summit and deal, where he will face a hostile reception from opposition parties, particularly Reform UK.

In a speech to the CBI conference on Monday, Nigel Farage, the Reform leader, will tell business leaders that rejoining the EU would be “a disaster” for the UK economy, and that his party would reverse the deal made in Brussels.

Kemi Badenoch, the Conservative leader, has been critical of Burnham’s negotiations, but noticeably less trenchant about the wider idea of smoothing trade links with Europe, part of a strategy to focus instead on subjects such as the economy and defence.

It has been revealed that “Seamless trade benefits the UK, but it also benefits the EU,” she will say, according to an advance extract of her own speech to the CBI conference. “If we can make our trading relationship better, good. I’m all for working on a smarter deal.”.

In contrast, Theresa May, who carried out the bulk of the Brexit negotiations with Brussels before being replaced by Boris Johnson, made a rare intervention on day-to-day politics to warn Burnham that rejoining would tie up his government for years.

She told the BBC: “That’s one of the reasons why I looked slightly aghast at what Andy Burnham was talking about – further negotiations of going into the EU.

“As a country, we voted for Brexit. When I was prime minister, I was very clear: we should deliver on that vote. So I’m afraid my message to him really is: people voted for Brexit. Brexit is done. Just accept Brexit and move on.”.

In his own interview with the BBC on Sunday, Miliband argued that closer links with the EU would mean the UK having a say on decisions made by Brussels.

“At the moment, what the situation is is that we are essentially rule takers,” the foreign secretary said. “Businesses are rule takers if they want to sell into the European market. So they have to abide by the rules of the European Union, but at the same time, because we are not integrated into that market for food and agricultural products, they have to fill in a whole bunch of forms.”.

He said this was “the worst of all worlds” and it meant there was a clear case to integrate more closely into EU markets.

‘Chhota Bheem’ Creator and ‘Black Warrant’ Publisher Weigh Book-to-Screen Economics at WAVES Film Bazaar

Streaming has changed how Indian authors write, and a screen option can pay a writer more than several editions of a book ever will, publishers, producers and creators noted during a panel at WAVES Film Bazaar, the market of the International Film Festival of India (IFFI) in Goa.

Sources indicate that the session, “From Print to Pixel: The Transmedia Paradigm,” was moderated by journalist Mayank Shekhar. On stage were Priya Kapoor, editorial director of Roli Books; South African filmmaker Tarina Patel, founder and CEO of Tarina P Productions; Rajiv Chilaka, founder and managing director of Green Gold Animation; Raghav Chandra, a retired Indian Administrative Service officer, novelist and founder director of the Bhopal Literature and Art Festival; and Prakash Magdum, former managing director of the National Film Development Corp. (NFDC). Kapoor, who has commissioned and edited titles including “Black Warrant,” “Dongri to Dubai” and “Taj Mahal Foxtrot,” said that since streamers arrived in India, writers have become aware their books can travel beyond the page. (Related: The Age of Machine Learning As Code Has Arrived...)

“We definitely get submissions where you read it and you know that somebody is 100% thinking of a film adaptation, and others who are writing because they had a book in mind,” Kapoor said.

In a recent development, production houses and directors in Mumbai call Roli from time to time with briefs that track the trends – a young adult story, say, or mythology, which is in vogue now. A while back, they wanted hard-hitting non-fiction.

Sources indicate that then there are writers who have already optioned or sold a screen story and come to Roli for a book version, because the deal needs underlying IP. “It’s essentially a screenplay that’s been summarized into a book, and that is where the trouble begins,” Kapoor stated.

Sources indicate that “Black Warrant” took a far less conventional route. Former warden Sunil Gupta, recently retired from Delhi’s Tihar jail, walked into Kapoor’s office with Praveen Jain, a former chief photographer at The Indian Express, and spent an hour telling stories. Gupta couldn’t write, so a legal intern named Satyabhan recorded and transcribed sessions built on Kapoor’s questions, and Sunetra Choudhury, author of the prison book “Behind Bars,” came on board as co-writer. The chapters followed the way Gupta remembered events rather than any episodic strategy, though the show’s writers later told Kapoor the structure suited them.

Industry observers note that gupta, the protagonist of both book and series, is very happy with the result, Kapoor mentioned. “He goes for the shooting also, he goes for the premieres, he’s a star.” He made a point of flagging scenes that never happened, and Kapoor has told him the filmmakers are allowed those creative liberties. The money gap between the two industries is wide. In Indian English-language publishing, Kapoor put a bestseller at 5,000 copies, and a hit adaptation does not change that much. “Our reading audience is not our watching, viewing audience,” she said. A screen deal, though, can pay an author more than five or 10 editions of a book would, even when the project never gets made.

Recent findings suggest that chilaka’s “Chhota Bheem” took the opposite path, starting on TV and spreading outward. The character borrows only the strength of Bheem from the Mahabharata, transplanted into an ordinary village kid. Over 18 years, Green Gold has made more than 1,000 episodes, over 100 TV features, around five feature films and four spinoffs, with some 2,000 characters and more than 1,000 licensed products. Chilaka put the business done on the property at more than INR10 billion ($112 million).

The Netflix spinoff “Mighty Little Bheem,” which reimagines the hero as a baby, is among the streamer’s top preschool shows internationally, Chilaka stated. Green Gold made it without dialogue. “So we gambled with that, and the gamble paid off,” he said. The show also leaves out Bheem’s father, which Chilaka said drew a strong response from single-mother households in North and South America.

Coming next year is “Sing with Bheem,” a musical spinoff series. A stage musical bowed last month at the Royal Opera House in Mumbai, a Chhota Bheem cafe is open in Hyderabad with seven or eight more planned across India, and the character has fronted public campaigns in West Bengal, Chhattisgarh and on Western Railways.

In a recent development, the franchise grew out of insecurity, Chilaka said. Green Gold was a 20- to 30-person studio making an animated “Krishna” while 17 other studios worked on their own Krishna projects. It sold the film to Cartoon Network, but animation budgets never covered production costs, so Chilaka planned for home video, comics, merchandise and birthday parties from the start, and modeled his ensemble on “Friends” so every child could find a match.

“Sustaining is still okay, we’ve done it for 18 years, but getting another hit like that is the biggest challenge because we’ve done so well,” Chilaka noted.

In a recent development, patel, whose credits include “Mandela’s Gun,” has mostly built films from firsthand accounts rather than books. Her current project is a real-life story set against boxing and apartheid, for which she optioned what she judged the best of five or six books on the subject. Announcing it brought out a string of claimants waving documents, followed by a long, stressful stretch with entertainment lawyers.

Sources indicate that “If it’s a public figure, anyone can tell the story. Irrespective of whether there’s a family, there’s an estate, their books, anyone is entitled to tell the story of a public figure,” Patel stated. Diaries, personal photographs and other private family material still require access, she added.

Chandra mentioned the Bhopal festival, now eight editions old, programs a session or two on literature to cinema each year, with past editions covering “12th Fail,” “Bandit Queen” and “The Railway Men.” He pointed to universality and contemporary relevance as what carries a book to the screen. His own novels, “Scent of a Game” and “Kali’s Daughter,” drew interest from a prominent director just before COVID that went nowhere. At the session he indicated an AI-generated pitch deck built from a “Scent of a Game” subplot about the last wild cheetahs in India, put together by a junior colleague in about 15 minutes.

In a recent development, magdum, who headed the National Film Archive of India during Mahatma Gandhi’s 150th birth anniversary, has been mining archival footage of Gandhi for a book, Shekhar noted. Magdum recalled how Fox Movietone crews hauled sound equipment by bullock cart to a Gujarat village to film Gandhi with sync sound shortly after India’s first talkie, “Alam Ara,” opened on March 14, 1931.

According to recent reports, “A book is a very private kind of a thing. You are imagining in your own world whatever is the writer creating,” Magdum stated, adding that an adaptation works when the director captures the soul of what the writer meant.

Asked for their favorite book-to-screen adaptations, Magdum and Patel both picked “Slumdog Millionaire,” drawn from Vikas Swarup’s novel “Q&A.” Patel, a friend of Swarup’s, mentioned he wrote it while posted in Cape Town and that screenwriter Simon Beaufoy’s love story turned it around. Chandra chose “Omkara” and “The Guide,” Chilaka the “Harry Potter” and “The Lord of the Rings” films, and Kapoor “The English Patient.”.

Industry observers note that coreWeave (CRWV -0.08%) has had a brutal month. The AI cloud provider, which rents out Nvidia-powered computing capacity to tech giants, closed at $39.51 on Nov. 20, below its $40 IPO price from March 2025. It briefly rebounded above $48 on Monday, then gave most of that back. At about $42.57 as of Wednesday's market close, the stock is down roughly 70% from its 52-week high of $138.25 and down nearly 77% from its all-time closing high. Wall Street analysts, meanwhile, are lowering their expectations, but not by much, and the gap between the bulls and the market is the crux of the debate.

Cantor Fitzgerald on Wednesday lowered its price target to $96 from $100 and kept its overweight rating. The firm stated fears about CoreWeave's business model "continue to be overblown" and told clients to "be thankful for the discount the market has given them." Cantor said the cut reflects a higher expected share count, because raising equity at a lower stock price means issuing more shares. (Related: How to train a newly introduced language model from scr...)

A day earlier, Evercore ISI cut its target to $80 from $100 and kept its outperform rating. The analyst said the sharp correction in AI stocks has put more focus on pricing and downside risks, and assessed where the sell-off "might have overextended.".

From Wednesday's closing price, Cantor's target implies upside of about 125%, and Evercore's implies about 89%.

According to recent reports, coreWeave's lenders are far less relaxed. Its notes due October 2031 fell nearly 2 cents to about 74 cents on the dollar on Wednesday, pushing their yield to 17.91%, Reuters reported, citing Tradeweb data. Its notes due June 2030 fell to a record low of 76.55 cents. The cost of insuring CoreWeave's debt against default has also climbed to levels usually seen at distressed companies.

According to recent reports, A yield near 18% is a warning sign. It means lenders are demanding a return closer to what you'd expect from a stock, and bondholders get paid before shareholders. CoreWeave does have a cheaper way to borrow: its banks are preparing to sell a $4.5 billion loan backed by chips it is installing for Meta Platforms (META +3.40%) as soon as next week, Bloomberg reported. Because Meta is the customer, that loan is expected to be rated investment-grade and priced around 7% to 7.5%. But that kind of financing only works for capacity tied to a specific customer contract, not for the company as a whole.

Even Cantor concedes the market is "extremely focused" on one concern: CoreWeave's need for as much as $5 billion of "parent capital" in 2027, meaning money raised at the manufacturer level to support its build-out. Cantor thinks management should address the funding plan "sooner rather than later" and remarked a clear answer could act as a "potential clearing event" for the stock.

That's the crux. If CoreWeave raises capital through newly introduced shares at today's prices, existing shareholders are diluted. If it borrows, it pays rates the bond market has just pushed sharply higher. Either route makes it harder to reach the upside in those price targets.

For most investors, not yet. The analysts may well be right that the sell-off overshot, and Cantor says its review of how CoreWeave resigns customer contracts gives it more confidence in the model. But when the firm's own bonds yield nearly 18%, the market is pricing in real risk that shareholders would absorb first.

Recent findings suggest that risk-tolerant investors who believe in long-term AI demand could start a small position, but the smarter move is to wait for the 2027 funding strategy Cantor is asking for. If management lays out a credible way to raise that money without heavy dilution, the stock could rerate rapidly. Until then, there are less leveraged ways to own the AI build-out among cloud stocks and semiconductor stocks.

Countries clash over fossil fuels as COP31 ends without a major climate deal

COP31 ended without a major climate agreement after countries clashed over fossil fuels, emissions cuts and climate funding.

Industry observers note that the final draft of the main agreement at this year’s UN climate summit had one glaring omission. Nowhere in the document did the words fossil fuels appear. (Related: Industry News: My Journey to a serverless transformers ...)

Several countries rejected it for that reason.

Recent findings suggest that COP31 ended on Sunday, November 22, in Antalya, Türkiye. The talks lasted two weeks and then ran two more days. That made it the longest UN climate summit on record.

Governments still did not agree on the main text.

UN Secretary-General António Guterres called the outcome “deeply disappointing.”.

“There may be no consensus in the negotiating room. There is no ambiguity in the science,” Guterres remarked in a statement.

Recent findings suggest that murat Kurum, Türkiye’s environment minister, was the president of COP31. He had little good news at the end of the summit.

“I understand that there is no agreement on this text. For that reason, these decisions have not been adopted,” Kurum said.

According to recent reports, australia led the negotiations. For several days it worked with Türkiye on latest versions of the agreement. Both countries tried to find language that everyone could accept. (Related: How to train a newly introduced language model from scr...)

Industry observers note that some countries wanted a firm commitment to use less coal, oil and gas. Countries that sell fossil fuels abroad worried about what that would mean for their economies.

The proposed text said the world is heading for more than 2.7°F (1.5°C) of warming.

Under the Paris Agreement, governments agreed to hold warming well below 2°C above preindustrial levels and pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5°C.

The text also asked for more technology to remove carbon dioxide from the air. It remarked nothing about burning less fossil fuel in the first place.

The European Union would not accept an agreement that avoided the subject. The bloc described the omission as “unconscionable.”.

Sources indicate that panama had sent a letter to the summit presidency with its priorities. Its delegation said governments had a responsibility “not to negotiate the minimum threshold, but to act.”.

In the Marshall Islands, where much of the land lies just above sea level, the stakes are particularly high.

Industry observers note that accepting the deal, the country’s delegation remarked, would mean betraying its own people. It rejected what it called “false solutions” and pushed for a faster, adequately funded shift away from fossil fuels.

Speaking for the African Group, Ghana raised concerns about climate funding and financial assistance for countries suffering climate damage.

There was also the question of how to support workers and communities as fossil fuel industries decline.

That issue got “a single generic sentence,” according to Ghana.

Nevertheless, the African Group chose not to block the deal, acknowledging that the text reflected the views of many countries.

Saudi Arabia saw things differently. Speaking for the League of Arab States, it defended keeping carbon capture and storage in the agreement.

This technology traps carbon dioxide and stores it before it reaches the atmosphere.

Was this simply greenwashing? Saudi Arabia argued otherwise, pointing to scientific assessments that give carbon capture a role in limiting global warming.

In a recent development, pakistan urged countries to keep talking. Its delegation noted climate action needs “bridges, not barriers.”.

Part of the frustration at COP31 goes back three years.

Sources indicate that at the 2023 climate summit in Dubai, governments agreed to transition away from fossil fuels. Guterres mentioned countries now need to speed up that transition and pay for it.

“We can’t put out the fire while pouring fuel on it,” he mentioned.

Guterres also blamed powerful fossil fuel interests for holding back climate action while they keep making profits.

It has been revealed that he wants deeper emissions cuts before 2030. He also wants more money for vulnerable countries to cope with climate change.

Not everything fell apart in Antalya. Governments agreed on several smaller decisions.

Simon Stiell, the head of UN Climate Change, pointed to progress on the just transition.

In a recent development, picture a town where hundreds of families depend on a coal mine. When the mine closes, those families need help. A just transition means supporting workers and communities through changes like that.

Governments will continue their work on the matter for three more years. They will also set up a mechanism in stages. Intends for it were first agreed at last year’s summit in Belém, and formal adoption is expected at COP32.

Countries that make up about half of the world economy backed a goal of 35% electrification by 2035.

In practice, that means more cars, heating systems and factory machines running on electricity instead of burning fuel.

The International Energy Agency estimates the pledge could raise the global electrification rate from 23% to more than 33% by 2035.

Stiell noted progress like this could help bring about positive tipping points in climate action.

Tropical rainforests got some good news too. Pledges to the Tropical Forest Forever Facility, which helps protect these forests, reached $8.3 billion.

Stiell talked openly about what went wrong in Antalya in his closing remarks. “The world cannot afford a repeat of this moment,” he said.

He also spoke about how governments make decisions at these summits.

It has been revealed that “This process belongs to 194 Parties. Consensus works best from the ground up,” Stiell said.

In a recent development, next year, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, will host the climate talks. Countries will pick up where they left off in Türkiye.

In a recent development, there is still no agreement on how quickly the world should move away from coal, oil and gas. For poorer nations, another problem remains: who will pay for the transition?

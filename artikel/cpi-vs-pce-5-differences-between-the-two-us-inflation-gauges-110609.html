CPI and PCE both measure U.S. inflation but differ on publisher, use, scope, weights and substitution. See 5 differences.

According to recent reports, anyone tracking U.S. inflation soon runs into two competing figures: CPI, the Consumer Price Index, and PCE, a price index for personal consumption expenditures. The two rarely match, and the Federal Reserve prefers PCE. A reader who checks only one of them could come away more worried, or less worried, than the other measure would justify. Any gap between them comes from deliberate choices, made by the people who build each measure, about what to count and how much each cost should matter. Recent findings suggest that the difference matters because each measure settles something real, and the two settle different things for different people. Fed officials judge their own record against an inflation target, so the reading they watch tells them whether they are succeeding. The yearly Social Security raise goes to about 75 million people, according to CNBC, and its size depends on an inflation index. When the two measures move apart, policymakers and retirees can be looking at different versions of the same year. Knowing which number a headline reports is therefore more than trivia. It tells a reader whose decision rests on that number.

The Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland has laid out how the two main U.S. inflation gauges differ, from whose spending each one counts to how much weight housing carries and what happens to each gauge when shoppers trade down. On the latest core readings, PCE inflation ran at 3.3% through October 2026 and CPI inflation at 2.6% through September 2026. Those readings cover different months, but both sit above the Fed's 2% target. None of the differences between the gauges makes one measure right and the other wrong. Each choice suits a different job, which is why one economy can produce two honest readings. (Related: Breaking: Influencer “screwed” after AI trick to get fl...)

Industry observers note that the two gauges come from different agencies, with the Bureau of Labor Statistics producing the CPI and the Bureau of Economic Analysis producing PCE, according to a 2014 analysis by the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland. BEA publishes PCE as part of its consumer spending data, so the Fed judges its own progress with a figure that another agency builds. The agency behind a number therefore matters. Two statistical agencies with their own methods can examine the same economy and report different results.

According to recent reports, separate agencies suggest two independent readings of prices, but the agencies do not work from separate raw material. BEA lists the Consumer Price Index, the Producer Price Index and the Consumer Expenditures Survey among its major sources for PCE, and all three are products of the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The gauge that usually appears beside the CPI is built partly from the CPI. When the two diverge, much of the gap can come from how each agency weighs and combines shared inputs, rather than from prices moving in one gauge and not in the other. A gap between CPI and PCE is a question about method first.

Industry observers note that for most households, CPI is the inflation number that matters. In a 2014 analysis, the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland describes CPI as the gauge used to adjust Social Security payments and the one cited in news headlines. The CPI that sets a retiree's raise is not the headline index, however. Social Security uses CPI-W, CNBC reports, and that index is the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers, built around one group's spending. A news story about "CPI" and the figure behind a benefit check can therefore describe the same month with two different readings.

The two readings do part ways. Over the 12 months to February 2026, headline CPI rose 2.4% and CPI-W rose 2.2%, according to , so the number in the news overstated the one that matters for checks. Retirees received a 2.8% raise in 2026, more than either of those readings, because the Social Security Administration typically reveals each year's adjustment in October, months before those readings came out. For a retiree, each raise reflects inflation already past rather than prices now.

Credit: Tierra Mallorca / Unsplash.

According to recent reports, the two gauges divide household spending in different proportions, so the same price changes can move them by different amounts. The Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland explains, in an analysis published in 2014, that the CPI puts a larger share of its basket on housing, while the PCE puts a larger share on medical care. The CPI's tilt toward housing is large: shelter had a weight of 35.238% in the index as of September 2026, according to BLS. Medical care services make up only 6.813% of the CPI. A jump in rents lands harder on the CPI, and a jump in doctor and hospital bills lands harder on the PCE.

According to recent reports, most of the CPI's housing weight is not rent checks or mortgage payments. Its largest piece is owners' equivalent rent, an estimate of what owned homes would rent for, and it makes up 25.853% of the index on its own, according to BLS. When the CPI's housing reading rises, much of that increase is in the estimated rental value of owned homes. Homeowners' actual monthly payments may not have changed at all.

BEA is open about where its own gauge is weakest. "Detailed level estimates are more likely to be either based on judgmental trends or on less reliable source data than estimates at higher level aggregates," BEA said in a note on its consumer spending tables. That warning comes from the agency that makes PCE, and it tells readers how to use the gauge. The headline PCE figure is the firmer number, and a single PCE category deserves more caution than its precise decimal suggests.

Knowing who publishes a gauge tells a reader whose methods shaped the number and where its own maker says it is weak.

The same lag ties each raise to prices from months earlier. The February figures came before the oil shocks tied to the Iran war, CNBC reports, so the cool benefit-index reading may have come just before a jump. Because benefits follow CPI-W, a rise in consumer prices tied to oil feeds into the next raise. "Geo/political tensions are driving up the price of oil right now which will continue to drive up my estimates of the COLA," Mary Johnson, an independent Social Security and Medicare analyst, told CNBC.

Retirees who want to know where their checks are heading should watch CPI-W, not headline CPI or the Fed's PCE.

Sources indicate that the two gauges start from different answers to whose bills count. CPI counts only what urban households pay out of their own pockets, leaving out rural areas and any spending that someone else covers for them, according to a 2014 analysis by the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland. PCE reaches further and counts costs that others pay on a household's behalf, such as a worker's health insurance when the employer covers it. A premium an employer covers can show up in the Fed's preferred gauge and never appear in CPI, so the two aren't pricing the same set of bills to begin with. The harder gap to spot lies inside categories that carry the same name in both.

A shared label can hide different contents. The Federal Reserve Board reports that PCE takes its software and accessories price straight from a Bureau of Labor Statistics category in the CPI. The CPI item behind it is named Computer Software and Blank Media, yet blank media has a weight of 0% in PCE's software category. The price PCE borrows describes a different basket from the one PCE is meant to measure. In this way, a CPI quirk can enter the Fed's preferred gauge without anyone changing a definition.

According to recent reports, the mismatch showed up in the goods inflation that policymakers watch. Over four months, at an annualized pace, core goods prices in the PCE climbed 5.5%, but the pace falls to 2.7% once software is removed, according to the Federal Reserve Board. Software accounted for much of the recent heat in goods prices. Alessandro Barbarino, Anthony Diercks and Stephen Miran, staff at the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, say measurement error may explain part of the category's recent rise. Under a scenario the three built themselves, rather than an official revision, fixing the label mismatch would lower how much software adds to core goods PCE by as much as 25%. That would trim the reading, but it leaves open how much of the rise reflects real price changes.

When PCE goods inflation suddenly runs hot, it is worth asking what sits inside the categories borrowed from CPI before treating the spike as real.

It has been revealed that housing costs are still rising. On a seasonally adjusted basis, shelter rose 0.3% from September to October 2026, according to BLS. Because housing has a heavier weight in the CPI, that increase adds more to the CPI reading than to the PCE reading. Neither set of weights is wrong. Each reflects a different picture of where household money goes, so every headline figure depends partly on how much weight each cost was given.

Before trusting either figure, check whether housing or medical care accounts for most of the change.

Credit: Nathália Rosa / Unsplash.

Shoppers rarely keep buying the same thing after its price jumps. They switch to something cheaper, and the agencies behind each gauge have to decide how promptly to count that switch. PCE is reweighted more often as shoppers trade down, which tends to leave CPI slightly higher, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland. The gap comes from timing. CPI's weights are updated less often than PCE's, and between updates the index keeps counting the pricier item that shoppers have already left behind. In practice, CPI measures what a household used to buy, while PCE measures something closer to what it buys after adapting. A family that has already switched to a cheaper brand can look more squeezed in CPI than it is at the checkout.

Recent findings suggest that across many months, the slower updates usually put CPI a little above PCE. A reader who follows both numbers would therefore expect CPI to be the hotter one. That edge is a tendency rather than a guarantee, and any single reading can break it. The effect of shoppers trading down is also small. In a given period, other differences between the two gauges can outweigh it and push PCE above CPI.

Don't assume which gauge is higher. The usual order is only a starting point, so check both before deciding whether inflation is cooling or heating up.

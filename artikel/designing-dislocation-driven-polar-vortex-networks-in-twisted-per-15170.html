Designing dislocation-driven polar vortex networks in twisted perovskites

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Twisting two atomic layers produces a geometric moiré pattern but bonding-induced interfacial reconstruction fundamentally transforms this into an ordered dislocation network—a distinction obscured in weakly bonded van der Waals systems. Although in-plane topological vortex nanostructures arising from twisting-induced lateral strain modulation have been linked to periodic moiré patterns in freestanding perovskite layers1,2,3 and 2D bilayers4, their coupling to the interfacial dislocation network in twisted layers remains unresolved. Here we demonstrate that twisted freestanding SrTiO3 (STO) layers undergo interfacial reconstruction into a network of screw dislocations, accompanied by the emergence of in-plane topological vortices. Unlike in previous reports, these vortices are associated with the dislocation network rather than with geometric moiré patterns. Four-dimensional scanning transmission electron microscopy (4DSTEM) reveals a beam deflection texture in registry with the interfacial dislocation network. A machine learning interatomic potential, trained on first-principles calculations, together with phase-field modelling, confirms that competing strains within the dislocation network stabilize polar vortex–antivortex pairs; first-principles calculations further reveal the emergence of an electronic superlattice with a well-defined periodicity. Our results establish twist-controlled dislocation networks as a newly introduced and versatile route to designing local polar and electronic structures in oxide materials.

Dislocations are topological line defects central to materials science5,6. By relieving elastic energy through local symmetry breaking, they strongly modify mechanical, electronic and structural properties. In two-dimensional materials, ordered defect networks and moiré structures demonstrate that controlled symmetry breaking can generate emergent electronic and optical states7,8,9,10,11,12, establishing dislocations as tunable building blocks for latest functionalities. Recent findings suggest that although two-dimensional materials provide a powerful platform for moiré engineering, they are mostly limited to compounds with non-covalent interlayer bonding, in which coupling is mediated by weakly hybridized s and p electrons. This inherently restricts the range of accessible interactions and properties. By contrast, transition metal oxides (TMOs), governed by strongly correlated d electrons, host a much richer electronic landscape absent from most conventional two-dimensional materials. Their strong coupling between charge, spin and orbital degrees of freedom result in phenomena including high-temperature superconductivity13, colossal magnetoresistance14, Mott insulating states15 and multiferroicity16. TMOs are traditionally grown epitaxially17,18, in which lattice matching and coherent interfaces restrict viable material combinations. Quasi-atomically thin freestanding oxide membranes now provide an alternative route to synthetic heterostructures through layer-by-layer stacking3,19,20,21. By bypassing the constraints of heteroepitaxy, this approach enables interfacial configurations and material pairings that are otherwise thermodynamically or kinetically inaccessible. It also permits control of the relative in-plane orientation between stacked membranes, creating twisted interfaces and in-plane moiré patterns2,3,22.

It has been revealed that stacking two freestanding membranes with a twist angle θ produces a geometric moiré superlattice with observed periodicity λ = (a/2)/sin(θ/2), in which a is the cubic lattice constant23. In two-dimensional materials, long-period moirés at small θ relax into stacked domains separated by dislocation networks24,25 and a similar network has been observed in twisted Si (refs. 26,27,28). Because coherent interfacial regions are energetically favoured, lattice strain drives dislocation formation at small twist angles, whereas larger angles promote preferred coincidence-site-lattice (CSL) configurations29. As illustrated schematically in Fig. 1a, interfacial energy at small angles scales with dislocation density30, whereas at larger angles, discrete CSL configurations produce local energy minima. Dislocation formation is therefore expected to be favourable below about 15° (ref. 31). However, direct experimental evidence for such networks in freestanding TMO membranes, and their impact on structural, physical and electronic properties (Fig. 1b), remains lacking32.

Industry observers note that we show experimentally and theoretically that twisted freestanding STO membranes reconstruct into an ordered interfacial network of polar screw dislocations. High-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) and 4DSTEM, supported by ab initio TEM (abTEM) simulations and a density functional theory (DFT)-trained machine-learned interatomic potential (MLIP), reveal a long-range ordered dislocation pattern with polar vortex arrays in the interface plane. Each moiré plaquette forms a locally coherent interface, whereas strain is accommodated by an orthogonal square network of screw dislocations. Phase-field modelling on the basis of atomistic strain gradients shows that flexoelectric polarization localizes along the dislocation lines, with in-plane nodal chirality of polar vortex–antivortex arrays and an out-of-plane component. DFT indicates that the reconstructed interface is chiral and hosts mid-gap states absent in bulk STO, with potential implications for electronic and optoelectronic properties.

Recent findings suggest that we studied STO/STO homobilayers with twist angles of 5°, 7.5°, 10° and 20°, for which dislocations are expected at or below the critical twist angle of about 15°. The rigid moiré pattern is visualized by overlaying two periodic STO (100) plane grids with a 5° relative rotation, without allowing for interfacial reconstruction. The experimental HAADF-STEM image (Fig. 2a) shows a well-defined moiré pattern that closely resembles the rigid moiré structure (Fig. 2b). By contrast, Fig. 2c shows the interfacial relaxed structure obtained from the MLIP-DFT calculations (SrO–TiO2 termination; see Methods, Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Figs. 5–8 for details). The relaxed structure reveals coherent domains separated by a network of screw dislocations, highlighted by the black square. These results indicate a dislocation network, absent from both the experimental image and the rigid structural model. This apparent discrepancy reflects the finite depth sensitivity of STEM imaging (Supplementary Note 3). The experimental HAADF signal is not generated solely by two bonded atomic planes comprising the interface but contains contributions from all unit cells above and below it, as illustrated schematically in Fig. 2a. The measured contrast therefore represents a depth-averaged projection of the twisted bilayer and naturally recovers an apparently rigid moiré pattern. Hence, Fig. 2a–d highlights that agreement between an experimental plan-view HAADF image and a rigid moiré model should not be interpreted as evidence for the absence of interfacial reconstruction. Rather, the depth-integrated nature of the HAADF signal can conceal the reconstructed interface altogether.

The relation between the rigid moiré pattern and the dislocation network can be understood in terms of local stacking. Following the previous convention3,33, we label the stacking configurations as AA and AB. However, because the repeated moiré sites are not all crystallographically equivalent, BA and AA′ regions must be distinguished (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 1a,b). On relaxation, the interface expands the energetically favourable commensurate AA and AA′ domains and concentrates the remaining twist-induced mismatch into screw dislocations located between AB and BA regions (Fig. 2e). The spacing between adjacent dislocation lines is denoted as d (Supplementary Note 2). In a strongly bonded oxide, this reconstruction maximizes interfacial coordination by increasing the area of coherent bonding across the interface (Extended Data Fig. 1). Away from the interface, the atoms regain full coordination and the driving force for reconstruction is removed, leading to rapid decay of the strain along the thickness direction (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10).

Recent findings suggest that to visualize strain in the interfacial layer, we calculated the strain field using MLIP-DFT. We show that the reconstructed interface is distinct from a rigid moiré pattern, with strain governed by the square dislocation network (Fig. 2f). The in-plane shear component, εxy, exhibits sharp gradients along the square dislocation lines, whereas the centres of the square domains remain coherent and weakly strained (Fig. 2f, Extended Data Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12). This indicates that the strain is localized at the screw-dislocation cores and arises from interfacial reconstruction, differing fundamentally from strain maps inferred directly from STEM images, in which the apparent strain follows the rigid moiré periodicity1,2,3,33. To compare with earlier analyses, we also estimated strain from the HAADF focal series (Supplementary Note 4 and Supplementary Figs. 13–16). This yielded a vortex-like strain pattern, consistent with previous reports1,2,3,33 but distinct from the interface-resolved MLIP-DFT strain field, which is governed by the dislocation network.

To assess the thickness dependence of the simulated STEM contrast and its relation to experiment, we added further layers above and below the relaxed interface in the abTEM simulations. In HAADF-STEM, the added layers drive the contrast towards the experimentally observed rigid moiré pattern. The same approach was applied to low-angle annular dark-field (LAADF)-STEM simulations (Supplementary Fig. 17), a mode commonly used to image dislocations owing to its high sensitivity to small atomic displacements34. With increasing thickness, the simulated LAADF contrast becomes progressively more diffuse, partially obscuring the screw-dislocation network and closely matching the experimentally observed LAADF focal series (Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19).

For the 20° twisted bilayer, the relaxed interface no longer exhibits the pronounced screw-dislocation network observed at smaller twist angles (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 3). Accordingly, the experimental HAADF/LAADF image agrees well with the simulated HAADF/LAADF images as well as experimental shear strain, based on a relaxed moiré structure (Fig. 2g,h and Supplementary Figs. 20–23), unlike the 5° twisted bilayer (Fig. 2a,c). These results indicate a crossover from dislocation-mediated reconstruction at small twist angles to a non-dislocated interface at larger twist angles. The corresponding strain distribution follows the spatial periodicity of the rigid moiré pattern, in contrast to the 5° case (Fig. 2i).

Consistent with this, both cross-sectional views show periodic dark-contrast features at the expected dislocation positions: 4.5 nm in Fig. 3d and 3.2 nm in Fig. 3e.

Pixel-intensity analysis based on several samples (Supplementary Fig. 25) yields spacings of d1 = 4.2 ± 0.3 nm in Fig. 3d and d2 = 3.0 ± 0.3 nm in Fig. 3e, in excellent agreement with the MLIP predictions. The recurring dark contrast is consistent with strain at the dislocation cores and lines, which lowers HAADF intensity through local disorder, lattice tilt and enhanced dechannelling. Such a regular dislocation array is unlikely without a sharp, strongly bonded interface35. Consistent with this, the reconstruction is confined to a few atomic planes and the dark contrast in Fig. 3d,e arises from the interface rather than imaging artefacts (Supplementary Fig. 24). The measured spacings differ by approximately √2, exactly as expected for a square lattice viewed along (100) and its diagonal (110), providing independent experimental confirmation of a square interfacial dislocation network. A Burgers circuit analysis for the θ = 5° relaxed structure provides a Burgers vector magnitude equal to the bulk STO lattice constant (|b| = 3.9 Å; Methods). A similar dislocation network was also observed for twist angles of 7.5° and 10°, with spacings in cross-section agreeing well with their expected values (Extended Data Fig. 4).

To determine whether the dislocation cores are also chemically distinct, we performed spatially resolved EELS (Methods) across the 5° twisted interface. The analysis was centred on the interfacial region marked in Fig. 3f, in which the HAADF-STEM imaging reveals a localized contrast feature at the dislocation core, labelled ‘D’, relative to the surrounding registry region, labelled ‘R’. Spectra acquired from D show clear differences from those measured in the bulk-like membrane region and from neighbouring non-dislocated interfacial areas (numbered (1)–(5) in Fig. 3f–h). Quantification of the elemental edges reveals the presence of Ti3+, indicating local oxygen deficiency at the dislocation core D as compared with R.

According to recent reports, the Ti L2,3-edge fine structure at D provides further evidence for a modified local bonding environment. The difference between the fine structure of the two core loss spectra indicates a reduction in the crystal-field splitting at the dislocation core compared with the surrounding region without a dislocation (Fig. 3i). This change is consistent with altered Ti–O coordination and partial oxygen-vacancy formation at the screw-dislocation core reported for screw dislocations in STO (ref. 36), supporting our assignment of chemically and structurally distinct dislocation cores.

To determine the functional properties of the dislocation network, we mapped the in-plane beam deflection at the twisted interface using 4DSTEM centre-of-mass (CoM) imaging and abTEM simulations along with phase-field modelling (Fig. 4a–h and Methods). In the 5° bilayer, the experimental CoM deflection map shows a repeating nanoscale texture with 4.7 ± 0.2 nm spacing, matching the reconstructed dislocation network (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 26). The strongest deflections are concentrated at the screw-dislocation crossings (dashed white circles), where the interfacial distortion is largest, with weaker contrast along the connecting dislocation lines (white rectangles). This assignment is supported by abTEM simulations: the MLIP-relaxed interface reproduces the periodic CoM contrast registry with the dislocation network (Fig. 4c), whereas a rigidly moiré twisted, unrelaxed structure does not (Fig. 4d). Thus, the measured field texture is not simply a moiré artefact but arises from atomic reconstruction at the bonded interface (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Note 5 and Supplementary Figs. 27–30). As seen in the CoM map, at the AB/BA stacking centres, the field winding number is n = +1. This is also reproduced in the abTEM simulation using the MLIP-relaxed atomic positions (Fig. 4b,c,e). At these specimen thicknesses, the CoM deflection follows from the projected atomic potential, any local field and multiple scattering within the sample37,38; we therefore read it as a structural signature of the dislocation network, with its polar character supported by the relaxed-structure displacements and phase-field results. Figure 4f shows the Ti-atom off-centring in-plane displacements in the MLIP-relaxed structure (black arrows, visualized in the SrO-terminated interface), which reveals that the twisted interface is chiral, hosting only clockwise displacement vorticity with n = +1. Hence, the screw character of the dislocations selects a single winding sense, breaking the mirror symmetry that would otherwise enforce equal populations of handedness.

To investigate the origin of the polarization, we implemented a phase-field model using MLIP-DFT-evaluated strain gradients of the θ = 5° reconstructed interface (about 0.03 nm−1), which exceed literature reports39,40 and lie firmly in the flexoelectric regime41. Our results show that the dislocation geometry underpins the emergence of flexoelectric polarization (|P| ≈ 2 μC cm−2) that localizes along the dislocation lines, particularly the AA–AA′ boundaries (Fig. 4g, Methods, Supplementary Note 6 and Supplementary Figs. 31–35). This polarization is confined to the interface and decays within about 2 nm, revealing non-zero out-of-plane components (Pz) (Fig. 4h and Extended Data Fig. 5) indicating a decaying strength of strain gradients into registry (Supplementary Figs. 10, 31 and 32). Although the in-plane shear εxy is strongly confined to the interface, accompanying out-of-plane strain gradients couple to Pz through the cubic flexoelectric tensor. Within the flexoelectric functional used here, the model yields polarization vortices of alternating clockwise and anticlockwise handedness at the network nodes—an achiral texture that preserves topological charge neutrality (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Figs. 33 and 34). In the 20° bilayer, the polarization also reveals alternating clockwise/anticlockwise vortices with handedness reversal across the interface (Supplementary Fig. 33), indicating that twist-induced polar textures persist beyond the dislocation-rich regime—in agreement with other works42.

According to recent reports, electronic structure calculations on the relaxed MLIP-DFT structure show that, for a low-angle interface, occupied states within 0.3 eV of the valence-band maximum (Ev) localize near the highly strained screw-dislocation lines (Fig. 4i, Methods, Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. 36). A partial density of states (PDOS) analysis indicates that these states lie above the bulk-like valence bands of less strained regions, whereas decomposition into node and edge-line contributions confirms their concentration at the dislocation network (Extended Data Fig. 6). Thus, the ordered screw-dislocation network may support emergent electronic behaviour as well as upcoming electromechanical properties. Consistent with this prediction, spatially resolved EELS across the network reveals electronic reconstruction localized to the dislocations (Fig. 3f–i). The electronic structure and polar topology, including possible extrinsic effects from oxygen vacancies, remain key targets for future work.

We present a defect-by-design approach: tuning interlayer commensurability drives self-assembly of targeted defect structures. At low twist angle, the interface reconstructs into a square, ordered polar dislocation network whose periodicity is set by the twist angle, providing a direct tunable structure parameter. Unlike in previous reports, the observed vortex–antivortex arrays at the interface are linked to the morphology of the dislocation network, not to a geometric moiré pattern. Interfacial termination (for example, SrO–SrO versus TiO2–TiO2) is also decisive, as different terminations stabilize distinct local bonding and thus distinct interfacial responses.

The interface reconstruction produces large strain gradients that drive strong flexoelectric polarization, which localizes along dislocation edge lines and extends several nanometres out of plane, imprinting the electromechanical response of the interface into adjacent layers. 4DSTEM CoM measurements reveal a non-trivial in-plane internal deflection texture at the dislocation cores, consistent with finite polar order in nominally paraelectric STO. The charge density at dislocation lines and nodes differs markedly from the bulk, highlighting the reconstructed interface as a platform for emergent electronic phenomena.

Recent findings suggest that these results show that reconstructed interfacial defects crucially determine polar chirality and electronic states in twisted membranes. More broadly, they suggest a bottom-up route to functional layered materials and devices, in which nanoscale control of twist angle and termination programs dislocation networks and the coupled strain, polarization, topology and emergent electronic properties they host.

The total thickness for the cross-section is assumed to be approximately 54 nm in the beam direction. In the cross-section simulations, periodic boundary conditions were applied only in the lateral directions, not along the beam direction, enabling tiling of the images in-plane. Both the plan-view and cross-section images include an interpolation with a Fourier-space padding of 0.2 pixels Å−1, followed by Gaussian blurring with a standard deviation of 0.5 Å, to better match the experimental contrast. Poisson noise was added using an electron dose of 4.2 × 106 e− Å−1, consistent with the experimental imaging conditions. A flexible annular detector spanning 0–200 mrad with a 1-mrad step size was used to generate the LAADF and HAADF detector ranges of 32–120 and 60–200, respectively.

According to recent reports, for 4DSTEM simulations, the electrostatic potential was calculated using the Lobato parameterization, with a sampling of 0.05 Å and a slice thickness of 1 Å, in the infinite-projection approximation. A vacuum region of 2 Å was included above and below the specimen along the beam direction (z). Electron propagation was simulated using the multislice method at 300 keV. The incident probe was defined by a semi-convergence angle of 2 mrad, a spherical aberration coefficient of 0.159 μm and a probe sampling of 0.5 Å. For each structure studied (Supplementary Note 5), the interface position was determined as the midpoint between the entrance and exit surfaces of the specimen. The probe defocus was set equal to the interface depth, so the probe was focused at the interface plane. The probe scanned the entire simulation cell using fractional scan coordinates that spanned the unit cell. At each probe position, diffraction patterns were recorded with a pixelated detector, with a maximum collection angle of 3 mrad, yielding a complete 4DSTEM dataset. CoM images were calculated directly from the simulated diffraction datasets. Virtual bright-field images were generated by integrating the diffraction intensity over 0–2 mrad. The CoM maps in Fig. 4c–e were generated using Gaussian averaging to mimic the experimental resolution of 2.7 Å per pixel.

Sources indicate that the DFT relaxations, which were used as data for the MLIP training, were performed at selected twist angles θ with small and computationally tractable moiré supercells from the method of refs. 42,47 (Supplemental Fig. 5) using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation (VASP) software48 with the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE)49. The local density approximation was used with the Hubbard +U correction of +5.3 eV applied to the Ti d orbitals50 within the projector-augmented wave formalism using a plane-wave cut-off of 500 eV. The forces on each atom in the training set were relaxed to less than 0.03 eV Å−1. An automated batch active learning framework for iterative uncertainty-guided data collection and model retraining (CURATOR)51 is used to generate a comprehensive dataset. An ensemble of three PaiNN graph neural network models52 were used, over four active learning iterations, for the uncertainty quantification necessary for the sampling in CURATOR. To maximize model accuracy, the MACE equivariant graph neural network architecture53 is trained on the generated dataset, yielding an accurate MLIP with a mean absolute error of 0.52 meV per atom (Supplementary Fig. 6). For the results presented, we relaxed the atomic structure while scanning the relative in-plane translation at each θ to identify the lowest-energy alignment. We refined the final structures by averaging atomic positions from molecular dynamics simulations at T = 300 K (after discarding the first 10 ps for equilibration out of 50 ps total trajectory), starting from the most stable translation (Supplementary Fig. 8). The in-plane strain tensor components at the interface are estimated from the equilibrated atomic positions using a local reference frame defined by nearest-neighbour averaging, which removes the rigid-body rotational contribution owing to the twist. The electronic structure calculations shown in Fig. 4i and Extended Data Fig. 6 are obtained with Fritz Haber Institute ab initio materials simulations (FHI-aims)54 for the relaxed MLIP-DFT atomic positions, using a linear combination of atomic orbitals in the light basis setting. Scalar relativistic corrections are included in the ZORA atomic expansion and a Hubbard correction of +U = 4.3 eV is applied to the Ti d orbitals (ensuring numerical consistency between the two DFT codes used; Supplementary Fig. 36). The atomic structures shown in this work are visualized with VESTA55. The magnitudes and directions of the Burgers vectors presented in Fig. 2e are verified using OVITO’s internal dislocation extraction analysis56. See Supplementary Note 2 for expanded and self-contained details on this approach.

Industry observers note that the 3D phase-field modelling used in this work follows the approach outlined in ref. 57, which accounts for both electrostrictive and flexoelectric coupling in cubic perovskites. The total free energy density includes bulk, gradient and elastic contributions as well as the coupling to the electric field. The polarization, elastic displacement and electrostatic potential are solved self-consistently at each time step through the time-dependent Landau–Ginzburg–Devonshire equation, mechanical equilibrium and the Poisson equation, respectively. The fourth-order Landau parameterization for STO is taken from ref. 58 and measurements of the flexoelectric coupling strength are found in ref. 41. The in-plane linear elastic strains, (εxx, εyy, εxy), owing to the interface reconstruction as estimated by the MLIP-DFT model, are fit to a truncated 2D Fourier series (N = 6) and used as an eigenstrain input for the phase-field simulation of the bilayer stack (total thickness 20 nm). These inhomogeneous strain fields are first reconstructed, ensuring strain compatibility. Periodicity is enforced on all system variables (elastic displacements and polarization vector components, as well as the electrostatic potential) in the xy coordinates. A decomposition method59 is used to ensure xy-periodic strain tensor components. The top and bottom surfaces are held fixed elastically, with the twist boundary (internal to the computational domain) allowed to be free to deform owing to the imposed strain. The orientation of the material tensors of the model (for example, elastic stiffness, electrostrictive and flexoelectric tensors) is rotated ±θ/2 about the global z for each layer in the stack.

Sources indicate that the initial condition on each polarization component is assumed to be random and small (<10−4 C m−2). The time-dependent evolution equation evolved until the change in total energy between adjacent time steps is less than 10−6 aJ. The systems of equations are solved within the finite element method, using a discretization of approximately 0.1 nm per element. The calculations are performed in Ferret60, an open-source module for studying ferroelectric nanostructures, in the Multiphysics Object Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE)61. ParaView 6.0.0 (ref. 62) is used to visualize some of the results. We refer the reader to the expanded and self-contained details of the continuum modelling approach in Supplementary Note 6.

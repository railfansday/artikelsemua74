Does Grand Theft Auto (GTA) 6 use AI? Not generative AI, claims its publisher. Nothing we found contradicts that, but the denial covers generative AI in this one game. Take-Two CEO Strauss Zelnick told GamesIndustry.biz in February that generative AI "has zero part in what Rockstar Games is building". In the same breath he noted the company's products "have always been built with machine learning and artificial intelligence". Take-Two's own annual reports used the phrase "artificial intelligence" 0 times from fiscal 2015 to 2023 and 7 times in fiscal 2026, by our count of twelve filings. Claude Code is Anthropic's AI coding agent, and it can also control a computer's screen. A viral post says it finished the game in twelve hours. The post comes from a seven-week-old account with no log, about a game that is not on PC.

Grand Theft Auto VI (the sixth main game, GTA 6 for short) came out on 19 November 2026 on PlayStation 5 (PS5) and Xbox Series X|S. On launch day its English Wikipedia page had 750,227 views, more than any other article that day (only the Main Page and the search page ranked higher). On 10 November it had 64,375.

I run a small consultancy that builds Atlassian apps and AI tooling. So I care less about the game than about how a publisher this size talks about AI to investors, in documents its lawyers sign. I read twelve of Take-Two's annual reports and three of its patents, and then I traced the viral post back to its source. A lot of it applies to any team putting AI into its own work.

Industry observers note that the question mixes up three things. The answers differ. (Related: Jensen Huang question gets reporter ejected from Trump-...)

The main source is Zelnick's interview with GamesIndustry.biz, published on 3 February 2026:.

"Specifically with regards to GTA 6, Generative AI has zero part in what Rockstar Games is building. Their worlds are handcrafted. That's what differentiates them. They're built from the ground up, building by building, street by street, neighborhood by neighborhood. They're not procedurally generated, they shouldn't be.". (Related: Phison details SSD with a built-in NPU and 64GB of memo...)

According to recent reports, the denial starts with a qualifier. So it covers one game. The timing matters too. GamesIndustry.biz notes the interview came after a week in which Take-Two's stock fell to an 11-month low following Google's announcement of Project Genie, which GamesIndustry.biz calls an experimental AI "playable world" tool. On the earnings call the same day, Take-Two president Karl Slatoff said Genie "is not a game engine". To him it looked "more like a procedurally generated interactive video at this point" (transcript).

Rockstar's own answer came second-hand. IGN reported in August 2026 that Kinda Funny's Greg Miller asked Rockstar "point-blank" about generative AI in GTA 6. The team told him there is "no AI" used in the game. That is a journalist's account of a verbal answer at a preview event, and I found no written Rockstar statement saying the same.

In a recent development, the same interview has the line most headlines skipped: "This company's products have always been built with machine learning and artificial intelligence ... right now we have hundreds of pilots and implementations across our company, including within our studios. And we're already seeing instances where generative AI tools are driving costs and time efficiencies." On the earnings call he said generative AI "is already moving into the category of efficiency".

So the company's line is simple: AI goes into the tools that build the game, and the game itself, the streets and the people in them, is made by hand. It never names the tools. It never indicates whether Rockstar is one of the studios running pilots either, and I found nothing public that does.

Recent findings suggest that the voice actors are part of the same picture. The Screen Actors Guild (SAG) and the American Federation of Television and Radio Artists (AFTRA) merged into one US actors' union, SAG-AFTRA. It struck video game makers in 2024-25 over "fair compensation and the right of informed consent for the AI use" of performers. A rule from an older agreement exempted games that started production more than a year before the strike, and GTA VI was one of them. The union's chief contracts officer, Ray Rodriguez, called that legacy rule "insane, but it's there" (PC Gamer). The deal that ended the 320-day strike passed with 95.04% of votes and requires consent and disclosure for digital replicas (TheWrap). No source indicates which agreement GTA VI's performers worked under.

I think upscaling is the most concrete answer to the question. It is the one place a neural network is publicly known to touch the game, the step that turns a lower-resolution frame into a sharper one. On PS5 Pro, Rockstar notes it uses Sony's PSSR (short for PlayStation Spectral Super Resolution) in its second version, PSSR2 (as Rockstar writes it), to upscale 1440p to 4K. Sony's Mark Cerny describes it as "an AI library that analyzes game images pixel by pixel", built with AMD (the chipmaker behind the console's processor). Digital Foundry's tests, as reported by Dexerto, found the PSSR output on the Pro "convincingly passes for native 4K".

According to recent reports, rockstar's technology leads stated in a statement to Wccftech that the Xbox Series S version starts at 720p and reaches 1080p "using AMD FSR Upscaling 4". FSR, short for FidelityFX Super Resolution, is AMD's upscaler. AMD calls it "ML-based FSR Upscaling" (ML, short for machine learning) and says FSR 4 is its earlier name. On PS5 and Series X, Rockstar upscales 1440p to 4K with its own TAA (temporal anti-aliasing) upscaler, which it calls its "in-house TAA solution". It does not mention machine learning for that one.

Interviews are what a CEO wants you to hear. A 10-K is the annual report a US-listed manufacturer files with the SEC (the US Securities and Exchange Commission). Its risk factors section lists what the company's lawyers think could hurt the business. So we downloaded twelve of them from EDGAR, the SEC's filing database, for fiscal 2015 to fiscal 2026. Take-Two's fiscal year ends on 31 March. A 20-line Python script strips the HTML and counts the terms. The part that matters is the patterns:.

The other three patterns ignore case.

It has been revealed that the jump came in 2024, but the nine reports with no mention at all say more to me than the seven mentions in 2026: until fiscal 2024, Take-Two did not name AI to investors anywhere in its annual report. The reports stay between 51,874 and 71,076 words. Length did not drive the count. And the term was not always absent. EDGAR's full-text search finds "artificial intelligence" in two much older Take-Two reports, for fiscal 2005 and 2006. There, "expanded artificial intelligence capabilities of new platforms" is one of the things that would make people buy new consoles. About twenty years ago AI was a selling point of the hardware. From fiscal 2024 it sits mainly in the risk factors, and the count has grown every year since.

I'd put this sentence in front of any team rolling out AI assistants, in games or anywhere else, because I think it names the likeliest way an AI tool ends up hurting a organization:.

"our employees, contractors, vendors, or other partners may use AI tools in ways that are inconsistent with our policies or expectations, including by entering confidential, proprietary, personal, or regulated information into third-party AI services, and some AI providers may have limited operating histories or governance processes".

The same filing states Take-Two is "investing in artificial intelligence ("AI") tools and technologies" to "enhance game play". So the 2026 report holds both positions at once: invest in AI, and warn that your own people may leak data into it. A risk factor says what "may" happen. This sentence reports no incident.

Recent findings suggest that search "gta 6 ai npc patent" and you get fan theories. Each NPC (non-player character, one of the people in the street) is where game AI lives. Here is what is on the record.

In a recent development, edge's cover story, quoted by PC Gamer, says Rockstar set up a Los Angeles team of writers and directors for its crowds. The studio "art-directed every [NPC], creating around 600,000 animations". Each pedestrian gets personality traits that help decide how it reacts: temperament, aggression, where it is, what it has seen and how you treated it. That reads like classic, hand-authored game AI. No source says how the traits work. Nobody says whether any of it is trained.

Industry observers note that the patents are Take-Two's. I read their claims (the numbered parts that say what a patent protects).

US11620781B1, "System and method for virtual character locomotion", was filed on 23 October 2020 and granted on 4 April 2023. Claim 1 describes "motion animation blocks" and "archetype blocks" that are "functionally independent from the core game logic". Claim 7 claims the blocks can be defined in XML meta files. I searched the page text for "machine learning", "neural network" and "trained model": 0 hits.

US11071916B2, "System and method for virtual navigation in a gaming environment", was filed on 24 April 2019 and granted on 27 July 2021. It builds a coarse graph from the detailed road network and looks a route on it, so the game loads fewer detailed nodes. US11684855B2 is a later patent on the same navigation idea, granted on 27 June 2023.

None of them names GTA VI or any other game. I don't think they prove anything about it. Kotaku described the locomotion patent's first-listed inventor, Tobias Kleanthous, as a former Rockstar lead AI and gameplay programmer, and said it had asked Rockstar for comment. The article prints no reply.

Two months after the "zero part" interview, Game Developer reported on 3 April 2026 that Take-Two had laid off its head of AI, Luke Dicken (in the role since January 2025), and at least four others from the AI team. Take-Two declined to comment. So the "hundreds of pilots" interview and the end of its central AI team are two months apart, and I can't tell how the two fit. I checked the 10-K for that fiscal year, filed seven weeks after the layoff report, and none of its AI sentences mentions the team or the cuts.

Ten days before launch, on the 9 November call, an analyst asked about AI-assisted reverse engineering and decompilation of games. Zelnick remarked "it's going to become more of a challenge" and that the manufacturer is "utterly focused on enhancing our technical defenses and enhancing our legal activities" (transcript). Rockstar has paid for leaks before: it said the 2022 hack that leaked unfinished GTA VI footage cost $5 million to recover from, plus thousands of hours of staff time (BBC).

In a recent development, on 19 November, launch day, the X account @⁠0xGoldTooth posted "Claude Code just finished GTA 6 in 12 hours while I was at work". The post says it "learned the controls in 15 minutes" and went "through the game files on its own". GameGPU wrote it up as news. The post had 3,763,124 views when we read it on 21 November.

Then there is the tool itself. Computer use is the Claude feature that looks at the screen and clicks and types. Anthropic's docs call it a research preview: in the Claude Code CLI it runs only on macOS, and the Desktop app adds Windows. Computer use "is the broadest and slowest" way for Claude to act, so it tries more precise tools first. Every screenshot is downscaled before the model sees it (a 3456×2234 screen goes to about 1372×887). Sony's Remote Play does stream a PS5 to a Mac, but Sony lists a DualSense controller among the things you need to play, and its on-screen panel only gives the PlayStation, Create and Options buttons, while computer use clicks and types. Once connected, Sony indicates, "the screen of your PS5 console displays on your Mac". Nothing on that page lets the Mac open the console's files. Tom's Hardware notes that PC players can stream the game through Xbox Cloud Gaming. The post does not say it used either service. The claim is a twelve-hour finish of a game rated 99 on Metacritic plus a trawl through "the game files", from a fresh account. I found no log anywhere. I would not repeat it as news, and I'm surprised GameGPU did.

The documented Claude-and-GTA story is smaller and more useful. In June, Gizmodo covered Ziwen Xu, a 25-year-old founder who tried to build a GTA-style game by prompting an AI agent instead of coding it himself. He upgraded to Claude Max 20x, an Anthropic subscription, to "beat the real GTA 6 to launch". On day two he wrote that the agent built downtown Los Angeles skyscrapers, "which is a problem, because this is supposed to be Florida". He had also burned 33% of his weekly usage in one day. If you want measured numbers on what these agents finish, our agentic coding benchmark results are where we keep them.

Industry observers note that take-Two split AI use in two: tools that make the work faster, and content that ships to the customer. Valve drew the same line on Steam. Valve's disclosure rules ask about "Pre-Generated" content made with AI during development and "Live-Generated" content made while the game runs. The January 2026 patch says "efficiency gains through the use of these tools is not the focus". Activision's Steam page for Call of Duty is an example of a Pre-Generated disclosure: "Our team uses generative AI tools to help develop some in game assets.".

Recent findings suggest that I think it's the right split, and it works as a policy for a software team too. AI that drafts the Jira ticket, summarises the Confluence page or writes the first version of a test is a tooling decision, while AI output that reaches a customer (release notes, a support answer, code in the product) needs the review and disclosure you would want from a contractor. Write the two lists down. Keep them separate and most of the arguments go away.

The people side is not settled, even in games. GDC's 2026 State of the Game Industry asked more than 2,300 professionals. 52% think generative AI is hurting the industry, up from 18% two years earlier. Still, 36% use it at work. 30% of respondents at game studios use AI tools, against 58% at publishers, support teams and marketing firms.

And the 10-K's data-leak sentence applies just as much to a Jira or Confluence site. Before arguing about whether an AI feature is allowed, check what your initiative lets you switch off. I'd start there. We wrote up which Atlassian prepares let you opt out of AI training and what stays on when you disable Rovo.

Sources indicate that still unknown: whether any of GTA VI's characters, animation or physics use a trained model, which AI tools Rockstar's own developers use, and whether the patents shipped. Rockstar may answer some of it in a technical talk one day. Has your team written down which of its AI uses are tooling and which ship to customers?

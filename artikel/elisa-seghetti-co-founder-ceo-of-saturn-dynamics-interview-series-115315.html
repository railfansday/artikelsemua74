Industry observers note that I founded AiGot in 2021 to help businesses use AI to automate complex processes. As we scaled the tech giant and worked with Fortune 500 businesses, I saw first-hand the challenges of making AI reliable in real-world environments: lack of data, misaligned expectations, hype. But I also began to see a newly introduced opportunity emerging. AI would not be confined to screens forever; increasingly, it would be interacting with the physical world. Having spent years building AI that enterprises could trust, I sought to apply that experience to a new challenge: developing better ways to train physical AI to operate safely and reliably side by side with humans. That led me to co-found Saturn Dynamics and pursue a physics-based world model.

According to recent reports, today, much of robotics training relies on video-based learning and egocentric data collection, with people wearing cameras to capture demonstrations of everyday tasks. While this provides valuable data, it is expensive and time-consuming to collect at scale, and watching an action doesn’t necessarily teach a robot how to replicate it or understand its physical consequences.

We saw an opportunity to move beyond learning through observation alone, giving robots an interactive environment to learn through trial and error without relying entirely on real-world data collection.

For readers unfamiliar with world models, how would you explain how a robot learns a task using Arc?

A world model is essentially a model that learns how the world behaves and how it changes in response to different actions.

Imagine you are training a robot to move a tyre along a factory production line. You could show it thousands of videos of the task, but that doesn’t necessarily teach it how much force to apply, how the tyre will respond if it slips, or how its approach should change if the tyre is heavier than expected.

We have created Arc, a world model that allows robotics teams to place physical AI in an interactive simulation where it can try different approaches, observe the predicted outcomes and improve its next attempt. It can rapidly explore multiple scenarios in parallel, testing different actions without having to carry out every experiment on physical hardware. This allows teams to test potentially risky situations without risking damage to equipment or putting people working on the factory floor in harm’s way.

How does embedding mathematical symmetries and physical principles into Arc distinguish it from conventional simulators and video-based world models?

According to recent reports, the physical world is governed by mathematical relationships that remain consistent across different environments. At Saturn Dynamics, our team combines expertise in theoretical physics, reinforcement learning and machine learning to build these principles directly into Arc’s architecture and training approach.

It has been revealed that conventional physics simulators typically rely on engineers defining the environment and specifying the physical properties and parameters that govern how objects behave. While this can produce highly accurate simulations, configuring and adapting them to different scenarios can require a lot of work. Video-based world models, meanwhile, are more time and cost effective but visual data does not always reveal the underlying physical principles governing an object’s behaviour.

A key part of Arc’s approach is mathematical symmetry. In physics, symmetries describe properties that remain unchanged when a system is transformed, such as moving an object to a different position or rotating it. By incorporating these relationships into Arc, the model can recognise how the underlying rules of physics apply across different situations, rather than having to learn them repeatedly from data and as a result, the computational cost is far lower.

How does Arc learn physical properties such as weight, friction, and flexibility that cannot always be determined from images alone?

Our world model is trained on multiple modalities of data, not only images or video. We also feed it physical information, such as depth and force, which helps Arc understand how objects respond to physical interactions. By learning from both visual information and these physical measurements, the model can develop an understanding of physical behaviours that extends beyond the specific examples in its training data. For example, when running experiments where most of Arc’s training data involved rigid objects, we actually observed that the model was able to learn how to simulate how softer, more flexible objects bend and change shape when handled.

How were the benchmarks behind Arc’s reported speed and trajectory accuracy advantages over NVIDIA’s Cosmos models designed?

We designed the benchmarks to evaluate two key aspects of a world model’s performance: how accurately it predicts the physical world, and how efficiently it can generate simulation data.

In the benchmark, we tracked and measured the movements generated by the world model. We checked the accuracy of this process by calibrating against real-world robotics data and simulation data. We then tested the model using two types of movements: real-world robot movements, to assess how accurately it could reproduce familiar actions, and more complex, artificially created movements to test how well it could handle a wider range of possible movements, including those it may not have encountered before.

We evaluated Arc against NVIDIA’s Cosmos world models, Cosmos Nano and Cosmos Edge. In this test, Arc achieved 73% greater trajectory accuracy than Cosmos Nano.

Sources indicate that we also evaluated how quickly each model could generate simulation data. We ran multiple benchmarks on different hardware from low-power deployment devices like NVIDIA’s Jetson Thor to high-end high-power GPUs such as NVIDIA’s B200. To ensure a fair comparison, we ran hundreds of generations under the same conditions. On an NVIDIA Jetson Thor, Arc generated one second of simulation data in 0.6 seconds, compared with 23 seconds for Cosmos Nano and nine seconds for Cosmos Edge, making it faster than real time. These benchmarks show that Arc can deliver greater trajectory accuracy and faster data generation than the models we tested against.

What evidence can you share that skills learned with Arc transfer successfully to physical robots?

In a recent development, for example, in an automotive kitting use case we are evaluating with a leading automotive OEM, a robot repeatedly struggled to grasp an awkwardly shaped component. Using Arc to generate thousands of practice runs, we exposed the policy to different ways of approaching and handling the component, helping it adapt to real-world variability and reliably complete the grasp without weeks of tuning on the factory floor.

The resulting policies can then be evaluated against actual production requirements, including cycle time, task success and intervention frequency, before being tested on physical robots.

How do you prevent robots from learning to exploit inaccuracies in Arc’s simulations?

One of the key challenges in robotics is the sim-to-real gap: the difference between how a robot behaves in a simulated environment and how it performs in the physical world. A robot may learn to complete a task in simulation by exploiting inaccuracies in the model, only to find that the same approach fails when deployed on real hardware.

According to recent reports, we address this by combining real-world and synthetic training data, rather than relying on simulation alone. Real-world data helps ground the robot’s learning in actual physical behaviour, while synthetic data gives it more opportunities to practise. As we collect more data from factories to improve Arc, its simulations become increasingly accurate, allowing us to generate more realistic training data and reduce reliance on real-world data collection.

Can you walk us through a customer or pilot example that illustrates how Arc fits into an existing robotics training pipeline?

Saturn Dynamics uses its world model to train robotics policies, either by generating synthetic training data or using the model as a backbone for the policy itself, known as a world action model.

This can help robots tackle difficult real-world tasks where they might otherwise struggle with unexpected variations. One example is industrial kitting, where a robot must pick different components from bins and assemble kits within strict production cycle times, accounting for variations in component position, shape and weight while maintaining a consistent success rate.

In a recent development, in a conventional workflow, robotics teams collect hundreds of hours of human demonstrations, train a control policy and repeatedly test it on physical hardware. This process is slow and expensive. Arc accelerates this pipeline by generating thousands of simulated execution scenarios from task-specific observations and robot actions, introducing variations in factors such as approach angles, surface grips and component orientations.

Our objective is to make existing robotics development pipelines faster and more scalable, without requiring customers to replace their hardware, collect data through human demonstrations or rebuild their entire software stack.

Where do you see Saturn Dynamics’ strongest competitive advantage in an ecosystem that includes NVIDIA and established simulation platforms?

In a recent development, our focus is on making high-quality simulation scalable and economically viable for robotics teams. Training robots requires vast amounts of data, and generating that data through simulation can become a significant bottleneck in terms of compute, time and cost.

Our benchmarks show that Arc can generate data faster than the NVIDIA Cosmos models we tested against, while using a fraction of the compute and cost. This means robotics teams can explore more scenarios, run more experiments and generate more training data without the same computational burden. We see this as a key step towards making large-scale robotics training more accessible.

Sources indicate that there is also a wider opportunity for Europe. While the US and China took the lead in the first wave of commercial AI, Europe’s industrial heritage gives it a distinct advantage in physical AI. As regions look to reduce their reliance on foreign technology, we believe making robotics training more scalable and cost-efficient can help European companies develop their own capabilities and compete in this next wave of AI innovation.

What milestone would demonstrate that Arc is helping robotics move beyond compelling demonstrations into reliable everyday use?

Our North Star has always been having robot deployments that are reliable, safe and scalable. Going past benchmarks and demos, the core milestone is when a robot can carry out a useful task across full production shifts, handle the variations that naturally occur on a factory floor and require very few human interventions.

In a recent development, A successful demonstration tells you very little about whether a system is ready for deployment. We need to measure sustained task success, cycle time, uptime, interventions per hour and, ultimately, whether the economics work for the customer.

From a commercial perspective, the milestone is when successful pilots consistently turn into larger fleet deployments, with a clear payback period for customers.

Just as importantly, we want to see the same training and evaluation process work across multiple tasks, robot platforms and production sites without starting from scratch every time.

That is when robotics becomes scalable: when deploying the next robot requires progressively less engineering effort than deploying the first.

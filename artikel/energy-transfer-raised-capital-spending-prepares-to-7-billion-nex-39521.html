Industry observers note that energy Transfer (ET -2.62%) looks to spend roughly $5.5 billion on capital projects in 2026. Next year, the strategy is to raise that figure to $7 billion, a more than 25% increase. It isn't unreasonable to worry that this North American midstream giant's spending could put its distribution at risk. Recent findings suggest that to fully understand what's going on with Energy Transfer's distribution, you need to examine how it generates revenue. The master limited partnership (MLP) owns energy infrastructure such as pipelines, storage, and transportation assets. It charges customers fees for using its assets, so energy demand is the key driver. Given the importance of energy to the global economy, demand tends to be strong regardless of commodity prices and remains resilient even during economic soft patches.

Recent findings suggest that notably, in the third quarter, Energy Transfer generated distributable cash flow of $2.522 billion. It only paid out $1.181 billion in distributions. Thus, it covered its distribution by a very comfortable 2.1x. Spending a little more on capital investments in 2027 probably isn't a significant deal given that coverage.

Recent findings suggest that but there's more to the story, because Energy Transfer just issued $2.7 billion worth of latest bonds. Like most companies, it isn't funding its capital investment plans out of its cash flows. And it takes a long time to build new energy infrastructure assets, so it has to cover the funding costs until the new investments start to generate cash flows. Given the strong distribution coverage, that probably won't be a problem.

Moreover, this dynamic is totally normal in the midstream niche of the broader energy sector. Once the fresh assets Energy Transfer is building come online, they will start adding to its cash flows. In fact, given the capital investment plans, it is far more likely that the distribution will continue to increase than that it will be cut.

In a recent development, there's one more wrinkle here. Energy Transfer cut its distribution in 2020 during the energy downturn that accompanied the coronavirus pandemic. But that cut allowed the MLP to reduce its leverage, which is now dramatically lower than it was in 2020. And the well-supported distribution is not only growing again but also above its level prior to the cut. So, if you can overlook that bit of history, the distribution story looks very different today and a lot more positive.

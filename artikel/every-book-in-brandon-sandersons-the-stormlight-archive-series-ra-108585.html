Industry observers note that the Stormlight Archive is an ongoing series by Brandon Sanderson, which exists in his shared literary universe known as the Cosmere. The premise of the Cosmere is that each series or stand-alone story takes place on its own planet within the same galaxy. Each planet has a complex magic system, though some stories take place on worlds that are completely non-magical. Over the years, The Stormlight Archive has proven to be one of the flagship entries in the universe, as well as the absolute longest in terms of word count.

Currently, the saga consists of five mainline novels, two novellas, and one stand-alone double feature that consists of two short stories, though the plan is to have 10 mainline novels. Each book is over 1,000 pages long, so it can be quite time-consuming to read, but it's totally worth it. Set on the barren, windswept world of Roshar, each book features flashback sequences from particular characters, which build on their history and establish why and how they first got to use their magic. According to recent reports, rhythm of War is the fourth mainline novel of the series, and it's also arguably the worst. It gets off to a strong start, with a huge, climactic evacuation in an emotionally significant location, but it all kind of winds down from there. The flashbacks are a bit awkward and disjointed, split between Listener sisters Eshonai and Venli rather than just one character. Almost all of the main story is set in the city of Urithiru, with little opportunity for exploring the vastness of Roshar. The interludes don't allow for this either, as they generally focus on already-existing characters rather than following the pattern of featuring brief viewpoints of one-offs in distant lands. By far the worst part of the story is how it just feels like one prolonged science lesson. So much of it is about how light works, how it is used in fabrials, how it can be manipulated, and the various properties of gemstones. It goes on and on and is incredibly overwhelming. Having a complex magic system isn't a problem, but the book tends to overload it a bit too much. Rhythm of War is not a bad book — in fact, it's a very good book — but it doesn't really hold up compared to the others.

Sources indicate that dawnshard is the second novella, which takes place between books three and four. Released alongside Rhythm of War, it is a thrilling tale across the sea to the lost island of Aimia. The main character is Rysn Ftori, a Thaylen merchant who, until then, was strictly an interlude character. Now in the leading role, Rysn desperately seeks Aimia in order to heal her pet larkin, Chiri-Chiri, who originated from the island.

In a recent development, however, Aimia is obstructed by a permanent storm, so this will be no easy feat. Aided by comic relief characters Lopen and his cousin, Huio, this is a simple, linear adventure to the farthest reaches of Roshar, where the mysteries of Aimia are finally unravelled after three full novels of buildup. It's not a perfect book, especially since it's only a side story, but it's a fantastic adventure with a thrilling climax that only thickens the overarching narrative.

It has been revealed that edgedancer is the first novella in the series, set between books two and three. The book opens with a prologue sequence that is a direct copy-and-paste of Lift's interlude from book two. This novella then continues the story, providing an exclusive look into the events that occurred afterwards. Lift takes the lead as she travels to the city of Yeddaw in Tashikk, where she hopes to try all 10 types of ceremonial pancakes made in the city. That might sound silly, but you must understand that Lift is kind of a chaos gremlin, so it's best to just go along with it.

Sources indicate that accompanied by Wyndle, her exhausted spren, Lift finds herself pursued by a vengeful Herald, the very same one she encountered in her interlude. The young rapscallion raises Hell in Yeddaw, ruining grain carts, befuddling guards, befriending orphans, and, as always, stealing food. It's a great, straightforward adventure into a fascinating location, with a hilarious buddy dynamic between Lift and her spren.

Sources indicate that four worlds and four completely different ideas of what a good life looks like. This is where you belong, what it would ask of you, and what you would get back in return.

According to recent reports, you belong somewhere old enough that the mountains have names and somebody can still tell you who gave them. Middle-earth is for people who think the right thing is worth doing even when it will not be finished in their lifetime.

Sources indicate that you want the sea, a crew, and absolutely nobody telling you where you are allowed to go. The Grand Line is the world for people whose main ambition in life is finding out what is over there.

According to recent reports, you would rather be feared and useful than liked and useless. The Continent is a world where the monsters are the straightforward part of the job and the people are where it all gets complicated.

You want the myths to be real and loose in the actual world, and you want to find out at about thirteen that you were never anywhere near as ordinary as everybody kept telling you.

It has been revealed that wind and Truth is the fifth book in the series, and the concluding novel of the first arc. Since it marks the halfway point of the series overall, it's best not to expect a happy ending with this one. This book wasn't received quite so well by fans, mainly because it took a lot of departures from the Stormlight formula. Namely, it doesn't feature five distinct parts — instead, it features ten shorter parts, which correspond to one day. This means part one is day one, part two is day two, and so forth.

It has been revealed that wind and Truth is also the longest novel in the series, coming in at a whopping 1,300+ pages. The ending is tragic but fitting, and presents a beacon of hope while also spreading a blanket of darkness over the world. The flashbacks focus on the Assassin in White, Szeth-son-son-Vallano, and his time in Shinovar, which is an utterly fascinating backstory. Though many fans felt this book had a lot of shortcomings, it is absolutely action-packed, full of great character moments, and serves as a satisfying ending and upcoming beginning all at once.

According to recent reports, king Lopen the First of Alethkar / Elsecaller is a specially released double feature consisting of two short stories occurring concurrently, around the end of the second novel. They don't really add anything crucial to the overall narrative, but they do answer some burning questions that fans had. King Lopen the First of Alethkar occurs when Lopen is temporarily made king by the real king, Elhokar, as a means of creating a decoy so Elhokar can stay in hiding. Lopen is a comic relief character who, armed with a sock puppet, goes on numerous ridiculous misadventures. It's honestly the best story of the two, because it's genuinely hilarious.

Sources indicate that elsecaller follows what happens to Jasnah Kholin, who is missing in action for most of the book. This story isn't particularly funny; it's just Jasnah's adventure across the parallel dimension of Shadesmar as she seeks to find a way back to the Physical Realm. However, she does meet some interesting people who come from planets not yet revealed, which adds to the mystery of the Cosmere. This book might not add much to the story, but it is incredibly fun and quick to read.

The Way of Kings is the very first book in the series, which is nothing short of mind-blowing. It's where readers are first introduced to the world of Roshar, along with its strangeness, size, and variety. The flashbacks centre on Kaladin, a soldier-turned-slave, who is used as arrow fodder in the war between the Listeners and the Alethi. Meanwhile, Dalinar Kholin, uncle of Alethkar's king, begins experiencing strange visions and becomes convinced he needs to unite the 10 Alethi highprinces in their efforts to defeat their enemies, rather than constantly maneuvering against each other in court.

In a recent development, in the distant city of Kharbranth, a young student named Shallan Davar hopes to study with Jasnah Kholin, Elhokar's sister and Dalinar's niece, and one of the brightest scholars in Roshar. This might be the shortest mainline novel in the series (though it is still roughly 1,000 pages long), but it is an absolute masterpiece, not only in its worldbuilding, but in its inspirational moments and action-packed climax. The Way of Kings is one of Sanderson's finest works and is still recommended as an entry point to the Cosmere by many fans.

In a recent development, words of Radiance is the second mainline novel in the series, picking up immediately where The Way of Kings leaves off. Instead of featuring Kaladin's flashbacks, the book sets its sights on Shallan Davar's past and her childhood growing up in rural Jah Keved. Words of Radiance is commonly cited as the absolute best of the series, with a lot of fans deeming it their favorite, and it's really not hard to see why.

The climax is absolutely unbelievable, genuinely filling the reader with anxiety, dread, sorrow, joy, and relief all at once. There are so many quotable moments in this book, from the fight scene in the arena to Szeth's first encounter with a Herald. A lot changes, yet a lot stays the same, all while the stakes are raised higher than ever before. This book is a solid 10 out of 10. It doesn't waste a single word, and is consistently exciting, even when little is happening.

Oathbringer takes the crown as being the absolute best of the entire saga (so far, anyway). The flashbacks are about Dalinar Kholin and his life as a terrifying combatant. Since much of Dalinar's history is marked by constant civil war and political upheavals, most of his flashbacks are chock-full of violence, with a few major and minor battles scattered in between. Outside of the flashbacks, the story is brilliant, too.

There are numerous large-scale battles that take place throughout the narrative, including the Siege of Kholinar and the climactic Battle of Thaylen Field. There are even more quotable moments in this book, and some utterly devastating death scenes, too. There's so much going on in this novel, yet it's never hard to keep track of things. Oathbringer is honestly one of the greatest fantasy novels ever written, and that's really saying something.

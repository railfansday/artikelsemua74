Sources indicate that if you've played Magic: The Gathering for a while, you've probably heard people mention "Power Nine" cards, especially the most infamous card in trading card game history, Black Lotus. The "Power Nine" are nine cards that were printed in the original MtG sets, starting in 1993, and are considered to be the most powerful cards in the game.

These cards are so strong that they were banned from most competitive MtG settings early on, and have become some of the most valuable cards ever created, particularly if you can get Alpha Edition versions. These days, some competitive formats will allow for these cards in rare situations, particularly the "Timetwister" card, or will let players use them in restricted ways in formats such as Vintage. Of course, in order to be able to play them in a Vintage format or casual game, you need to be able to own them first. The rarity and notoriety of these cards mean that even later reprints of them don't come easily or cheaply. (Related: GTA 6 glitch lets players access first-person mode...)

Out of the nine cards in this list, five of them are the "mox" cards for each of the five colors in the game. A "mox" is a piece of jewelry that generally contains a gemstone that produces mana for its player.

Before restrictions were placed that would either ban the cards entirely or only allow for one per deck, players could play multiples of these cards in a single turn, which would give them an extremely powerful and unbalanced mana acceleration.

"Mox Emerald" is the green mana variety of these cards. Tapping the artifact could be played as an interrupt and adds one green mana to the player's mana pool. Alpha Editions of the card can run around $7,500 at the time of writing, according to TCGPlayer, with Unlimited Editions being priced closer to $3,000.

According to recent reports, "Mox Pearl" plays the same as its Emerald version, as do all the "mox" cards in this list. In this case, tapping the card will add one white mana to the player's mana pool.

Recent findings suggest that most competitive play formats have these cards Banned entirely, although Vintage format games will allow players to use them at a Restricted legality of only one per deck. This prevents players from having the overwhelming mana acceleration, at least a bit.

Due to its age, it's very hard to find an undamaged version of the original Alpha Edition card, so near mint listings are around $12,000 per TCGPlayer. Damaged versions can be much cheaper, as are later reprints such as the 30th Anniversary Edition, which is priced closer to $550.

As one can probably guess, "Mox Ruby" is the red mana variety of these "mox" cards and gives red decks a significant boost in mana acceleration. Like its companions, it is not Legal or is Banned in most competitive formats and Restricted in Vintage format games. Although, of course, with prior discussion and agreement, players can use them in casual games.

In a recent development, the original version of this Mono Artifact was part of the first 1993 MtG sets, and is difficult to come by in good condition these days. Finding a near mint version can cost upwards of $16,500 per TCGPlayer, with Collector's Editions of it running around $750.

According to recent reports, with artwork looking fairly similar to the "Mox Ruby" card, with a gemstone set in the middle of a gold necklace, the "Mox Jet" card once again follows the same ruleset. Tapping this card will add one black mana to the player's mana pool, and all of the "mox" cards come with the benefit of having a zero cost, making them even more impressive and dangerous.

Recent findings suggest that values of the "Mox Jet" card tend to bounce around quite a bit, with recent sales and listings according to TCGPlayer showing near mint varieties of the Alpha Edition coming in at around $16,500. Even the more recent Collector's Edition version of the card is inching towards four digits, with its average price currently being close to $900.

Next up we have one of the Power Nine cards which is not of the "mox" variety. This "Ancestral Recall" card adds quick card draw at low cost, giving players the ability to immediately draw three cards. Alternatively, they could force their opponent to draw three cards, but in a game where having the right cards in your hand can change your luck, most players would rather get the three cards themselves.

"Ancestral Recall" was also part of the original MtG set from August 1993, and is Restricted in Vintage formats while being Banned in all other official competitive play.

In a recent development, at the time of writing, TCGPlayer does not show any listings for the Alpha Edition card that are in better condition than heavily played, with even those cards coming in at prices around $10,000. Unlimited Edition versions are much more common and typically priced around $3,000 to $5,000.

The most valuable and rare "mox" card in the Power Nine collection is the Alpha Edition of "Mox Sapphire," which adds one blue mana to the player's mana pool.

Currently, TCGPlayer does not show any listings for the card that are better than moderately played condition, with even those cards being priced between $12,000 and $17,000. Unlimited Editions of the card average around $3,500 in value, although Collector's Editions are more reasonable at an average market price of $650.

It has been revealed that despite having some of the coolest art of the Power Nine cards, "Timetwister" was another 1993 card that was put on Restricted lists in 1994 and later Banned in most formats. However, this is the only Power Nine card that is presently Legal to play in Commander formats.

The card gave players the ability to shuffle their hand and draw another seven cards for a fresh hand at a low cost of only three mana. It is assumed that the decision to make it legal in Commander comes from the fact that not only does the card benefit the player, but it applies to all players.

Near mint versions of the card are priced at around $16,500 according to TCGPlayer.

It has been revealed that reading the ability of "Time Walk" makes it pretty clear why this card is considered overpowered. "Time Walk" costs only two mana and allows its player to take another turn with no limitations or drawbacks. Giving a second turn in a row at such a cheap mana cost can create wildly unbalanced games where opponents may not get to play much at all, particularly if its player has multiple copies.

Like the majority of Power Nine cards, it is Restricted in Vintage formats, and Banned everywhere else in competitive play. Although if you can convince someone to allow you to play with multiple copies in a casual game, it can be pretty fun. For you, at least.

Per TCGPlayer, Alpha Editions of the card in near mint condition can run around $22,500.

Easily the most infamous card in MtG, if not just in general amongst all trading card games, we conclude with "Black Lotus." This card is so well known that many people who have never even played the game are aware of its existence and value.

According to recent reports, the card costs nothing to play and allows players to sacrifice it to add three mana of any color to their pool immediately. If someone gets this card in their hand on turn one, it's entirely possible to win the game on the very first turn by casting a high-cost spell right out of the gate. Because it can add any color, it fits into nearly any deck and strategy with ease.

According to TCGPlayer, the card is valued at around $120,000 for near mint versions of the Alpha Edition. However, in the past, the card has sold at auction for upwards of $500,000.

So, even though it is possible to play as a Restricted card in Vintage formats, it's probably better to protect it from wear and tear in playing Magic: The Gathering.

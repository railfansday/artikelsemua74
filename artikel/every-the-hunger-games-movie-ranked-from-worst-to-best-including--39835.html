The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping's release shakes up the ranking of all The Hunger Games movies from worst to best, as they aren't all of equal quality. Lionsgate brought Suzanne Collins' best-selling YA dystopian sci-fi series to the big screen in 2012, just four years after the first book. Since then, audiences have been treated to six movies following a few different protagonists.

Sources indicate that the action-packed but socially relevant films about fighting back against oppressive regimes began with a focus on Katniss Everdeen (Jennifer Lawrence), as she enters the 74th Hunger Games, becomes the symbol of a rebellion against the Capitol and President Snow (Donald Sutherland), and navigates her feelings for Peeta Mellark (Josh Hutcherson) and Gale Hawthorne (Liam Hemsworth). These three, along with Haymitch Abernathy (Woody Harrelson), were at the heart of the first four blockbusters.

Industry observers note that given the popularity of the Hunger Games movies, Collins wrote prequel books that Lionsgate was quick to turn into movies themselves. The first made Snow the main character, as The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes indicated him mentoring Lucy Gray Baird (Rachel Zegler), District 12's female tribute in the 10th Hunger Games. Sunrise on the Reaping brought a younger Haymitch (Joseph Zada) into the spotlight to show his participation in the 50th Hunger Games. All six Hunger Games movies have been positively received by critics and general audiences, which has only made it tougher for many to rank them. Sources indicate that on the heels of the first two films' success, Lionsgate elected to split Collins' final book in the Katniss trilogy into two parts. This was always viewed as a risky decision, especially since the first half is very different from what came before. Mockingjay Part 1 is stripped of any real interactions between Katniss and Peeta, and shifts the setting from the arena to the war-torn Districts.

It has been revealed that the result is a movie that hinges much more on Katniss and Gale's relationship, which was fairly underdeveloped in the previous installments. Lawrence and Hemsworth's chemistry is fine, but it isn't strong enough to support the film. Katniss (and the movie) misses Peeta's optimism and charm desperately.

Katniss' arc of accepting her role as the Mockingjay is effective, and there are a few solid set pieces as she explores other Districts. However, Mockingjay Part 1 never shakes the feeling that it is stretching out the franchise's conclusion for an easy cash grab. The result is the stakes feeling very low, as no major characters die, with all casualties coming in the form of nameless citizens.

Mockingjay Part 2 was a low point for the franchise in terms of general interest and reception, but the movie has held up better than its predecessor. It leans back into the blockbuster spectacle that the franchise conditioned audiences to expect. Katniss and her team navigating a booby-trapped Capitol allows the film to deliver some exciting and fresh life-or-death situations, with mostly effective deaths.

It has been revealed that coin's death is the most satisfying of them all, as Katniss rids the world of another potential dictator ready to spill children's blood to incite fear. That decision, paired with her getting a peaceful life with Peeta and their children, makes for a great conclusion to her story.

According to recent reports, ultimately, Mockingjay Part 2 is a solid, thrilling conclusion to Katniss' saga. There is a fresh layer of tension to her and Peeta's dynamic that slowly fades throughout the movie, showing just how strong their relationship really is. It might fly through some major moments and become too dark at times, but its action and strong performances across the board help.

Recent findings suggest that after Katniss' story ended, there was not a huge push to see Snow's origin story. The evil, poison-loving ruler of Panem was hardly a character people would cite as someone they wanted to spend more time with. Yet, The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes proved that there was much more to explore in his teenage years to explore how he rose to power.

Remarkably, The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes does its job of making audiences care about Snow and understand (but not necessarily relate to) why he became a charming, ruthless ruler. Seeing the tough conditions of Snow's earlier years is effective, while he is humanized by introducing Lucy Gray Baird, the District 12 tribute who wins the 10th Hunger Games with Snow as her mentor.

Their dynamic is why the movie works, with Tom Blyth and Rachel Zegler selling the unexpected romance. They have better chemistry than Lawrence and Hemsworth in Mockingjay Part 1, even if the movie is also lacking in action overall. It goes above Mockingjay Part 2 thanks to having better, more developed characters and relationships.

Sunrise on the Reaping is the prequel fans wanted for years. Haymitch's games have been a point of interest ever since Catching Fire's book, and now we have seen just how captivating and devastating his origin really was. Anchored by a strong cast, epic action, and a lot of franchise connections, Sunrise on the Reaping becomes an invaluable entry.

In a recent development, joseph Zada does a fairly great job of becoming a believably younger version of Woody Harrelson's character, giving him the same wit, protective spirit, and edges. Seeing him compete in the 50th Hunger Games and become part of an early rebellion plot is incredibly exciting, with the film really making you care about what happens to him and other tributes, even though its prequel status means some of this is already well-known.

In a recent development, sunrise on the Reaping is bolstered by stellar supporting cast members in Mckenna Grace, Whitney Peak, Jesse Plemons, Glenn Close, and more. Book purists will ultimately be disappointed by a few notable scenes that were cut and how those change the story, while some of the returning characters come across as more forced than natural. All in all, it strikes a better balance of its characters, action, emotionality, and themes than the films ranked lower.

The Hunger Games debuted the franchise in a terrific fashion. The movie wastes little time endearing Katniss to audiences, ensuring that we care about her from the moment she volunteers in Prim's place until the 74th Hunger Games are over. Lawrence immediately embodies the character from Collins' books, giving her all the resilience and compassion expected from the strong-willed District 12 teenager.

Sources indicate that since The Hunger Games was not yet a proven franchise, there is a slightly more grounded approach applied by director Gary Ross. His action is more brutal and frenetic, which adds to the feeling of experiencing what it's like in the arena for the first time. The less polished look feels right for District 12 and seeing the whole experience through Katniss' eyes.

Industry observers note that the movie, and its Games, hinge on accepting there is a genuine connection between Katniss and Peeta, and Lawrence and Hutcherson do their part to make it work. Harrelson and Elizabeth Banks' Effie nearly steal the whole show, while Amandla Stenberg's Rue adds a wonderful (and heartbreaking) dynamic with Katniss. The Hunger Games set a very high bar for everything that came after.

For as great as the original is, Catching Fire took the Hunger Games franchise to fresh heights that still have yet to be eclipsed. Director Francis Lawrence steps aboard to level up the action and scale, which is very fitting given the stakes of the 3rd Quarter Quell. Everything from the characters, performances, action, sets, and visuals stands above every other entry in the series.

According to recent reports, the personal stakes are clear for Katniss even before the Games begin, thanks to showing what her life has become in 12 after the win. Once the tributes enter the franchise's best arena, the unique dangers and setup elevate things further. Katniss and Peeta's relationship is once again key to it all, but Finnick, Johanna, Beetee, and Plutarch are excellent latest additions to the world. They each feel fully realized no matter how big or small their presence really is.

Ultimately, Catching Fire is a perfect fusion of blockbuster action and YA romance. The faked love between Katniss and Peeta becomes real and believable thanks to sequences like Peeta's near-death experience or their talk on the beach. The action is impeccable, and the stakes that come with it are about as high as the franchise can go. Add in the surprise conclusion of the 75th Hunger Games for any non-book readers (or how well executed the plot is for those in the know), and there's no doubt this is the best The Hunger Games movie from start to finish.

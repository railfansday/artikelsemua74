FAA is set to reveal the cause of the router outage that halted U.S. flights

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, federal Aviation Administration chief Bryan Bedford stated Thursday that officials proposal to disclose by the end of the week what caused the Nov. 13 nationwide ground stop, which stemmed from a router outage, according to Reuters.

According to recent reports, bedford would not talk about what investigators had found, according to Reuters. He mentioned Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy would be the one to present the outcome to the public. The disruption hit over 18,000 flights and 1 million travelers.

The FAA said it issued the ground stop at about 3:30 p.m. ET on Nov. 13 after a technical problem, and air traffic resumed at 8:15 p.m. that day. Instability hit 17 of the FAA's 22 Air Route Traffic Control Centers. The agency said its legacy equipment became unstable when data flooded the network at a rate of 1,000 connection attempts every 4 to 5 seconds.

It has been revealed that the FAA remarked its initial investigation points to a radar site in Amarillo, Texas, and a radio communications site in El Dorado, Arkansas. L3Harris, the FAA's network service provider, had been working at the El Dorado site, including deploying a new router. The agency said it has no indication of a cyberattack and that it and L3Harris continue to investigate the root cause. Operators canceled approximately 12,462 flights on Nov. 13, though not all cancellations were directly caused by the ground stop, the FAA said.

Duffy has blamed the ground stop on a Juniper router that L3Harris put in at a remote facility in Arkansas, according to Reuters. He stated the earlier rollout of 68 routers from Juniper Networks $JNPR, a unit of Hewlett Packard Enterprise, caused no trouble. The FAA said it disabled the router function involved at the originating routers and more than 60 others, and has paused installing them. According to recent reports, testing is ongoing at L3Harris’ Florida lab, where personnel from the FAA, Cisco $CSCO and the FBI are present, according to Reuters. A Hewlett Packard Enterprise spokesperson stated the hardware performed the way it had been set up, according to CNN.

It has been revealed that earlier this week, Duffy stated officials had not determined whether contractor error or a faulty router caused the flood of data. Bedford said the router replacement was routine maintenance of the existing telecommunications network and not part of the $12.5 billion air traffic control modernization effort. Bedford mentioned the FAA Telecommunications Infrastructure network, which L3Harris has operated since 2002, needs replacement, according to Reuters. "We have a patchwork, archaic federal telephone network, which is why we've got to get off of it as fast as humanly possible while maintaining safety," Bedford said.

The FCC Space Bureau on November 25, 2026, granted Space Exploration Holdings (SpaceX) conditional authority for Starmind, a proposed low-Earth-orbit constellation of up to 1 million satellites operating as data centers, while deferring parts of the application.

The FCC’s announcement described the action as the first U.S. authorization for a constellation of data centers in space, with orbital compute modules intended to process large amounts of data for use on Earth and support super-intelligence computing. The order specifies the licensed activity: radiofrequency communications for backup and emergency telemetry, tracking and command, subject to technical and operating conditions. In a recent development, spaceX filed the application on January 30, 2026. A public notice issued February 4, 2026, accepted it for filing and sought comment on an initial design using high-bandwidth optical intersatellite links. Industry observers note that starmind’s primary communications path is optical. Its intersatellite links can connect within the upcoming system and with SpaceX’s first- and second-generation Starlink constellations, which would route traffic to authorized ground stations. The Ka-band radio authorization covers 18.8–19.3 GHz space-to-Earth links and 28.6–29.1 GHz Earth-to-space links for backup and emergency telemetry, tracking and command, rather than routine data traffic.

According to recent reports, those radio operations are unprotected and must not cause harmful interference. SpaceX must coordinate with potentially affected federal operators, immediately stop operations responsible for documented harmful interference until the issue is resolved, and obtain required gateway earth-station authorizations before using them for Starmind. Semiannual reports must cover conjunction events, satellites removed from operation or screened from deployment, atmospheric reentries, deployment totals and orbital parameters, and satellites using the Ka-band links.

Industry observers note that the authorized design spans 500 to 1,002 kilometers, below the 2,000-kilometer upper end of SpaceX’s January proposal. It uses orbital shells 50 kilometers wide, with 30-degree and sun-synchronous inclinations, placing explicit orbital boundaries on the grant.

The bureau found SpaceX’s debris-mitigation proposal compliant for satellites below 600 kilometers, but required a modification application containing a final plan before any Starmind satellite is deployed above that altitude. It separately deferred SpaceX’s request to dispose of some of those higher-altitude satellites in Earth-disposal or heliocentric orbits rather than through atmospheric reentry because the tech giant had not supplied enough information to assess the alternative.

Industry observers note that spaceX’s commitments include testing satellites at lower altitudes before moving them higher, controlled deorbiting, collision-avoidance capability until immediately before reentry, ocean reentry targeting, and designs intended to burn up completely during reentry. Those measures address collision and casualty risks, but they do not remove the requirement for a separate higher-altitude debris strategy.

The 15-year license term begins on the date SpaceX certifies that the first Starmind satellite is in orbit and operating in compliance with the authorization.

The order denied petitions from Kuiper Systems, Viasat and the American Astronomical Society. The wider record raised radio interference, orbital debris, optical and infrared astronomy, and the technical and economic feasibility of orbital data centers. The bureau declined to judge the business case for orbital computing or to create a satellite-count cap absent from its rules, while addressing radio operations through the authorization’s conditions.

It has been revealed that on environmental review, the bureau applied the FCC’s October 2026 position that licensed space-based operations are extraterritorial activities rather than major federal actions triggering the National Environmental Policy Act, the federal environmental-review law. It therefore required no further review under that statute. It also imposed no additional optical-astronomy conditions, citing SpaceX’s commitment to coordinate with the National Science Foundation, and treated the astronomical society’s concerns about infrared waste heat as outside the authorization’s radiofrequency scope.

The FCC remarked it is continuing to review additional orbital data-center proposals and intends to decide them under its regular procedures. For Starmind, the order creates a route to deployment without settling the full design: higher-altitude operations still depend on a future debris filing, and the deployment checkpoints make the authorized maximum contingent on satellites actually entering compliant service.

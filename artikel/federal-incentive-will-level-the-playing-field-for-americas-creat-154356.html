For more than a decade, CreativeFuture has fought for one simple idea: Members of our creative communities deserve to make a living from the work they create. We have taken on piracy. We have taken on Massive Tech. We have taken on criminal enterprises that profit from the theft of our movies, television, music, and books. Sources indicate that I have spent my entire career in this business – at Vestron, where we made Dirty Dancing, and then at United Artists, Fine Line Features, and Paramount Classics, where we released films like Child’s Play, Road House, Shine, You Can Count on Me, and Hustle & Flow. I have watched independent filmmakers scrape together budgets one dollar at a time. Today, I hear the same question from filmmakers repeatedly: Can we afford to make this in America? More and more often, the answer is no.

According to recent reports, at least 65 countries now offer national production incentives, sometimes in addition to local or regional ones. The United States does not. Thirty-nine states have built programs of their own, but a state program cannot outbid a government overseas with more competitive incentives in place, and the numbers show it. America’s share of global film production has fallen from 52% in 2022, to just 38% at the start of 2026. The share was 65% just over a decade ago.

Sources indicate that let’s be clear about who pays the price. Film and television support more than two million American jobs and pay over $200 billion in wages a year. Nationally, the industry supports 162,000 businesses, 93% of which employ fewer than 10 people.

Recent findings suggest that those paychecks go to editors and composers, costume designers and carpenters, camera operators and writers’ assistants. Many of them work from project to project, with no guarantee there will be a next one. When a production moves overseas, their jobs move without them.

That is why I am so encouraged by the Motion Picture, Television, and Entertainment Revitalization Act (MPTERA). Introduced last month with strong bipartisan support in both chambers, it would create federal tax relief for film and television made in America based on the U.S. jobs created, including for postproduction and visual effects. Editing suites and effects houses can be anywhere in the world – which means they can just as easily be somewhere other than here.

MPTERA also gives independent productions extra relief, up to 30% of the amount spent on U.S. jobs. As someone who has built much of my career championing independent film, I can tell you that matters. Independent films are where new voices are discovered, and run on margins so thin that an overseas incentive can decide whether they get shot in Albuquerque or Belfast. Or whether they get shot at all.

The upside is enormous. A new study estimates that a federal incentive would support an average of more than 143,000 jobs a year and generate nearly $250 billion in additional economic activity over eight years. And this won’t help only California and New York – our industry is in every state of the union, leaving its economic mark everywhere it goes. There is also a bonus for productions in rural areas or for producers that film in at least 10 states in a year. And keep in mind, a feature film’s location shoot generates an average of $670,000 per day for local communities. For a one-hour television episode, it’s $475,000 per day.

Bottom line: This is not a giveaway. Not a single taxpayer dollar goes to the production – the legislation simply reduces the tax liability of U.S. productions. It is a smart investment in one of the few industries where America still leads the world. Our film and television industry maintains a trade surplus with nearly every nation on Earth. When Americans were asked what their country does better than any other, 58% named movies and television as their top answer. Why would we let that slip away?

Other countries have swiftly realized the economic benefit that production brings. Standing still here at home is not a policy. It is clearly a forfeit. This is a bill that has the support of President Trump, as well as Senators Tim Scott and Adam Schiff and Reps. Nathaniel Moran and Linda Sánchez, and 90 other members of Congress. This is the rare bill that has reached across the aisle and to the White House. That doesn’t happen often.

Industry observers note that congress should pass MPTERA this year. We already have the talent and resources and will see the benefit immediately. America’s creatives are ready to work. Let’s give them the chance to do it here at home.

Ruth Vitale is the CEO of CreativeFuture, a nonprofit coalition of over 500 companies and organizations and nearly 300,000 individuals devoted to promoting the value of creativity in the digital age. She has held top posts at Paramount Classics, Fine Line Features, and Newly introduced Line Cinema.

Fewer than half in UK will pay TV licence fee within a decade, report warns

Analysis for thinktank points to cycle of falling income leading to lower spending on content in ‘spiral of decline’.

Fewer than half of households in Britain will pay the BBC’s licence within a decade, according to analysis that warns that the broadcaster’s funding is locked in a “spiral of decline”.

With senior BBC figures concerned about the accelerating fall in licence fee sales, the independent projections also warn that the proportion of households paying the levy will further decline to less than a third by 2037-38 should the government persist with the corporation’s current funding model. Lisa Nandy, the culture secretary, is preparing to publish proposed changes to the licence fee that will force anyone who watches a streaming service to pay it. Currently, only people who watch live TV or the BBC’s iPlayer have to pay the fee. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

Projections by the respected independent media consultancy Oliver & Ohlbaum Associates point to an “unsustainable” cycle of falling income leading to lower spending on strong content that attracts audiences.

They show the BBC’s licence fee income – paid by 77% of households last year – could be worth less than half its current level in real terms by 2037-38, the next point at which the broadcaster’s funding model is renegotiated.

The BBC’s own internal licence fee income modelling is also said to be bleak, with the corporation already making painful cuts to programmes and staff.

Nandy’s proposed latest funding model, which could form part of the BBC’s next 10-year charter, is expected to be published before the end of the year.

Other more radical proposals, such as a household levy for the BBC, have already been ruled out by the Treasury as politically impossible.

Like all media companies, the BBC is facing a myriad of pressures. The costs of making television have hugely increased, while the arrival of streamers and digital platforms has transformed viewing habits.

Meanwhile, freezes to the licence fee over the past decade, as well as extra costs loaded on to the BBC by previous governments, have given the corporation less to spend.

The newly introduced independent projections, included in a report on the BBC by the Reset Tech thinktank, show why the broadcaster’s leadership and ministers accept that the status quo cannot continue.

The study suggests the BBC’s spending on TV content will more than halve by the next charter review. BBC viewing per person via TV sets would fall from 46 minutes a day in 2024-25 to just 23 minutes a day in 2037-38. Revenue would fall from £3.8bn to £1.6bn in real terms.

Sources indicate that the BBC remains the UK’s most-used media brand, reaching 94% of adults every month. However, the projections suggest YouTube could overtake BBC TV set viewing in 2032-33.

Sources indicate that it is predicted that more than half of households will not pay the licence fee by 2033-34. Non-payment of the licence fee has already risen from about 9% of households in 2015 to about 23% last year, and the trend has been accelerating.

Dylan Sparks, the UK director at Reset Tech, said the report confirmed that the BBC’s current funding model was broken. “The BBC is critical national infrastructure and it deserves a long-term settlement that ensures it not only survives but thrives in the decades to come,” he said.

“We urge the government to defend this vital British asset and to use the BBC charter renewal process to come forward with a fresh funding model that is universal, affordable and sustainable.”.

A BBC spokesperson said: “We have consistently argued that the current funding model is not sustainable and needs reform. We will continue to engage with government to secure a fresh charter that provides sufficient, sustainable and universal funding so the BBC can deliver for audiences for generations to come.”.

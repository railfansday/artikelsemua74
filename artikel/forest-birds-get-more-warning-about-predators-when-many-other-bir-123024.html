Forest birds get more warning about predators when many other bird species live nearby

More bird species meant more alarm calls when a model hawk flew past, and recordings of more species drew bigger crowds of mobbing birds.

According to recent reports, when an owl demonstrates up in a forest during daylight hours, the small birds that see it make lots of noise. They gather around the owl and chirp out their calls. Almost immediately, birds of other species hear the calls and come to join the outcry.

Industry observers note that A new study of forest birds in eastern China found more of these interspecies callings in places with greater bird species diversity. Joining forces allowed them to make more calls per minute, alerting everyone within earshot of the owl’s presence. (Related: Industry News: My Journey to a serverless transformers ...)

The result suggests that birds in forests that have fewer species may get less predator warnings, leaving them more vulnerable.

It has been revealed that two doctoral students, Agnishikhe Kumar and Jiahao Wu, are the study’s first authors. Both are at Xi’an Jiaotong-Liverpool University in Suzhou, China, and the University of Liverpool.

Kumar and Wu worked with Eben Goodale, the study’s senior author, and with colleagues at Zhejiang University and other institutions in China, France, and the United States. All of the fieldwork took place in forest reserves in China.

Over more than two years, the team worked in ten nature reserves spread along about 1,025 miles (1,650 kilometers) of eastern China, from tropical Hainan to temperate Shaanxi.

Birds make two different kinds of calls about predators. Alarm calls go out when a predator is moving and attacking, and they often send the birds in a flock scattering into thick vegetation. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

Recent findings suggest that mobbing calls are for a predator that isn’t moving, like an owl in daylight. These calls draw other birds toward the caller, and the crowd harasses the predator and can drive it away.

To set off alarm calls, the researchers used a slingshot to fire an 8-inch (20-centimeter) plastic hawk into the air beside mixed flocks of several species.

For mobbing, they put out a painted foam model of a collared owlet, a small owl that can attack birds, and played recordings of its calls.

It has been revealed that each time, they recorded how many species called, how many calls came per minute, and how rapidly the first call came. They also measured the range of pitch the calls covered.

In an interview with Earth.com, Goodale credited his two students with the fieldwork. “Yeah, this was a ton of work,” he noted. By his count, the total was 1,277 experiments.

Sources indicate that before each owl test, the team counted the bird species at the site. Where they counted more species, more kinds of birds joined the mobbing and more individual birds took part.

The birds at those sites also called at a higher rate, and their calls covered a wider range of pitch.

Sources indicate that pitch range matters because birds respond most to calls of other species that sound like their own. A broader mix of calls should reach more kinds of listeners.

Recent findings suggest that on 41 islands in Thousand Island Lake, a reservoir in Zhejiang Province, the team saw a similar pattern. The number of bird species on those islands goes up with the size of the island.

It has been revealed that alarm calls were far rarer than mobbing calls. The plastic hawk set them off in 79 of 150 flocks.

According to recent reports, the flocks that called gave a median of four alarm calls a minute, compared with a median of 52 calls a minute in the owl tests. One highly social species, Huet’s fulvetta, made 60.2% of all the alarm calls.

A bird that calls while a hawk is near may put itself at risk. Goodale told Earth.com the team thought the pattern might not hold for alarm calls, reasoning that “after one species calls, everyone else should eavesdrop on that and remain quiet.”.

Instead, in flocks with more species, more kinds of birds gave alarm calls. The calls came at a higher rate and covered a wider range of pitch.

Recent findings suggest that “We think it may be because the initial information may not be reliable,” Goodale said. Other species may need to keep calling, he added, to stress the threat and make sure the warning reaches their own kind.

According to recent reports, in Sri Lanka, the authors wrote, the first bird to call in a flock is usually a babbler. That babbler often calls at any large or fast-moving bird, so the later calls of other species help mark a real attack.

The team found no significant link between a flock’s species count and how rapidly the first alarm call came.

In a recent development, the hawk and owl tests demonstrated how much calling went on. To learn whether birds of other species made use of it, the team ran an experiment at seven sites on the largest island in the lake.

Through two speakers, the researchers played the mobbing calls of one, two, four, or eight local species and recorded which birds responded. A recording of crickets, played to check that birds weren’t simply reacting to noise, drew no response.

Across 406 trials, recordings with more species drew more kinds of birds and more individuals. The birds that came made more calls, across a wider range of pitch. The increase leveled off as the number of species in the recording went up.

Recent findings suggest that birds of species that weren’t in the recording were also more likely to call within the first minute when the recording held more species. When the team counted all responding birds together, the first-minute pattern was too weak to rule out chance.

Industry observers note that huet’s fulvetta leads the mixed flocks in the region and often starts the mobbing. Goodale noted the team expected it to play an outsized role in spreading information.

The experiment highlighted little sign of that. The team compared recordings of four species with and without each of the eight birds, across five measures.

In only four of those 40 comparisons was the response stronger when a particular species was included, and the four involved four different species.

Industry observers note that all eight species drew relatively similar responses, Goodale said. “It seems birds are responding to the small acoustic differences between species and registering a higher response when they hear more species,” he added.

To test that idea, the team had built each recording two ways. In one version, every recording had 104 calls per minute, however many species were in it. In the other, each species called at its usual rate, so more species meant more calls.

Birds responded more to recordings with more species even when the number of calls stayed the same. The researchers took that as evidence that the variety of calls matters.

On most of the five measures, adding species had a stronger effect when the number of calls rose too. So the amount of calling appears to matter as well.

Goodale said the finding also applies in reverse. In areas with fewer animals because of habitat loss, hunting, or other causes, the animals that remain could be less informed.

It has been revealed that or they could come to rely more on collecting information themselves. When the information is about predators, he stated, that could come at great cost.

The authors compared their finding to earlier studies that tied species diversity to pollination, seed dispersal, and pest control, and wrote that it helps justify conservation.

The experiments recorded how birds called and gathered, so they can’t show whether birds in places with more species are caught by predators less often.

Only mobbing calls were tested with recordings, and only on a single island. Earlier experiments elsewhere, which mostly compared one species with two, found weak effects on communication or no difference in response.

The larger scale of this study partly explains its stronger result, the authors wrote.

It has been revealed that the same idea could apply to other information, such as where to find food or habitat, and to other animals. The researchers suggested that experiments with the chemical signals of insects and other invertebrates may be particularly useful.

The full study was published in the journal PLOS Biology.

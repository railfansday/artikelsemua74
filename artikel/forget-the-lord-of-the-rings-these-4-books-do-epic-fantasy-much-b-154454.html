Sources indicate that back in 1937, J. R. R. Tolkien published The Hobbit. This children's high fantasy novel about a home-loving hobbit going on a grand adventure rapidly became a critical and commercial success, and is still one of the bestselling books in history. That same year, Tolkien's publisher urged him to begin writing a sequel, and after several false starts, the story of the One Ring finally came to the author's mind. Over several years, what had begun as a concept for a relatively simple and straightforward sequel began transforming into something far bigger and more complex, bringing together worldbuilding and lore ideas from the rejected early drafts of The Silmarillion. Enter The Lord of the Rings, which was published in three volumes between 1954 and 1955. To this day, it's still generally remembered as the greatest and most foundational work of fantasy literature of the 20th century.

Industry observers note that it's far and away one of the best epic fantasy books of all time, and for many years, it completely redefined and dominated the genre. Going forward and well into the '90s, almost all high fantasy books published were in some way either inspired by or directly trying to imitate Tolkien. However, though it's undoubtedly one of the greatest works of speculative fiction in history, there are four particular books which arguably do epic fantasy even better. Sprawling worlds on a grand scale, large ensemble casts, deep lore that readers can't get enough of, and sky-high stakes are all characteristics of these undeniable masterpieces, which certainly give Lord of the Rings a run for its money. In the end, literary appreciation is entirely subjective, but there's a very strong argument to be made that these gems all understood epic fantasy even better than Tolkien. Canadian authors Steven Erikson and Ian Cameron Esslemont originally came up with the world of Malazan as a backdrop for role-playing games they made using a modified version of Advanced Dungeons & Dragons back in 1982. The world and its lore eventually grew so large that the duo wrote a movie script titled Gardens of the Moon, but after that failed to find interest, Erikson and Esslemont agreed to each write a series set in Malazan. Erikson wrote the Gardens of the Moon novel, which was published in 1999 and thus kicked off the now-legendary Malazan Book of the Fallen, one of the greatest high fantasy book series of all time. Erikson agreed to a contract for nine sequels as soon as he sold Gardens of the Moon, and the result is a gargantuan fantasy epic composed of over 3.3 million words. Indeed, the Malazan series was so large and so pop-culturally significant that once its final volume came out, it felt like one of those fantasy books that marked the end of an era. We are, of course, talking about The Crippled God, one of the greatest fantasy epics of the 2010s. Erikson, writing as an educated and trained archaeologist and anthropologist, took the genre blueprint built by Tolkien decades prior and crafted a conclusive chapter that's as exhilarating as they come. Larger in scope, more structurally complex, and more thematically mature than The Lord of the Rings, The Crippled God masterfully brings together dozens of storylines and hundreds of characters into a single chaotic collision. The resulting novel is as morally complex as it is philosophically profound, and that's why it's a must-read for any fan of the epic fantasy genre.

Sources indicate that the legendary Brandon Sanderson wrote throughout his undergraduate and graduate studies, with 12 novels already written by 2003 despite having no publisher or any offers of publication. It was in 2005 that Elantris, his debut novel, was finally published. Since then, Sanderson has become one of the most prolific speculative fiction writers of contemporary times, delivering several of the highest-rated books on Goodreads since 2000. Many of these books belong to his iconic Cosmere universe, composed of subseries as masterful as The Stormlight Archive. As exceptional as the series' first installment, The Way of Kings, was, it's the sequel that remains Sanderson's highest-rated book on Goodreads. It's Words of Radiance, the peak of 21st-century heroic high fantasy.

It doesn't feel quite as mythological as The Lord of the Rings, instead delivering a far more cinematic and character-driven experience that should appeal to anyone who tends to enjoy modern fantasy literature. For one, it's structurally perfect, a 1,000-page doorstop of a novel that nevertheless doesn't ever feel like it meanders. There's no fluff on the way to the Sanderlanche, a term fans have coined to describe the way Sanderson novels like Words of Radiance tend to culminate in fast-paced third acts where everything comes together in spectacular fashion to deliver a myriad of cathartic payoffs. Add to that a fascinating magic system, some of the most thematically layered and psychologically profound character writing that the genre has ever seen, and worldbuilding that even the most demanding epic fantasy readers should find satisfying, and you get a modern classic unlike any other. If there's any one fantasy novel that deserves to be called the best of the 21st century, it's this one.

Will George R. R. Martin ever finally finish writing A Song of Ice and Fire? Only time will tell. But even if the long-awaited The Winds of Winter never came, one thing would still remain undeniably true: A Storm of Swords is the last fantasy epic masterpiece of the 20th century. Back in the '90s, Martin had been wanting to take a stab at epic fantasy writing since reading the works of Tolkien, eventually coming up with the idea for A Song of Ice and Fire, now widely regarded as one of the greatest and most influential fantasy book series of modern times. It helped prove that the genre was more than ready to tell stories far darker, more mature, and more morally complex than what Tolkienesque high fantasy had been delivering for decades. The third book in the series is A Storm of Swords, still remembered by many as not only the best chapter in A Song of Ice and Fire so far, but also as one of the best fantasy novels ever written.

In a recent development, it is, at the very least, one of the best fantasy books since 2000; a bleak, exciting, brilliantly paced masterpiece that went on to win the 2001 Locus Award, be nominated for the Nebula Award for Best Novel, and become the first novel in the series to be nominated for the Hugo Award. There's no denying that there could never have been any Song of Ice and Fire—nor, by proxy, Storm of Swords—without Tolkien's influence in Martin's writing, but that's exactly what makes this novel so exceptional. Crafted in many ways as a direct subversion of many a Tolkienesque epic fantasy trope, A Storm of Swords is as politically complex as it is emotionally devastating. Balancing death, tragedy, action, and psychological realism to utter perfection, Martin delivered one of the most brilliant works of dark fantasy in history, culminating in an entire second half that's as breathtaking and propulsive as The Lord of the Rings' best battles.

It's complicated to compare ancient tales and myths to modern fantasy, since both genres are so dramatically different yet so essentially intertwined. Nonetheless, no one would call anybody crazy for saying that Homer's Odyssey does epic fantasy better than not only The Lord of the Rings, but practically any other epic fantasy book ever written. It is, after all, the fantasy epic par excellence, the poem that laid down the foundational blueprint for how Western writing would approach the fantasy and adventure genres forevermore. It established the core tropes of the genre long before "fantasy" actually existed as a properly distinct literary category, and is still a must-read for pretty much anybody and everybody with the ability to read a book.

Born as an oral tradition and not put into writing until around the mid-6th century BC, The Odyssey is the literal bedrock on which the entire Western epic literary tradition is built, which almost makes it unfair to compare any other subsequent masterpiece to it. Indeed, there aren't very many fantasy epics better than The Lord of the Rings, but if there's one whose superiority is pretty much undisputed, it's Homer's masterpiece. That's because, far from only being historically crucial, The Odyssey is so timeless that it's arguably still just as entertaining as any other epic fantasy masterwork. Intelligent, full of high-stakes set pieces, and infinitely analyzable from start to finish, it's the most perfect work of epic fantasy the world has ever seen.

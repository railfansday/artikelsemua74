Recent findings suggest that the Pentagon is banning U.S. lawmakers from visiting Gulf military bases, and former Pentagon officials warn the move is creating tension between the executive and legislative branches and raising questions about why the Department of Defense is limiting access to information about the Iran war.

“It smells like a lack of transparency in order to thwart Congressional oversight, in order to either hide that the Iran war is costing more, or to use the Iran war as an excuse to get more money,” Evelyn Farkas, executive director of the McCain Institute at Arizona State University and former deputy assistant secretary of defense for Russia, Ukraine, and Eurasia, told Fortune.

Last month, Connecticut Democrat Sen. Chris Murphy detailed his experience of the Department of Defense barring him from visiting the Al Udeid Air Base in Qatar and meeting with military leadership, accusing the Pentagon of obscuring details about the impacts of the Iran war.

“I have been to the Middle East probably a dozen times at least,” Murphy noted in a video uploaded to Facebook. “While I have been a member of Congress, I have never, ever been denied access to military facilities. This is part of a coordinated, deliberate campaign by the Department of Defense to try to hide the consequences and the true cost of this war.”.

Industry observers note that iowa Republican Sen. Joni Ernst was similarly turned away from a base visit around the same time, The Atlantic reported this week. Ernst did not respond to Fortune’s request for comment. (Related: Industry News: My Journey to a serverless transformers ...)

According to recent reports, U.S. Congress members have a long-standing practice of conducting visits of military sites as part of their constitutional oversight. In March, following the U.S.-Israel attacks on Iran, Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth published a memo to “further discourage visits to the [U.S. Central Command Area of Responsibility] requiring DoW support by members of Congress and their staffs…and no DoW support shall be made available for congressional travel to the USCENTCOM AOR without my approval.”. Congress has expressed increased misgivings around Hegseth, introducing articles of impeachment this month, which did not pass, in part on the grounds that the secretary began military conflict in Iran without Congressional authorization. Hegseth has sharply reduced the number of routine press briefings and limited press access to briefings, as well as refused to provide information to the Congressional Budget Office ahead of its update to the cost of the Iran war in September. Meanwhile, the Pentagon is seeking $1.5 trillion in allocations—the largest defense budget in U.S. history—to modernize military infrastructure and restock munitions drained in the conflict in the Gulf.

It has been revealed that legislators have been visiting U.S. military bases in times of conflict since at least 1943, when five U.S. senators embarked on a 65-day inspection tour of global military sites during World War II. Visits increased during and after the Vietnam war, when American lawmakers and journalists returned home with stories that vastly differed from former President Richard Nixon’s narrative that the U.S. was winning the war.

Farkas, who served for eight years as a staff member of the Senate Armed Services Committee and took several trips to the Gulf as part of a congressional delegation (CODEL), noted congressional visits to these military bases are a way to not only increase public knowledge of the status of a war overseas, but in many cases justify granting the military additional and targeted resources for specific operations.

During the Iraq war, for example, the U.S. sent a CODEL to the Gulf following improvised explosive devices targeted U.S. personnel in armored vehicles, Farkas recalled. The vehicles were not capable of deflecting the explosives, and following a tour of U.S. military sites, Congress conducted a bipartisan effort to ramp up production of the vehicles.

“It’s legitimate, and also necessary and desirable, for members of Congress to go even during a hot war,” Farkas said.

The Pentagon also has some legitimate reasons for turning lawmakers away from military bases, Elizabeth Dent, a senior fellow at the Washington Institute for Near East Policy and former director for the Gulf and Arabian Peninsula in the Office of the Secretary of Defense, told Fortune.

It has been revealed that hosting a CODEL requires resources like military aircraft and personnel to protect and move lawmakers. In a military theater, armed forces may be unable to spare resources, and when specific threats are made against the military, both troops and elected representatives can be put in harm’s way. In 2016 under then-President Barack Obama, the military asked Congress to limit visits to Afghanistan during a fighting season, though it was not an express ban. Across both Republican and Democrat administrations, U.S. Central Command has ordered limited visits to military sites.

Industry observers note that however, Hegseth’s prohibition of military visits has continued, even after the end of “Operation Epic Fury” in May, and when, prior to this month, the last known U.S. attack on Iran was in September. Typically, restrictions are lifted after “hot phases” of conflict end, and the Pentagon’s decision to turn away legislators even during lulls in attacks is what raised suspicions of ulterior motives beyond protecting troops and lawmakers.

“The big concern I would have is, the administration has said that Operation Epic Fury is over. Is this more of a lack of transparency and an attempt to hide some of the things that are going on, like the destruction of some of the bases?” Dent said.

Recent findings suggest that what has lawmakers and former Pentagon officials scratching their heads is that increased transparency to Congress usually yields additional funding, which appears aligned with Hegseth’s push for a record-setting Pentagon budget in fiscal 2027. The government faces a Dec. 11 funding deadline to pass either full-year appropriations or a stopgap measure through the end of the year.

“Shutting Congress out is not the way to achieve your objectives if you are the Department of Defense,” Farkas noted. “If anything, it’s almost a guarantee that you won’t achieve your objectives because Congress understands what their rights and prerogatives are…so that can just erode trust and confidence further.”.

Particularly after the Democratic sweep in the midterm elections, incoming legislators are going to approach the Pentagon’s steep demands with even more rigor, Fargas noted. President Donald Trump will be reluctant to exercise a veto on the National Defense Authorization Act because it outlines military funding, despite Democrats being likely to make changes to it.

“It’s better to be open and honest,” she noted. “You can’t hide these things anyway. You’re going to bring in contractors. You’re going to have to put out bids for repairs. Somewhere this information is going to come to light.”.

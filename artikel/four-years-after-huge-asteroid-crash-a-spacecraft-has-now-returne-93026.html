Four years after huge asteroid crash, a spacecraft has now returned to assess the damage

The Nasa craft was obliterated on impact. Four years on, a European Space Agency (ESA) mission, called Hera, is scouring the scene to fully assess the aftermath of the collision - with the first images set to be released later on Wednesday.

The asteroid poses no threat, instead this was the first ever test of our planetary defences to see what action we could take if a space rock was heading for our planet.

"Hera is a sort of Inspector Columbo going back to the crash scene to investigate what is the outcome of this impact," remarked Patrick Michel, principal investigator of ESA's Hera mission.

This was the last image from Dart before it crashed into Dimorphos in 2022.

The crash test focussed on a pair of asteroids: a larger one called Didymos - about 730m (2,400ft) wide - which is orbited by a smaller rock called Dimorphos, about 150m (490ft) wide.

The Dart spacecraft targeted Dimorphos, hurtling into the rock at 22,000km per hour (14,000mph). Images beamed back showed the asteroid's rocky surface coming ever closer until the feed cut out on impact.

It was such a substantial smash-up that the trail of debris, measuring about 10,000km long (6,200 miles), was spotted by telescopes on Earth and in space.

The crash showed it was possible to change the path of the smaller asteroid.

Before the collision it took Dimorphos 11 hours and 55 minutes to orbit around Didymos. After the Dart crash, the duration of its orbit had shrunk by about 33 minutes.

But the arrival of the second spacecraft, Hera, will help scientists to answer many more questions.

"Dart exploded in the asteroid, and therefore we don't have the information of what is its final state," explained Michel.

It has been revealed that "The impact could have left a crater - and if it is the case, we need to understand the size and the depth. Or [the impact] may have entirely reshaped the asteroid.".

A 10,000km-long (6,200 miles) debris trail was spotted by the Hubble Space Telescope.

The Hera mission will spend the next year studying Dimorphos, and take a look at Didymos too.

The spacecraft is packed with instruments, and it also has two small shoe-boxed sized satellites onboard, which can study the impact site up close, and then later land on the asteroid.

A key task is to measure the mass of Dimorphos, to work out how effective the spacecraft's shove was.

This information would be vital if we ever spotted a space rock that was on a collision course with Earth.

"It would help us to predict what would happen if we had to hit another asteroid," remarked Prof Simon Green, a planetary scientist who is on Hera's science team.

"We [could] measure its properties and say: 'Well, we need this mass of spacecraft, travelling at this speed, this early, in order to deflect it enough so that it misses the Earth'.".

Hera has already sent back an image of Didymos with Dimorphos in the background.

Asteroids pose a significant threat to our planet.

The biggest, which measure more than a kilometre across, could cause global devastation. But medium-sized asteroids around the same dimensions as Dimorphos could wipe out a city.

In a recent development, "We all know what happened to the dinosaurs, right? And we don't want that to happen to us," stated Carolyn Ernst, Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory, who works on both the Dart and Hera missions.

"The good news for us is that there are far fewer potential threats out there in the Solar System than there were millions of years ago. But there are objects out there.".

She remarked the biggest asteroids have been spotted, and none are heading to Earth.

"The smallest stuff isn't too much of a threat because our atmosphere protects us," she added.

"But there's a size range in the middle where we don't know where all of them are, and it could, if impacted, cause problems for humanity. And if we can find out about that and know about that, well, we sure as heck would want to try to stop that.".

Hera has two small satellites onboard to study the asteroid up close.

Scientists want to gather as much intelligence as they can about space rocks - to make us as well equipped as possible to deal with whatever the Universe throws at us.

A new mission is launching in 2027 to track potentially hazardous asteroids: a space telescope called the Near-Earth Object (NEO) Surveyor.

There are also more visits planned to asteroids, including to one called Apophis, which is about 450m (1,480ft) long.

Apophis was discovered in 2004 and it was initially thought it might hit the Earth as early as 13 April 2029.

But now scientists understand its orbit better, and say it won't hit us for at least the next 100 years. Instead of a crash in 2029, it will fly just 32,000km (20,000 miles) above our surface.

This will give scientists the rare chance to study a space rock up-close.

Two spacecraft will head to Apophis, including Nasa's Osiris-Apex (which previously grabbed a sample from another asteroid called Bennu) and Ramses, a joint mission between ESA and the Japanese Space Agency (JAXA).

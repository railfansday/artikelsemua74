Three Choirs unveils First Release white wine after long, hot summer led to earliest harvest start in 50 years.

Forget beaujolais nouveau: a Gloucestershire vineyard has uncorked its 2026 vintage, giving the first taste of this year’s long, hot summer – and an answer to France’s autumn tradition of opening young wine straight after harvest. (Related: Summer at Hugging Face...)

Three Choirs near Newent has unveiled its First Release white wine after the earliest harvest in its 50 years of producing wine.

Recent findings suggest that the First Release is what the vineyard calls its “Gloucestershire nouveau” – its tongue-in-cheek nod to beaujolais nouveau, a young, fruity red that is released on the third Thursday in November. Beaujolais nouveau was traditionally made for local consumption and was a way for workers and villages to enjoy the first sip of that year’s harvest.

Vines were first planted on the Gloucestershire site of a fruit farm in 1973, while Three Choirs has been bottling a young wine every November since the 1980s. Customers can buy the wine directly from the vineyard’s shop or try it in its restaurant. (Related: Latest Update: How we sped up transformer inference 100...)

In the 1980s, the harvest used to begin in mid-September, about a month later than it did this year, according to Three Choir’s winemaking director, Martin Fowke.

Industry observers note that this year’s prolonged summer of warm temperatures – and rain at the right time – meant the first grapes were picked on 20 August, a week earlier than the previous record set in 2025. The harvest ended on 9 October, by which time 200 tonnes of grapes had been collected across the vineyard’s 30 hectares (74 acres).

The 2026 First Release is a run of 5,000 bottles of a dry, white wine containing solaris, bacchus, siegerrebe and madeleine angevine grapes.

“We now have an extra month to make the wine. We had riper raw material and longer in the winery,” remarked Fowke, calling 2026 a “record-breaking year”. He added that higher sugar levels and lower acidity allowed winemakers to make a product that was not possible in Britain 30 years ago.

Sources indicate that the early timing of the harvest surprised even the Three Choirs winemaker Jack Abbotts, who was on his summer break when picking started. “It’s usually a safe week for me, but this year it all snowballed,” he said.

The changing climate has made it possible to grow grapes on a commercial scale in Britain, but it also brings challenges.

In a recent development, “We found it hard this year,” stated Fowke. “People kept saying: ‘This hot weather must be good for you’, but it was difficult because we knew the heat and lack of rain was challenging for farmers. We don’t need it to get any warmer, that’s for sure.”.

In a recent development, vines have been planted as far north as Yorkshire and Scotland, as Britain’s wine industry expands. More than 1,100 vineyards are now registered in Britain.

While Britain remains far down the list of global wine producers in terms of volume – behind countries including Uzbekistan and Tunisia – it is the fastest-growing wine region in the world, according to the property group Knight Frank. The area of planted vineyards in the country has quadrupled since the turn of the century, it reports.

Industry observers note that some of the newer, smaller vineyards do not have their own winemaking facilities, relying on contract winemakers such as Three Choirs. The Gloucestershire vineyard also produces wine for about 40 others, including Llanerch, located west of Cardiff.

English and Welsh wine producers had their second-largest harvest in 2025, when the equivalent of 16.5m bottles, or 124,377 hectolitres, was produced, according to the industry body WineGB.

The data for the 2026 harvest will not be available until early next year, although WineGB noted in August it was “optimistic” about the size and quality after the hot growing season.

If the UK climate continues to change and summers such as 2026’s become the norm, Three Choirs may consider growing new varieties. “We can evolve and change, but we would be planting cabernet sauvignon like they did in Bordeaux 50 years ago,” stated Fowke, noting the shift from white to red grapes. “But that is not what we set out to do.”.

In the fog and damp of November in Gloucestershire, pruning of the vines ready for the 2027 growing season has already begun. “To have two good years in a row is not uncommon, to have three is unusual,” said Fowke.

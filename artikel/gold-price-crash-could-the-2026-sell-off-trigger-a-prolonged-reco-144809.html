Gold has suffered a sharp correction after reaching a record high near $5,595 in January 2026, with prices falling to around $3,500 in November.

Rising US Treasury yields, a stronger dollar and expectations of further Federal Reserve rate hikes have weakened demand for non-yielding bullion.

Historical gold bull markets have also ended in deep corrections, but previous recovery timelines do not establish how long the current downturn will last.

Gold’s historic rally has turned into one of the year’s sharpest commodity reversals. After reaching record levels above $5,500 in January, the precious metal has surrendered a substantial portion of its gains, with prices briefly falling below $3,500 in November. The correction has raised concerns about whether the long-running gold bull market has entered a deeper downturn.

Rising interest-rate expectations, a stronger US dollar and weakening investment demand are creating fresh challenges for bullion, while historical market cycles offer a warning about the potential duration of major corrections.

Recent findings suggest that gold’s decline has reversed a significant part of the rally that pushed prices to record levels earlier this year. Bullion reached an all-time high near $5,595 per ounce in January before entering a prolonged correction.

By November, prices had fallen below $3,500, representing a decline of roughly 37% from the peak. Gold subsequently rebounded, with spot prices rising 1.6% to $3,622.91 on November 27, but the recovery has not yet established a clear reversal in the broader trend.

The correction has revived comparisons with previous gold bull markets. Historical cycles show that powerful rallies can be followed by extended periods of losses and sideways trading.

For example, the 1970s bull market was followed by a severe correction that kept gold below its previous peak for decades. Similarly, after the 2011 peak, bullion did not establish a sustained move to a new record until 2020.

Sources indicate that these comparisons highlight the risks of buying near market peaks. However, the size and duration of previous corrections should not be treated as a forecast for the current cycle. Monetary policy, central-bank demand and global investment conditions are different across each period. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

Industry observers note that the biggest pressure on gold has come from changing expectations around US monetary policy. Persistent inflation has strengthened expectations that the Federal Reserve could raise interest rates again. Higher yields make interest-bearing assets such as Treasury securities more attractive relative to gold, which does not generate income. The US dollar has also added pressure. Since international gold prices are denominated in dollars, a stronger greenback makes bullion more expensive for buyers using other currencies and can weaken demand.

Recent market developments have reinforced these concerns. Gold fell below $3,500 on November 25 as investors responded to stronger inflation pressures and expectations of further Fed tightening. The metal remained under pressure the following day, before recovering on November 27.

Recent findings suggest that the broader market environment has also contributed to selling. During periods of market stress, investors sometimes liquidate gold positions to raise cash or cover losses elsewhere, weakening its traditional safe-haven appeal.

Gold’s price chart demonstrates a clear deterioration in its technical structure. After reaching its January peak, the metal formed a sequence of lower highs and lower lows, with selling pressure becoming more pronounced in November. The asset price has also moved below its short- and long-term moving averages. These indicators are now positioned above the current price, suggesting that buyers face multiple resistance barriers before a sustained recovery can develop.

The latest rebound from the November low offers some relief, but it has not yet reversed the broader downtrend. A sustained move above the short-term moving averages would be an initial signal that selling pressure is easing. However, failure to hold the recent recovery could bring the November low back into focus.

A decisive break below that area would increase downside risks, while a stronger recovery accompanied by improving momentum could help gold establish a more stable base.

Historical corrections show why investors should be cautious after an unusually strong rally. Gold’s previous major peaks were followed by lengthy recovery periods, leaving investors who bought near those highs waiting years to regain their positions.

The current correction raises a similar question, particularly as higher interest rates challenge the investment case for a non-yielding asset. However, gold’s long-term outlook also depends on factors beyond US interest rates. Central-bank purchases, geopolitical uncertainty, currency diversification and renewed demand for safe-haven assets could support prices if macroeconomic conditions change.

In a recent development, gold’s November rebound has provided some relief, but the broader technical structure remains under pressure. Investors will closely monitor US inflation data, Treasury yields, dollar movements and Federal Reserve signals for clues about the next direction.

If rate-hike expectations continue to strengthen, gold could face further selling pressure. A shift toward a less restrictive monetary outlook, combined with renewed investment demand, could support a more durable recovery.

The key question is whether November’s correction marks a temporary reset after an extraordinary rally or the beginning of a longer consolidation cycle. For now, gold needs stronger buying momentum to demonstrate that its latest rebound can develop into a sustained recovery.

According to recent reports, guilherme Joventino is Co-Founder and COO of MIGNOW. His background includes work on global SAP teams at Philips, Sandoz, and Bayer. At MIGNOW, he focuses on making enterprise modernization more predictable through phased delivery, automation, and continuous system evolution, while retaining the business expertise and accountability needed for critical ERP environments.

Recent findings suggest that MIGNOW develops proprietary software that uses AI and machine learning to automate SAP migration, upgrades, and ongoing modernization. Its approach combines strategy and planning, technical conversion, stabilization, and continuous evolution, with tools for assessing legacy environments and moving toward SAP S/4HANA and a cleaner, more adaptable core. Industry observers note that you’ve worked on global SAP teams at Philips, Sandoz, and Bayer before co-founding MIGNOW. What recurring challenges convinced you that enterprise modernization needed a different approach? (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

Industry observers note that one pattern kept repeating. Companies kept adding “what ifs” to their SAP projects. What if we also redesign this process? What if we add this new requirement? What if we solve that old problem while we’re at it? Each idea made sense on its own, but together they turned migrations into huge projects that were very hard to predict. Timelines slipped, budgets grew and everything rested on one high-risk go-live.

Scale made this worse. When SAP revealed the end of mainstream maintenance for ECC, it became clear the market couldn’t keep working this way. Around 40,000 SAP ECC customers worldwide need to move to SAP S/4HANA, and the industry doesn’t have enough specialists to run that many complex, manual projects.

Recent findings suggest that that’s why we see transformation as a series of clear, measurable phases rather than one big event. You start with the destination, then move step by step. Each phase has a defined scope, so the organization always knows where it stands and what comes next. A predictable process carries less risk, and it starts delivering value sooner.

For readers who are less familiar with enterprise software, how can an aging SAP system limit a manufacturer’s AI ambitions, and which limitations are hardest to overcome?

It has been revealed that I usually compare it to a smartphone that hasn’t been updated in ten years. It still makes calls, but the newly introduced apps don’t run on it.

Recent findings suggest that an older SAP ERP keeps the business running, but it wasn’t built for the way AI works today. SAP’s newest AI capabilities, such as Joule and SAP Business AI, are designed for SAP S/4HANA and the cloud. On top of that, many legacy environments are hard to integrate. One IT leader we worked with put it simply: before the migration, every time the business asked to integrate something, the answer was “we can’t.”.

It has been revealed that technology isn’t the hardest limitation to overcome. Accumulated complexity is. Years of custom code, poorly documented business logic and data spread across different structures make it difficult for any AI tool to understand what the system is doing. AI is only as useful as the environment it works in. If the foundation is fragmented, AI just makes that complexity move faster.

It has been revealed that what does making an SAP environment “AI-ready” actually involve beyond migrating to the cloud? How do you assess whether its data, integrations, and business processes can support useful AI applications?

Moving to the cloud changes where the system runs. It doesn’t automatically change how ready the system is. For me, an AI-ready SAP environment has four characteristics: a current version of SAP S/4HANA, a core that is as clean and standard as possible, reliable data and integrations that work through modern interfaces instead of point-to-point workarounds.

Clean Core is a central part of this. When customizations are kept outside the core, the system can receive SAP’s updates and innovations, including AI features, without breaking what the business built over the years.

Our assessment starts before any conversion. In the first phase of our methodology, we use our own AI-powered tools to analyze the current landscape: what is customized, what is actually used, where the critical gaps are and which dependencies could create risk. That gives the organization a factual picture of its environment and a roadmap based on its real system, not on assumptions or deadlines.

Could you walk us through how MIGNOW modernizes a legacy SAP environment, explaining where you use AI, where you rely on rules-based automation, and where human expertise remains essential?

Our methodology has four phases: strategy and planning, technical conversion, stabilization and continuous evolution. Each one has its own goals and deliverables.

Sources indicate that our AI is based on machine learning. It learns what worked in previous projects and applies that knowledge to new ones. In practice, this means that in our case AI and rules-based automation aren’t really separate things. Everything our AI automates follows the same rules and patterns, consistently, because those patterns come from hundreds of real projects. Every new project makes the next one more accurate.

Recent findings suggest that human expertise comes in mainly where an environment has a very high volume of complex custom code. Even there, depending on what the code does, our AI can often automate much of the work. At Eternit, for example, 11,535 of 11,652 custom code objects were adapted automatically, and only 117 required manual work.

People are also essential to the decisions around the conversion: understanding what the business needs, validating results with key consumers and owning the go-live. Automation removes repetitive work. It doesn’t remove accountability.

Industry observers note that years of custom code often capture business logic that is poorly documented. How does your technology determine what to preserve, rewrite, or retire—and verify that automated changes maintain the intended behavior?

Recent findings suggest that our view is that the business logic should be preserved, but not the code exactly as it was written. Instead, we update and optimize custom code so it performs the same functions using SAP’s more modern tools, such as SAP BTP and other current technologies.

Sources indicate that keeping custom code exactly as it is can feel safer in the short term, but it leaves the system “dirty.” Every customization built directly into the core makes future upgrades slower, riskier and more expensive. In the end, the firm is moving its technical debt to a upcoming platform instead of solving it.

That’s the idea behind our Clean Core solution. Our AI, trained on hundreds of projects, identifies the changes that pull the system away from SAP’s standard and recreates outdated objects using models SAP recommends. That can mean turning older reports into SAP Fiori apps, moving RFC interfaces to OData services or moving PI/PO integrations to Cloud Integration on SAP BTP. In SAP’s terms, we turn low-maturity legacy code into extensions aligned with the Tier 1 and Tier 2 models SAP recommends.

Verification is part of the process. The solution generates tests and technical documentation for each change, which supports audits and governance. The results are then validated in non-production environments, with the business areas involved, before anything reaches production.

The goal goes beyond a successful migration. We want a system that is easier to evolve every time SAP releases something new.

With mainstream maintenance for SAP Business Suite 7 core applications ending in 2027 and optional extended maintenance available through 2030, how should companies weigh the cost of waiting against the risks of rushing a migration?

I don’t think the real choice is between waiting and rushing. The real choice is between planning on your own terms and reacting to a deadline.

Recent findings suggest that extended maintenance buys time, but it comes at an additional cost, and it doesn’t solve the underlying problem. Meanwhile, technical debt keeps growing, the system falls further behind and the company postpones its access to new SAP capabilities, including AI.

There is also a capacity concern that I think many companies underestimate. Tens of thousands of companies still need to migrate, while the market can deliver only a few thousand projects per year. As 2027 and 2030 get closer, specialists become scarcer and more expensive.

In a recent development, one of our clients mentioned it very clearly: the longer you postpone this decision, the worse it gets. My recommendation is to start with a solid assessment now. Once you understand your landscape, you can move at a pace that is safe for your business instead of one dictated by the calendar.

Could you share a concrete example from your work with Carrefour or Eternit, including the original challenge, measurable results, and whether the modernization enabled any AI capabilities that were previously difficult to deploy?

Two projects show different sides of this.

Carrefour Brasil came to us with a very specific challenge. They wanted to move from SAP ECC to SAP S/4HANA through RISE with SAP and be live in time for Black Friday, which is the most vital date for retail e-commerce in Brazil. Other proposals had estimated about 18 months. We completed the project in three and a half months, converting 7,566 objects with 99.3% automation, and the latest environment supported Carrefour’s transaction volumes during Black Friday. With SAP S/4HANA in place, Carrefour could start delivering new functionalities and technologies to the business, including recommendation capabilities based on historical data.

Industry observers note that eternit, a leading Brazilian building materials organization, had a different situation: an outdated SAP version, two separate SAP environments that needed to be unified into one instance, several parallel subprojects and a lean team. Working with Seidor and AWS, we completed a Shell conversion in 11 months with about 99% automation of custom code. The go-live was smooth. Within a day and a half, the company was invoicing normally, and the project closed with zero escalations. Eternit was also ready for Brazil’s tax reform.

Recent findings suggest that to be precise about AI: in Eternit’s case, the migration removed the barrier rather than delivering AI on day one. Their team is now working on Clean Core and identifying AI quick wins to reduce operational workload, something the old environment simply couldn’t support.

It has been revealed that when AI is involved in changing systems that support finance, inventory, or procurement, errors can have significant consequences. What testing, audit trails, and human approval processes do you use before changes reach production?

I agree completely. In an ERP, a small error can affect invoicing, inventory or a company’s financial statements, so speed can never come at the expense of control.

It has been revealed that that’s why we developed our own methodology, inspired by SAP’s methodology and supported by professionals with more than a decade of SAP experience. Every project follows clear phases, and nothing goes directly to production. Changes move through SAP’s standard landscape, from development to quality assurance and only then to production, which gives every change traceability. The key consumers from each business area test their own processes before go-live, because they are the people who know whether a result is actually correct.

The go-live itself is a planned, joint decision with the client, with clear acceptance criteria and a contingency plan. After the project, we have a dedicated hypercare period to monitor the environment and resolve issues before they affect operations.

For me, the role of AI in this process is to give people better information and remove repetitive work, not to replace their approval. The more critical the process, the more important it is that a person with business knowledge signs off.

As enterprises explore AI agents that can take actions inside business systems, what safeguards are needed around data access, permissions, and transaction execution—and how should modernization prepare for them?

An AI agent inside an ERP shouldn’t just be capable of taking action. We need to know exactly what it can access, what it’s allowed to change and how every action is monitored and audited.

According to recent reports, in practice, that means applying the same principles we already use for people, and making them stricter: least-privilege access, clear authorization profiles, approval steps for sensitive transactions and complete logs of what the agent did and why. Companies also need to know where their data is processed and which models they are using, especially in regulated industries.

It has been revealed that modernization is what makes these safeguards possible. In a legacy environment full of undocumented customizations, it’s very hard to define clear boundaries, because nobody knows exactly what each piece of code does. A current, cleaner SAP environment with well-defined processes and permissions gives AI agents a safe space to operate. The more autonomy we give these systems, the more the foundation matters.

According to recent reports, looking ahead three to five years, do you expect SAP modernization to become a continuous, increasingly autonomous process? What technical or organizational barriers still need to be solved for that vision to become practical?

Recent findings suggest that yes, I believe that’s where the market is going, and it’s the core of how we think at MIGNOW. Migration isn’t the destination. It’s the starting point. After moving to SAP S/4HANA, companies should keep their systems updated continuously, the same way we expect our phones to receive updates without a major project every time.

Industry observers note that technically, the main barriers are custom code that keeps accumulating, testing that still depends heavily on manual effort and the lack of visibility into how everyday changes affect the landscape. Clean Core and automation help address all three.

In a recent development, the organizational barriers may be harder. Many companies still see ERP modernization as a large, painful project they do once a decade, and that perception has to change.

Recent findings suggest that governance has to keep up with autonomy, too. Companies need clear rules about what can be automated, who approves what and how results are measured.

I think the companies that make progress will be the ones that treat modernization as an ongoing capability rather than a one-time event.

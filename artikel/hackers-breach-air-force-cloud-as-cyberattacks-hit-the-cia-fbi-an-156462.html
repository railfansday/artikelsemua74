Reuters tracked 2026 intrusions at the Air Force, CIA, FBI, Pentagon and more. Five incidents, and who did it is still unclear.

According to recent reports, A government hack is often pictured as one dramatic event, but in 2026 break-ins at U.S. agencies became public one after another. President Trump confirmed in a Fox News interview on Nov. 21 that hackers had broken into an FBI database, according to CNBC, and within days the public learned of a breach at an intelligence agency as well. Each new disclosure arrived before the last one had been explained. The targets also went beyond spy and military agencies to departments that most people rarely connect with national security. Industry observers note that what ties these breaches together is not a single attacker but how little is known about what the intruders took. No one has spelled out exactly what they got at the intelligence agency, according to the Honolulu Star-Advertiser, or which records they removed from the FBI database. Justin Sherman, a senior associate at the Center for Strategic and International Studies, claims no database hack should be waved off as routine because each one connects to others, according to TIME. Reuters lists the major cyber intrusions of 2026 into U.S. government agencies and other targets. Intruders got into the Air Force's main cloud platform and into a research tool that the CIA shares with the country's top intelligence office, while other attackers went after dozens of commercial ships. What each intruder could reach shows where the real exposure lies, from weapons data and intelligence tools to police records, personnel files and shipping.

Hackers who broke into Cloud One, the Air Force's flagship computing platform, got into the part of the system that manages the entire platform rather than a single application. They reached its control plane by exploiting a software flaw, according to TNW. From there, the intruders potentially exposed aircraft and radar data, along with data from a munitions system used at more than 200 sites.

Recent findings suggest that getting into the controls does not prove that anyone read the data behind them. No one has shown that the intruders looked at any aircraft or parts records, or publicly identified who got in. U.S. Cyber Command is assessing the damage. The Air Force stated in a statement to Reuters that it "does not comment on the security posture, vulnerabilities or operational status of specific networks and systems.".

It has been revealed that whether or not the hackers took anything, the Air Force has already lost the use of its flagship cloud for fresh work. The service's initiative to shift its back-office programs onto the platform is on hold, and the software already hosted there can no longer receive updates.

In a recent development, A breach at an intelligence agency makes most people picture stolen secrets, but these hackers got into the tool the agencies use to read the open world. They accessed the open-source scanning system of the CIA and the Office of the Director of National Intelligence, which scans unclassified, publicly available material.

The only public reassurance is narrow. The Office of the Director of National Intelligence remarked there were "no disruptions to critical capabilities or classified systems." That statement rules out breakage, not entry.

Early signs point to possible Chinese involvement, but no account of how the intruders got in has been made public, so China remains a suspicion rather than a confirmed finding.

The hackers reached a tool two intelligence agencies use, and who did it is still unsettled.

Sources indicate that at the Defense Manpower Data Center, hackers went after the people who serve and support the military, not its weapons or its intelligence tools. An earlier intrusion into the center affected 2.76 million living people, a fraction of the more than 60 million people the agency held records on in fiscal 2024.

Recent findings suggest that for the people in those files, the risk is personal. The exposed details include birthdates and military specialties, which together link who a person is to the work that person does for the military.

Intruders were inside the center's systems for months before anyone noticed. Unauthorized access began in October 2025, and no one found the weakness that let them in until July 16, 2026. No one has publicly named the attacker.

The people in those files may not learn for years whether anyone has used their details.

At Cloud One, the hackers did harm simply by getting in, and any theft that comes to light would only add to it.

Industry observers note that the FBI suffered two breaches in one fall. The first came in September, when intruders took data on FBI employees and forced the agency to shut down part of its operations until the breach was resolved. The second reached the National Crime Information Center, a shared criminal-justice system that police well beyond the FBI rely on, so a breach there becomes a problem for every department that checks a name against it.

This breach puts everyday police records at risk, so what matters is whether departments can still trust what they look up. The September theft was the work of a cybercrime group called ShinyHunters, which shows criminal hackers, not only foreign governments, are getting inside federal law enforcement. No one has publicly named who broke into the criminal database.

When Trump talked about the database break-in, he spoke of a possible upside rather than the damage, suggesting the breach might turn up evidence of voter fraud. "I always like to put things on a positive spin," Trump stated.

Police nationwide still rely on a system whose breach the government has acknowledged but not explained.

According to recent reports, the 2026 breaches reached civilian departments and commercial ships as well as spy and military systems. Personnel data leaked from the Department of Homeland Security, intruders broke into the Treasury Department, and as many as 50 ships carrying oil and other explosive cargo are believed to have been hit by cyberattacks. No details have been made public on what the intruders reached at either department.

Iran is the primary suspect in the ship attacks, according to The Wall Street Journal, and U.S. officials also see Russia as a possible culprit. A Trump administration official disputed that, saying the U.S. has found no evidence of Iranian involvement and that four attacks amounted to little more than a minor nuisance. Coast Guard cyber teams have responded to at least four of the ships as they approached U.S. ports.

Industry observers note that the attackers are exploiting a flaw in Fortinet $FTNT routers that are popular in the maritime industry. Some ships also received bomb threats, and the attacks come as Iran continues to mount physical attacks against ships in the Strait of Hormuz.

The ship attacks can't easily turn a tanker into a weapon, but a hacked vessel can still stall a cargo of fuel.

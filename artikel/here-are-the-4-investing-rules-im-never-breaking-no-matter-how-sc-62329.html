It remains to be seen whether the market's due for any further downside. But there's certainly no denying it's possible.

As scary as the past few weeks have been, from peak to trough the S&P 500 (^GSPC -3.70%) has only tumbled about 7%, leaving it shy of the minimum sell-off of 10% just to qualify as an ordinary correction. Given that we're overdue for such a move, the pullback from October's peak so far still feels like it could continue unfurling. Plenty of investors, including me, are understandably concerned. Nevertheless, I've been in and around this business long enough to know there are just some things I and every other investor should make a point of doing no matter how scary things get. Here's a rundown of my four non-negotiable rules for surviving market setbacks, and for that matter, thriving during bullish periods. (Related: Think AI "knows" what it’s doing? Scientists say think ...)

It has been revealed that it will come as no surprise that all four are so well-preached that they've almost become cliché. There's a good reason for that, however.

As legendary investor Warren Buffett put it, "it's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price." This simply means that your priority should be owning quality investments even if it means paying a premium for them. In the long run, quality ends up paying for itself. Or as the antithesis adage goes, "cheap stocks are cheap for a reason.".

This idea is likely to give many investors more than a little pause here. Now three years into a bull market that's been led by AI-mania (which has also in turn drawn out some very speculative IPOs), it's going to be tough for most people to admit they've taken on some risk that's more rooted in hope than actual opportunity. It happens.

The good news is that any major reset for the market should jolt everyone -- and valuations -- back to more historically sustainable norms.

Most everyone has a basic time frame in mind when they're investing. For most of us, it ends with retirement, when we're all likely to dramatically reshape our portfolios for reliable income rather than growth.

According to recent reports, if we're being intellectually honest with ourselves, however, all of us are probably at least a little bit guilty of scooping up the occasional name that's not actually aligned with our target time frame. We're attracted by the buzzy bullish rhetoric, and dive in expecting above-average gains in a below-average amount of time. And that's OK. Again, it happens.

With the risk of a correction (and even a slim chance that it could turn into a full-blown bear market), however, I'm going through each of my remaining positions and asking myself if this is a stock I'm actually interested in holding through the next bear market just so I own it at the beginning of the next bull market. If not, I'm likely letting it go.

After 27 years in this business (which includes the dot-com mania of the late-90s followed by its implosion in the early-2000s), I've learned a thing or two about the boom and bust patterns that play out over and over again.

Sources indicate that the VIX soars to a wildly-high level? By that point, most everyone who was thinking about getting out has already gotten out. Are the pundits (literally) laughing at anyone that's cautiously concerned? Then buying has likely run its full course. No two scenarios are exactly the same, but all of the market's major turning points look pretty darn similar.

The thing is, exceptions to these seemingly predictable norms aren't particularly unusual, and even when they are more or less materializing when and where expected, being just a few days early or a few days late can cost you more than it's worth.

According to recent reports, numbers from mutual fund manufacturer Hartford put things in perspective. The company points out that the S&P 500 gains an average of 13.6% in just the first month of a new bull market, and during its first three months the index soars an average of 25.3%. As much as I'd like to sidestep marketwide weakness, the biggest risk and my biggest fear is missing out on those early moves just by being a few days late to the party.

Recent findings suggest that to be a successful market timer, you have to be perfect 100% of the time. I'm not. So, I'm not even going to try. I'll just improve my odds and scope of success by waiting out short-term weakness with long-term winners.

Finally, while most investors understand the benefits of diversification -- and many of them even make a tentative strategic allocation plan -- too many of us also forget to put these plans into real practice when and to the degree we need to.

Industry observers note that it's not just a matter of sector diversification either. After a long dry spell, small-cap stocks have finally come alive again to outperform large caps. International, precious metals, and real estate are also opportunities to keep on your radar even when they're not performing and everything else is.

Your allocation will never be picture-perfect. If you don't proactively make a point of strategically diversifying for both offensive and defensive reasons, though, you'll obviously never enjoy any of the very real net performance benefit of doing so.

I meet Oobah Butler on a wet afternoon in Chinatown outside an empty claw-machine arcade.

I spot the 34-year-old British hoax artist and gonzo journalist from way down the street, his platinum-blond mop top bobbing between umbrella-wielding tourists.

The reason he picked this interview location — a few blocks from the open-air counterfeit handbag hub of Canal Street — becomes clear when he pulls out his phone. Giddily, he indicates me a TikTok page called Clawmaster, an account dedicated to teaching followers how to win prizes out of these notoriously tricky arcade machines. According to recent reports, “Let’s out-scam the scammers,” he notes, grinning like the Cheshire Cat. We buy 50 tokens, and, as a bonus, the cashier lets Butler select from a mysterious deck of cards. Butler pulls from the top, revealing a Joker emblazoned with an anime character. She tells him he can redeem it later, so he slips it in his pocket. Butler sets his sights on a Charizard plushie, and we get to work. Scamming is Butler’s strong suit. Over the last decade, he’s gone viral a dozen or so times, pulling off stunts like listing bottles of Amazon drivers’ urine as an energy drink on the shopping platform and creating the Shed at Dulwich, an entirely fake restaurant that climbed to No. 1 in London on TripAdvisor. During a television press tour for the Shed, Butler auditioned lookalikes to do interviews in his place, fooling journalists around the world.

Industry observers note that now, Butler is preparing to reveal five more fraudulent feats on his new HBO show, “Oobah: Scams for a Better Tomorrow,” which debuts Sunday, Nov. 22. In the series, Butler immerses himself in the worlds of wellness, wine, music and online dating, and scams his way to the top. With a style somewhere between Nathan Fielder and Sacha Baron Cohen, Butler hilariously exposes the bullshit at the heart of our culture.

An example: In the second episode, Butler takes smoke-tainted wine, which is considered so unsellable that vineyards distill it into hand sanitizer, and through clever manipulation and questionable marketing, earns endorsements from two of the world’s top sommeliers and the Latest York Times wine critic. By the end of the episode, he’s selling it for $500 per bottle.

This penchant for scheming is in Butler’s blood.

“My great-grandfather came to America in the late 19th century and was in the fur trade. It notes in our family history that he used to sell rabbit droppings rolled in flour as medicine to people,” he says. “So I come from a long line of bullshit artists — or rabbit shit artists.”.

Sources indicate that by this point, Butler and I have sunk at least 30 tokens into the claw machines with nothing to show for it. Marios, Pokémon and blue aliens in Italian soccer jerseys continue to slip our metal grasp, and we’re starting to doubt the Clawmaster’s expertise. Who is scamming whom?

It has been revealed that “He suggested that if you drop the claw right down into the hole, it loosens up,” Butler indicates. But none of the TikTok tips are working. See, Clawmaster never posts footage of him losing. And, also, he only works on machines where the claw dangles from an elastic cord; the machines here at Anime Claw are much more rigid, dropping down from a taut wire. “For God’s sake, this is infuriating!” Butler yells as another Charizard falls back into the pile. “Clawmaster fucked us.”.

Aside from a finale stunt six years in the making, Butler filmed nearly all of “Scams for a Better Tomorrow” in the last few months. He initially pitched the last episode — which is far too ambitious and surprising to spoil here — to A24 as a feature documentary, but the studio passed. Later, its television department reached out to Butler and mentioned, “We want to make this a show.” With the help of Josh Safdie’s Central Pictures and “Nathan for You” production house Abso Lutely, Butler landed a pilot order at HBO, which would turn into a full series.

The first episode sees Butler breaking into the wellness industry with a skin serum made of children’s tears. The influencers love it; Wall Street wants to throw money at it. A real Newly introduced York dermatologist, after witnessing a group of kids crying into tubes, offers to endorse it — in exchange for a fee, of course.

Industry observers note that I ask Butler to define his ethics when it comes to duping people, but his eyes are lasered on a captive Jigglypuff. “Let me do this first, because that’s a harder question,” he says, chuckling. He misses.

“We tried not to punch down,” Butler finally notes. The joke is rarely on average people, but rather the highly awarded wine experts or the money-grubbing doctor. “I tried to be fair with people,” he says. Or, in the case of the dermatologist, “I’m being as fair as I need to be.”.

According to recent reports, still, Butler claims it’s “a bit nerve-racking” that his subjects have yet to see the show. In the edit, he had to cut a few experts from the series, but “I’d have to go off the record to tell you why,” he says, “because it’s a legal thing.”.

In a recent development, despite its puckishness, “Scams for a Better Tomorrow” is not so much a prank show. With each stunt, Butler seeks to reveal something deeper about human psychology.

“Our argument in the wine episode is not that these guys suck because they fell for my trick,” he states. “It’s that the snobbery of that industry is getting in the way of the reality, which is this could be a good product.”.

Sources indicate that butler was born Paul Arthur Butler, but his older sister nicknamed him Oobah as a baby. The name stuck, so much so that he legally adopted it in his early 20s. He grew up in Feckenham, a working-class English village of 700 people, and he credits his humble upbringing with instilling in him a confidence that would later come in handy.

According to recent reports, “I grew up with this sense that I could talk to anybody — it didn’t matter if it was the lord of the manor or the farmer down at the pub,” he indicates. “That gave me a sense of acting like I belong in any scenario. It’s a bit of a gift.”.

In his 20s, Butler sang and played guitar in a rock band, writing for music publications on the side. His band was so bad at selling records, he says, that it gave him an idea for an article. He would embed himself with the Jehovah’s Witnesses to learn their door-to-door marketing methods, but instead of hawking religion, Butler would go around peddling CDs.

“I wanted to see if it was harder to sell music than God,” he claims. In the battle of religion versus music, it turned out, music lost.

It has been revealed that he pitched the piece to Vice, which loved the idea, and the magazine brought Butler on as a freelancer. Soon enough he was writing regularly and making videos for Vice’s YouTube page that amassed millions of views. In a few years, Butler had leveraged his internet fame into a hosting gig on the British version of MTV’s “Catfish,” and he made two documentary features for the U.K.’s Channel 4.

In 2024, like his rabbit-shit-peddling ancestor, Butler relocated across the pond. He arrived in the United States with no job and no prepares. “I moved here on a whim and a feeling that my work would make sense here,” he states. “I found Latest York insanely inspiring.”.

America proved to be the perfect playground for “Scams for a Better Tomorrow.”.

Recent findings suggest that “There’s so much more you can say here that you can’t say back home in the U.K.,” Butler claims. “It’s harder in Europe to make claims about things. Here, you get a wider berth to scam — or market.”.

Case in point: He advertises that his dirt-cheap smoked wine is “Imported from Florence” but doesn’t exactly disclose that it’s simply been shipped to and from Florence, Massachusetts.

“Inauthenticity is easier than ever now,” he says, mentioning the number of AI-generated commercials he endures while watching “Frasier” reruns. “I find it fascinating how comfortable we’ve become with it.”.

Throughout the show, Butler meets with Wall Street investors and tech founders who open their checkbooks and offer to fund his crazy ideas. I ask if he’s ever tempted to turn off the cameras and actually pursue one.

Industry observers note that “I find it really hard to be motivated by money,” he notes. “It’s chasing the answer to questions, I suppose, that’s motivating to me.”.

Fifty-nine minutes into the interview, we suddenly and shockingly win our first prize. Butler lets out a squeak of joy and we instantly embrace, examining the brown plushie — a drumstick with ears. “I think it’s, like, a chicken-bone bear,” Butler claims.

The next 15 minutes are wildly prosperous. Clawmaster-style, Butler and I shake the joysticks violently, securing a tuxedoed penguin, a polar bear with a yellow beanie and a miniature Kirby. But as we head toward the exit of the arcade, Butler realizes he never caught the one thing he truly wanted: the Charizard.

In a recent development, he flashes his card at the cashier. “So what do I get with this?”.

“Any of the plushies,” she says, and Butler’s face lights up. She escorts us to the Pokémon machine and unlocks the cage.

According to recent reports, “This is America,” Butler says, smiling as he clutches his newly introduced orange fire breather. “There’s always a Joker card.”.

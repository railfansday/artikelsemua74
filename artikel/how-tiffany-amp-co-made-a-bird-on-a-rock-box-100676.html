How does a yellow cockatoo flitting near an open window in Guadeloupe end up a jewelry icon? That bird knew what it was doing when it perched outside the home of Jean Schlumberger, one of Tiffany & Co’s best-known jewelry designers. Nature was always the apple of Schlumberger’s eye, and in his vision that bird would become a diamond-studded feathered friend nestled in lapis lazuli and pinned on Bunny Mellon’s beret (he would go on to make for her a full, whimsical bestiary. Theirs was a partnership of hundreds of jelly fish, camels, and seahorses, plus the beloved fruit and flowers inspired by Mellon’s own garden). (Related: The Partnership: Amazon SageMaker and Hugging Face...)

That was 1965. How to keep reaching fresh heights? A partnership between Tiffany & Co. and the Musée des Arts Décoratifs (MAD) in Paris offers inspiration: An archive of thousands of Schlumberger’s sketches, now being restored and digitized, reveal a mind constantly at work and a vision without boundaries. And so it continues, in spessartine and chrysoprase, in rubellites and mother-of-pearl, and turquoise. The next reincarnation of the bird, though, might be one of the most exciting horological spectacles of the year: a glimmering, singing automata atop a burst of sun, a singing gold and titanium bird on a clock. Though, we must note, it does not play music: instead, it tweets. Look, and listen.

Three locations a website can read, and what a VPN actually moves.

Does a VPN change your IP? Yes. That is almost certainly what was being asked. When you connect to a VPN, your traffic is sent from a server in the selected country. Websites will see an IP address associated with that server, not your IP address over your home internet connection. Based on the information they deduced from your old IP address, they will now deduce information based on your newly introduced IP address.

It has been revealed that what does not change is your device. Your phone still knows its location via mobile masts, Wi-Fi and satellites. Your browser still remembers the permissions you've given. Your accounts still remember which country you signed up in. These are separate systems, and a VPN does not extend into them.

Try testing the connection by itself before you start looking for a problem. If you once clicked Allow on the popup that asks whether a site may know your location, you have given that website the geolocation permission, and it can continue to access your device's location instead of deducing it from your address.

On a phone that is more accurate than a location guess based on an IP address. This means that your browser can inform a website that your phone is in Dubai, while you are actually connected to a server in London. Both can indeed be true at once, which is why the website looks as though it has caught you out. Changing servers does not revoke any permissions you have already granted.

One tap on Allow, months ago, and the site no longer has to guess from your address at all.

An IP address is just one piece of the puzzle. Location on a mobile phone can be determined using satellite data, nearby Wi-Fi routers, mobile masts and also location sensors within the phone itself. This system is completely unaware of which country your data leaves from when you use a VPN. As a result, a location service can still determine your position even though your connection may seem to be originating from a different country.

This is more apparent with apps than with browsers. Location permission for an app is requested by the app from the operating system independently of the VPN. Thus, an app granted months ago will continue to report your true location.

Satellites, Wi-Fi hotspots and mobile masts put your phone on this street corner. None of them ask which country your VPN traffic leaves from.

Now imagine that you visit an online store based in the UAE and choose that location as your region. If you then return later from a German IP address, you might still see the UAE storefront. This is because your saved region preference is more specific than an inference made from your address, and switching IP addresses does not cause previously saved preferences to be removed from your browser. A private window would resolve this immediately.

Signing in gives a service far more than a lookup by address. A user account can remember the country associated with the profile, the card used to pay for it, the region of the app store where it was created, previous shipping addresses, and all previous sign-in locations.

Industry observers note that most services tend to place more value on that history than on whatever your connection claims today. Google does this with its search results, which can use past activity and location information from a user's device as well as the address in front of it. A latest address does not eliminate this information, which is why you may see a different version of the same site when you are signed out compared to when you are signed in.

There is also a less obvious possibility. Your VPN may have changed your address correctly, but the site then looked up that address in a database that disagrees. No one physically locates an address. Instead, sites query a commercial database. These databases vary from one another and also go out of date, a point Cloudflare makes plainly about geolocation.

One evening in November, Celia Rowlson-Hall slipped into a conference room for a rare break. The choreographer has spent the past four weeks rehearsing a fresh Off-Broadway revival of Spring Awakening, directed by Tony winner Danya Taymor, and it’s the second day of tech week. She had been holed up in Studio Seaview’s adjacent theater, fine-tuning “The Bitch of Living,” an angsty number about wet dreams, busty piano teachers, and Nietzsche. Previews were set to begin in a little over a week, and Rowlson-Hall eagerly tucked into a takeout grain bowl, fueling up for her evening ahead. Set in 19th-century Germany, Spring Awakening is about the turmoil of developing sexuality in teenagers in a society attempting to keep them in ignorance. In 2006, the playwright Steven Sater and the musician Duncan Sheik adapted it into a musical that won eight Tony Awards, including for choreographer Bill T. Jones, who translated rebellion and repression into a gestural vocabulary. That original production also launched a generation of musical theater stars, including Jonathan Groff, Lea Michele, and John Gallagher Jr. In this spirit, the production held an open audition that attracted 2,532 performers (two actors were cast from this process). Rowlson-Hall kept her eye out for those actors with a sense of grounded earthiness and truthful storytelling. “I’m always going to be looking out for authenticity and personality inside of movement,” she says. “I don't care about the pointed foot or the high leg; it's never interested me, even as a dancer. I've never understood dance for dance’s sake.”.

In a recent development, spring Awakening will mark Rowlson-Hall’s theatrical debut after nearly two decades of directing and choreographing for screen. Among her work are the scenes of ecstatic prayer in The Testament of Ann Lee and Aftersun’s devastating rave sequence (she also played the adult version of the lead character). Humorously, she recently appeared in The Drama as a dance teacher intent on perfection, a mentality that couldn’t be farther from Rowlson-Hall’s own intuitive and empathetic style. With major blue eyes and a choppy mop of dark hair, Rowlson-Hall, 42, is expressive even when sequestered behind a conference room desk. She frequently puts her fork down to gesture, including when recalling a friend’s suggestion “that all artists have one or two rocks that they will turn over their entire lives.” Rolling a pair of invisible stones around in her palms, she names religion and femininity as her thematic preoccupations. “The impact of religion in the body, specifically the female body, and what femininity looks like as taught to me by my parents versus from society and my own experiences.” She laughs, adding, “As my other friend remarked, ‘Celia loves a repression era.’”.

According to recent reports, like the characters in Spring Awakening, Rowlson-Hall grew up in a small, religious community. In her case, it was Urbanna, Virginia, population 492 as of 2020, in a Christian Scientist household. She started dancing at 5 and knew from a young age that she wanted to use movement to tell stories of her own. After studying at the North Carolina School of the Arts, Rowlson-Hall moved to Upcoming York in 2006, the same year Spring Awakening premiered. “I remember hearing about the show, but also being too poor to go,” she notes.

Two years later, she was planning to start her own dance company when she was asked to choreograph a music video for MGMT. She immediately felt at home on a film set, and also appreciated that the medium offered a more enduring result than a concert dancen ephemeral live performance. “The forever part,” she claims. “I needed it then in my life.” She continued working on music videos, commercials, and television, absorbing lessons on filmmaking along the way. After a series of movement-based feminist shorts, Rowlson-Hall used Kickstarter to finance her first feature, Ma (2015), in which she stars in the dialogue-free dance film as a modern-day Virgin Mary on a pilgrimage to Las Vegas.

Rowlson-Hall met her now-wife, director and showrunner Mia Lidofsky, while choreographing Girls’ acclaimed “Beach House” episode in 2013. Rowlson-Hall was in a heterosexual marriage at the time, and she describes coming out at 30 as “this incredible aha moment.” But then, “Shame came in and clamped me down. I stopped moving.” It was a confusing contrast: while experiencing a profound sense of freedom and selfhood, she became disconnected from her body for the first time. Rowlson-Hall is anxious to elaborate. “The impact of me coming out was very difficult on my family, they initially had a hard time accepting it,” she indicates. “Then my mom got very ill, and I put my life and body on pause because I was so scared for her.” From there followed a decade of extreme highs (falling in love, marriage, motherhood) and lows (divorce, several deaths including both parents). “There were a lot of roadblocks for me to be able to find physicality and movement again because I kept getting knocked down,” she says.

Sources indicate that she credits Ann Lee with allowing her to feel that connection again. The writer and director, Mona Fastvold, is a close friend; the two met back in the day on Gossip Girl. Rowlson-Hall was eager to inhabit the 18th century visionary, an illiterate blacksmith's daughter who became the founder and leader of the Shakers. “As a kid, I used to love to pray, it was my favorite thing,” she notes. “I really wanted to ask myself ‘what if I got to decide what prayer looks like?’” She could also relate to the Shakers’ use of dance as transcendence. “From when I was young, I always felt like I was needing to exorcise things out of my body that weren't mine,” Rowlson-Hall says. “So much of what I do is trying to clear out to actually understand who I am.” The viscera is not strictly a metaphor. “When I feel like I'm going to vomit, I know that I've tapped into the right thing,” she says. “I choreograph from there, and then I can't touch it again.” She makes a gagging sound. “And then it's out of my body.”.

Sources indicate that rowlson-Hall returned to the stage last year with Sissy, a semi-autobiographical dance-theater piece reimagining the myth of Sisyphus as the struggles of a female artist tasked with caring for a young child and an ill father. “There's something about theater and motherhood that feel very aligned,” she indicates with a laugh. “It's all alive and happening in front of you, and you've got to love it and let it go. It journeys on without you, you actually have no control.” That said, she’s working on her next film, which she describes as “a time traveling love story of Eve and Lilith exiting the Garden of Eden” and spending lifetimes together and apart. “There they are again,” Rowlson-Hall says. “Those old stones.”.

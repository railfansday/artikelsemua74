I looked again at Berkshire Hathaway (BRKA -0.47%)(BRKB -0.36%) on Nov. 17, and I ended up with a higher estimate of what the stock is worth. I now think the Class B shares are worth around $700 each, or about 22% over the stock's price near $575 as I write. (Related: Think AI "knows" what it’s doing? Scientists say think ...)

In a recent development, my estimate climbed mainly because of how CEO Greg Abel is managing Berkshire's money. He's been patient with the firm's huge cash pile, while the businesses Berkshire owns outright keep growing. (Related: AI breakthrough cuts energy use by 100x while boosting ...)

The $700 comes from adding up four parts: the cash and Treasury bills, the stock portfolio, the operating businesses and the insurance operation. But they don't carry equal weight.

It has been revealed that berkshire's balance sheet does a lot of the work before any business is valued. As of Sept. 30, its insurance and other businesses had around $352 billion in cash, cash equivalents and Treasury bills (net of Treasury purchases that settled in October). Its stock portfolio was worth about $336 billion more. Spread over the outstanding shares on that date, that's around $166 a Class B share in cash and about $158 in stocks. Together, they cover more than half of today's stock price.

According to recent reports, I'm counting both at full value, which is a bit generous. Berkshire would owe taxes on its $227 billion in unrealized gains if it sold, and the parent had around $20 billion in debt. Sure, taxes are due only when Berkshire sells, and it has owned some of its largest holdings for decades.

And Abel doesn't look to be in a rush to sell. Berkshire's stake in Apple has stayed at around 228 million shares for three quarters running, according to its 13F filings. Berkshire last trimmed it in the fourth quarter of 2025, Warren Buffett's final quarter as CEO.

That leaves around $377 of my estimate for the rest.

It has been revealed that in the past four quarters, BNSF, Berkshire Hathaway Energy and the manufacturing, service and retailing group earned about $25.4 billion after tax. And insurance underwriting added around $6.4 billion. Altogether, these operations earned around $31.8 billion.

According to recent reports, at $700 a share, I'm valuing them at around $800 billion, or about 25 times their total earnings. That's about the same price-to-earnings ratio the S&P 500 (^GSPC -3.70%) carries today, based on the last year of earnings.

At today's price, the market's valuing Berkshire's operations at just around 17 times earnings.

Industry observers note that put another way, the businesses are the swing factor. Almost all the gap between $575 and $700 depends on how much credit the operating companies get.

First, Berkshire's operating businesses are growing. Showing the momentum outside insurance, after-tax earnings at BNSF, Berkshire Hathaway Energy and the manufacturing, service and retailing group rose around 13% year over year through September. The OxyChem chemicals business Berkshire bought in January helped.

And BNSF's pre-tax earnings climbed around 15% in that time, with growth picking up to about 19% in the third quarter.

According to recent reports, insurance is the bumpier part. Third-quarter underwriting profit dropped around 43% year over year, driven by higher claims costs at GEICO. That sort of swing is normal for an insurer, and Berkshire itself warns that its underwriting results can be volatile from period to period.

Recent findings suggest that the bigger prize might be the float (premiums Berkshire keeps before paying claims), which stood at about $178.5 billion at the end of the third quarter. Through September, its average cost was negative, so Berkshire was effectively paid to keep it.

In a recent development, abel's patience demonstrates in Berkshire's trading, too. In the third quarter, the S&P 500 gained around 2%, and Berkshire sold a little more stock than it bought. Meanwhile, it spent about $9.6 billion on buybacks at average prices around $500 a Class B share -- two-thirds of its $14.4 billion nine-month total.

This restraint is also why I see Berkshire as a hedge against the artificial intelligence (AI) trade. Yes, Alphabet is one of its five biggest stock holdings. But most of what a buyer gets is cash, insurance, a railroad, utilities and factories, which might hold up better if AI spending ever slows.

In the end, I think around $700 is a fair value for a company whose cash and stocks alone cover more than half of today's price. The market is paying about 17 times earnings for the businesses, and I think they deserve more credit than that.

With the stock near $575, I'd be comfortable buying shares now and holding them for years. Berkshire is still my best stock pick for the next 10 years.

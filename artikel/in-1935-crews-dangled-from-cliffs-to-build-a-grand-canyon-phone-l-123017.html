In 1935, crews dangled from cliffs to build a Grand Canyon phone line, now the Park Service wants to its copper wire

Sources indicate that hanging wires in the inner canyon put helicopter and emergency operations and wildlife at risk. The Park Service says removing some would be an adverse effect on a listed historic line.

In the 1934-35 season, Civilian Conservation Corps crews built a telephone line down into the Grand Canyon and up the other side, according to a National Park Service history page. Workers often had to dangle from ropes on the cliffs to set poles into the rock.

It has been revealed that the line connected the North and South rims in 1935. It gave the two rims instantaneous communication, though it could handle only one call at a time, according to the park.

More than 90 years later, the Park Service has proposed removing sections of the line’s wire. Its original copper wire, now hanging or lying in places across the inner canyon, has become a hazard for helicopter and emergency operations and for wildlife.

The Trans-Canyon Telephone Line runs through the inner canyon, generally following the Bright Angel and North Kaibab trails, with a spur toward the South Kaibab Trail.

Industry observers note that it was strung from tree to rock, the park says, and it was frequently out of commission. While the park’s history page says the CCC built the line, the agency’s newest notice describes it as “reconstructed by Civilian Conservation Corps crews in 1935.”. It has been revealed that the Civilian Conservation Corps was created in 1933, when President Franklin Delano Roosevelt signed the law that set it up. The program gave poor, unemployed young men work on conservation projects across the country’s public lands. (Related: Stop treating agents as one-off projects and start runn...)

Recent findings suggest that at the Grand Canyon, its crews built trails, walls and shelters, and they helped the park put in roads, campgrounds and telephone lines. The program ended in 1942.

Today the line is listed in the National Register of Historic Places for its importance to the park’s communications, engineering and administrative history.

Industry observers note that but according to the Park Service, suspended, hanging and fallen telephone wires have created risks to helicopter and emergency operations in the inner canyon. They can also pose entanglement hazards for wildlife.

In a recent development, under the proposal, the park would remove sections of the historic wire where necessary to eliminate those hazards. On the wire it keeps, it would add visibility markers where appropriate.

The historic poles, cross-arms, insulators and other hardware would stay in place, unless one of them presents a safety hazard of its own.

The plan is still a proposal, not finished work. The park took public comments for 15 days, from October 28 until midnight on November 11, 2026, and that comment period has now closed.

The Park Service acknowledges a cost to the historic line. Because the original copper wire is an key historic feature of the line, removing portions of it and adding markers “would alter characteristics that contribute to the property’s historical significance,” the agency remarked.

Under the National Historic Preservation Act, it added, “those changes are considered an adverse effect.”.

To deal with that, the park worked with Arizona’s State Historic Preservation Officer on a draft Memorandum of Agreement.

According to the Park Service, the agreement would let the park address known safety hazards while documenting and preserving the history and significance of the telephone line.

Sources indicate that the telephone line is one of many pieces of the Grand Canyon that date to that era. Hikers on the Bright Angel Trail still use resthouses built by the CCC, and Bright Angel Campground sits on the site of the corps’ camp at Phantom Ranch, the park notes.

Elsewhere in the park, the South Rim hotels reopened 81 days after a deadly flood, which echoed a disaster in 1966.

According to recent reports, the canyon’s rocks hold a far older record. Geologists have found fossils of unexpected creatures in the Grand Canyon’s Bright Angel Formation.

The information was obtained from the National Park Service news release Grand Canyon National Park Seeks Public Comment on Trans-Canyon Telephone Line (October 28, 2026) and the Grand Canyon National Park history pages Building the National Park and Civilian Conservation Corps.

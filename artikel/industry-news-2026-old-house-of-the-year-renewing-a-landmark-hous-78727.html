Fine Homebuilding’s 2026 Old House of the Year is a 1914 home in the San Francisco Bay Area designed by renowned architect Julia Morgan that underwent an extensive restoration led by architect Jerri Holan and owners Ed and Noelle Long. The project preserved the home’s original Arts and Crafts character while addressing years of neglect, and it included structural and seismic improvements, upgraded heating and plumbing systems, carefully replicated historic windows, a redesigned kitchen, and restored woodwork. The renovated house and redesigned Arts and Crafts garden successfully blend historic craftsmanship with modern comfort, ensuring that the home retains its distinctive character for generations to come.

Recent findings suggest that this house in the Berkeley Hills occupies a large lot with views of the San Francisco Bay, the Golden Gate Bridge, and San Francisco. Designed in 1914 by the renowned architect Julia Morgan, the structure of stucco and timbers has aspects of an Alpine chalet. Exuberant exterior details include diamond-pane windows of leaded glass, deep eaves, and a central dormer with a wide, picturesque window box that mimics a balcony. In a recent development, current owners Edward and Noelle Long were moving from the East Coast to the San Francisco Bay Area. Both had lived in historic houses: Ed had restored a 1927 Sears Craftsman bungalow, while Noelle had grown up amid early American Colonials. They hoped their West Coast home would be historic, preferably with an Arts and Crafts aesthetic: casual, open rooms, natural woodwork, and gardens to bring the outdoors in. They patiently looked for nearly two years before finding the George Bell House.

While the structure was mostly intact and the original design easily read, the place had suffered serious neglect and required significant restoration. Twenty-one of the windows were too rotted to salvage, electrical and plumbing systems needed an upgrade, and the roof—covered in asbestos shingles that were more than 50 years old—was uninsurable. The kitchen, while not original to the home, was cramped and dated with spray-painted cabinets, three bathrooms were worn and unappealing, and the basement had rot and leaks. The detached garage was a safety hazard with a cracked foundation, a hole in the roof, and dangerous wiring.

Industry observers note that the couple worked with architect Jerri Holan of Albany, Calif., who specializes in historic restoration. The team committed to not altering the original footprint or details. This thoughtful approach to restoration is why the George Bell House was selected for this year’s Old House Award. The owners and Holan adhered to the Secretary of the Interior’s Standards for the Treatment of Historic Properties (SIS), restoring and replicating original materials and details wherever possible. Newly introduced work, including custom wood windows and a functional kitchen, was executed with respect for the original design intention and period of the house.

According to recent reports, “Berkeley has a strong historic-preservation ordinance,” Holan notes. It requires that any proposed exterior changes to a landmark (or other potentially historic building) follow the SIS guidelines for historic buildings. If any proposed exterior changes are significant, they would need review by the city’s Landmark Preservation Board. Interiors aren’t covered by the ordinance. Holan didn’t propose any changes to the exterior, so the design review passed easily.

Recent findings suggest that the original house was designed with an angled living room, generous porches, and a initiative oriented toward the western views. The restoration preserved the footprint as seen in the original intends (right), while making selective updates: relocating a kitchen door, removing a dropped kitchen ceiling, and restoring the basement as guest space.

Even before Holan came on board, work had begun with bench piers installed to stabilize the foundation. These reinforcing concrete structures were cast against the existing footings to counteract soil pressure and transfer weight to stable, subgrade levels. Seismic upgrades, including anchored girder post footings, were done by a seismic-retrofit contractor.

Roof replacement was another early project, hastened by the owners’ difficulty in getting insurance had they chosen a historically appropriate wood-shingle roof. (The house is in a major fire zone.) They selected high-quality asphalt shingles with a wood look.

Recent findings suggest that fireplace Refresh: The original brick fireplace was elevated to a focal point in the spirit of the Arts and Crafts tradition. The surround now features custom tile and wood trim stained to match the existing trimwork.

In a recent development, A spectrum of HVAC updates came next. The old furnace was replaced with a two-zone, forced-air gas furnace rated at 96.7% efficiency. In the basement, the former maid’s bedroom and bathroom were restored to become guest quarters. To free up space in the basement, a tankless water heater was installed in the crawlspace, with access provided through a hatch next to the shower in the bathroom.

It has been revealed that the team added a laundry room and, below the hearth, a wine cellar. They also stuccoed the garage using a traditional two-coat finish.

Deciding which windows were unsalvageable took into account the SIS call for “repair before replacement,” which is meant to preserve quality of materials and craftsmanship. When something can’t be repaired—a highly controversial notion, Holan interjects, since just about anything can be repaired—the call is for “replacement in kind.” In this case, the 21 single-glazed windows facing west, which had weathered for over a century, were severely damaged.

“Even if they were repaired, repairs would not have lasted,” Holan indicates. Replacement was truly in-kind: A local window maker created exact replicas. “The quality of the wood could not match the old heart redwood, of course,” Holan acknowledges, “but the craftsman selected the best old-growth Douglas fir he could find.”.

In a recent development, the windows were stained to match originals. The significant question was whether to go with double or single glazing. “While the city and the SIS have accepted double glazing as an appropriate substitute replacement material, the Longs opted for single-glazed replacements, which are in character with the originals in the Morgan landmark,” Holan says. She adds, “I think they made the right choice.” All other windows in the home are restored originals.

The George Bell House, built in 1914 in Berkeley, was designed by architect Julia Morgan (1872–1957).

According to recent reports, the first woman licensed to practice architecture in California, Morgan was one of the state’s most celebrated and pioneering architects. She designed over 700 buildings, both residential and commercial, during her long career—the most famous being Hearst Castle at San Simeon, the mansion commissioned by publisher William Randolph Hearst.

Morgan worked in a variety of styles, including the popular First Bay Tradition, unique to the Bay Area, which, like the Arts and Crafts movement, evolved in reaction to the fussiness of Victorian design.

It emphasized the Arts and Crafts tenets of handcraftsmanship, use of natural materials (often redwood), and sensitivity to the outdoors.

The interior of the house had been designed in the First Bay Tradition, an Arts and Crafts vernacular developed for Berkeley Hills homes in the early 20th century (see sidebar, facing page). The style emphasized handcraft and the use of natural materials.

It has been revealed that A century later this craftsmanship is still intact, from the cutout balusters of the open staircase to the Douglas-fir woodwork. An unusual living room set at a 45° angle centers on a rustic sunken hearth. Generous second-floor bedrooms overlook the gardens. Such built-ins as window seats, corner cabinets, and a linen press are charming period conventions from the original design.

The open living room is the heart of the house. A plain brick fireplace, an afterthought during construction, was given presence with a tile mural. Its design and the tiles are by Pasadena Craftsman Tile, a contemporary mother-daughter tech giant working in the tradition of early-20th-century California potteries. The swirling pattern of ginkgo leaves reinforces the Arts and Crafts emphasis on nature motifs.

It has been revealed that the staircase was invisibly strengthened and its woodwork refinished. The largely cosmetic preservation work included refinishing the wainscoting and buffet in the dining room, refinishing some wood in the living room, and restoring the exist-ing flooring.

Sources indicate that the house’s footprint remains the same in the fully renovated kitchen, although an interior door was relocated to improve circulation. A dropped ceiling was removed, adding a foot of height to the room. The oak floor is new. The small center island was inspired by the built-in buffet that is still in the dining room. High-quality fittings are modern but with traditional lines. Holan’s sensitive design for the kitchen relies on natural materials, including marble, soapstone, oak, and copper.

The goal of a sensitive renovation is to make upcoming work feel at home in the original design. These products helped renew the house while honoring its early-20th-century roots and Arts and Crafts character.

Industry observers note that ed and Noelle Long’s commitment went beyond the walls of the home. The landscape was updated from a neglected yard to a picturesque Arts and Crafts garden that anchors the house. The northwest front yard was regraded 31/2 ft. to level it.

Curving paths laid with Tuscany peastone lead from the entry gate into the front garden. Antique brick pavers were laid for walkways and steps to the front and side porches. Off the kitchen, a latest brick terrace has become the spot for morning coffee.

Recent findings suggest that upcoming garden structures include an arbor on the east perimeter, which supports the trunk and tendrils of a century-old Lady Banks rose that climbs 40 ft. into neighboring trees. Colorful perennial beds and over 100 new roses were planted.

According to recent reports, the restoration of the George Bell House resulted in a structure that feels neither frozen in the past nor disconnected from it. Repairs preserved what could be saved, replacements respect what was lost, and fresh work accommodates modern life without competing with the character of the original house. A century after Julia Morgan designed it, the George Bell House remains distinctly of its time—and ready for the next hundred years.

It has been revealed that in today’s world, all professional employees use AI, whether it’s candidates submitting highly polished CVs with 100% perfect fit with your job description to AI-generated work. In this environment, how can we promote the right employees and how do we hire the best canidates when everyone appears to be perfect?

Imagine this: You’re hiring a financial analyst and give two finalists the same AI-generated acquisition memo. It reads beautifully and recommends buying a company at what looks like a reasonable price. There’s one problem. The model counted the same revenue twice. In a recent development, AI has become a significant part of how people apply for jobs and how they do that work. Using AI to police that behavior is a losing game. Employers have more to gain by making AI use visible and designing hiring and performance reviews around what people do with it.

Indeed illustrates both sides of the hiring loop. Its Career Scout offers job seekers résumé help, while its employer tools help review applications against hiring criteria. Both can save time, and résumé assistance can help a qualified applicant explain experience they’ve struggled to put into words.

But it can also make two very different candidates look equally well-suited for a role on paper.

An AI detector adds another score without answering the question the employer cares about: Could this person do the job?

According to recent reports, I’d use AI to help the recruiter prepare a better follow-up. If someone claims they led an integration, ask them to walk through a decision they made when two teams disagreed. The résumé gets you to the question. The answer gives you something to evaluate.

CoderPad offers an interview environment where candidates use AI on realistic tasks while interviewers watch how they prompt, evaluate and revise the output. That gives employers a practical way to observe a skill candidates will increasingly need on the job.

According to recent reports, I’d give every finalist the same flawed acquisition memo, the same source documents and access to the same AI tools. Then I’d ask what they would verify before sending the memo to a client. One might catch the double-counted revenue. Another might go further and work out whether the corrected numbers change the price we should offer. The most revealing moment may come when the tool confidently insists its original calculation was right. What does the candidate do then?

Lattice lets managers use AI to draft reviews from goals, feedback and meeting notes. Synq, formerly Gloat, says its software can build and run a review cycle from a plain-English instruction. These tools make it easier to collect and summarize evidence. That makes the choice of what to collect more consequential.

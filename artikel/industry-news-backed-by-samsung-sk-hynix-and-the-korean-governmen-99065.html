Industry News: Backed by Samsung, SK Hynix, and the Korean government, Rebellions CEO Sunghyun Park wants to prove sovereign AI pays off

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that few corporate rivalries are as fierce as the one between Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix. The world’s leading manufacturer of memory chips and the world’s leading manufacturer of high-bandwidth memory, respectively, are fighting for the title of South Korea’s most valuable company. In one area, though, the two are on the same team: Both back Rebellions, the $2.3 billion chip startup cofounded by Sunghyun Park—a veteran of Intel, SpaceX, and Morgan Stanley—that fronts South Korea’s push for sovereign silicon or AI chips that don’t depend on market leader Nvidia or any other U.S. or Chinese company.

Sources indicate that park’s organization sits at the center of government policy. “AI requires huge capital and huge talent,” he states. “Instead of having multiple players compete, the Korean government stated, ‘We need one champion. SK Hynix, please help him. Samsung, please help him. SK Telecom, KT’—top two data center companies—‘please become Rebellions’ customers.’”. Rebellions may be the clearest test of whether sovereign AI can produce something more than an expensive also-ran. Korea is funneling its best resources toward Rebellions: Samsung’s foundries, SK Hynix’s memory, and government money. The Korea National Growth Fund co-led Rebellions’ $400 million round in March.

Will it pay off? Rebellions eventually wants public investors to back a risky bet on a chip startup in a market that—for now—is skeptical of AI stocks. Still, Park is hopeful. Rebellions’ backers make it “more powerful compared to any other player in the United States,” he notes. But market volatility and a wave of shelved data center IPOs will put his confidence to the test.

Park took the long road to national-champion-in-waiting. A graduate of the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology, he spent his compulsory military service in Afghanistan alongside the U.S. Army, where he learned English. Afterward, he earned a PhD in electrical engineering and computer science at MIT, and worked in chip engineering roles at Intel, SpaceX, and Morgan Stanley in the U.S. before returning to Korea to launch Rebellions. Sources indicate that park and his cofounders—including Jinwook Oh, who designed AI chips at IBM, and Hyo-eun Kim, who developed deep-learning technology at a medical AI startup—founded Rebellions in 2020. It’s a “fabless” chip startup in that it handles only design and contracts manufacturing (or “fabrication”) out to a third party—in this case, Samsung.

In 2024, Rebellions merged with Sapeon, another chip designer backed by the SK Group. Samsung invested in the combined company in 2025.

Rebellions is part of a wave of startups challenging Nvidia by going after inference—what happens when someone uses ChatGPT, Claude, or DeepSeek—rather than training, which builds the models.

In a recent development, inference is “highly cost-sensitive,” claims Rakesh Kumar, a University of Illinois engineering professor and author of The Chip Age: How Semiconductors Became the World’s Most Powerful Resource. A $2 difference in price could be the reason someone chooses OpenAI over Anthropic.

Park, too, is pitching efficiency, rather than pure power. “From the hardware side, we claim only one metric,” he notes. “Tokens per second, per dollar, per watt.”.

Industry observers note that outside the U.S. and China—the clear leaders in the AI race—countries are trying to figure out how they can insulate themselves from the whims of foreign suppliers and governments.

It has been revealed that south Korea may take the idea most seriously. It has run a state-backed Korean-language-model competition since June 2025, and its “AI for All” initiative prepares to give Koreans free local chatbots. Stanford’s Institute for Human-Centered AI counts only three countries with more than a handful of “notable AI models”: the U.S., with 59; China, with 35; and South Korea, with eight.

“Even my mom talks about sovereign AI, [and] she doesn’t know what sovereign AI is,” Park says.

“Elon didn’t just talk.” He went into the manufacturing side of things himself, Park claims of his ex-boss. “There are a lot of books on leadership, but the baseline is this: We need to work hard—whatever the role.”.

Recent findings suggest that the catch is that sovereign AI programs may lead to models and chips that are less capable, less efficient, and more costly than what comes out of the U.S. or China. Such startups can’t take advantage of the same economies of scale as established players and are selling their products to a smaller customer base. “You’re spending all this money with no guarantee of success,” Kumar says. Without Nvidia’s scale, “you might actually have higher costs.”.

Recent findings suggest that rebellions, which counts backers KT and SK Telecom as customers, hopes to list in Seoul next year, though Park says it will depend on market conditions. The government wants a Korean listing first—for “national security”—before a second listing abroad, he says.

Other AI IPOs have gone astray. At least three AI-related companies—Australia’s Firmus and AirTrunk, and SK Hynix’s U.S. unit Solidigm—suspended their IPOs in October, citing market uncertainty. Investors have also punished AI shares amid worries about high debt and massive capital expenditure.

According to recent reports, park is unfazed by market swings—perhaps a benefit of having the Korean government and the country’s two most valuable companies as backers. But he knows that AI firms will eventually have to prove they can turn a profit. He expects the judgment to come by 2029: “Then, the market will test whether these investments are paying off or not.”.

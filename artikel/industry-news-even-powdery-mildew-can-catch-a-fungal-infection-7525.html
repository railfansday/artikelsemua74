Researchers found a fungus that infects powdery mildew in preserved specimens, offering clues to how it might help control the crop disease.

Powdery mildew is usually the one doing the damage. The fungi behind it infect crops such as wheat and grapevines, yet some of those mildews are infected themselves, by a fungus called Ampelomyces that lives inside their cells.

Researchers checked dried mildew specimens up to about 150 years old and found the parasite on mildews from many countries.

The study also found that about 13% of the public DNA records labeled Ampelomyces appear to belong to other fungi.

Michael Bradshaw, an assistant professor of entomology and plant pathology at North Carolina State University, led the research with first author Jacklyn Thomas.

Bradshaw directs the university’s Larry F. Grand Mycological Herbarium.

It has been revealed that he sees the parasite as a possible tool against the disease. Part of the reason is host specificity, meaning some strains infect only certain kinds of mildew. (Related: Latest Update: At least seven progressives voted agains...)

“By understanding hyperparasite diversity and how many different strains are out there, it might give us insights on if some are better at controlling the fungus than others,” Bradshaw said. (Related: Breaking: How to generate text: using different decodin...)

“There is a degree of host specificity to these hyperparasites, and if we are able to delve deeper they may be very useful in containing this plant disease.”.

In a recent development, powdery mildews include more than 900 species. Together they cause disease in more than 10,000 kinds of flowering plants.

Recent findings suggest that powdery mildew fungi need living plant tissue to grow, so scientists can’t grow them in lab cultures. That makes their parasite hard to study as well.

In a recent development, so the team turned to herbaria, collections of preserved plants and fungi. The researchers screened 609 mildew specimens held at North Carolina State and at Harvard University’s Farlow Herbarium.

Sources indicate that the specimens came from 28 countries, from Argentina and Morocco to Japan and Ukraine. Many reached North Carolina as gifts from herbaria in Germany and Japan.

From each specimen, the team scraped a bit of mildew and tested its DNA for the parasite.

The researchers then checked every positive result under a microscope.

Researchers lifted the mildew onto clear tape and found Ampelomyces under a microscope.

Ampelomyces turned up in 40 of the 609 specimens, or 6.6%. The authors wrote that old collections let them detect a parasite that is hard to observe directly.

Some kinds of mildew were infected far more often than others. Sawadaea mildews had the highest rate, at 14.3%.

Podosphaera and Phyllactinia mildews were among the lowest, at 3.2% and 2.7%.

Some of those groups had only a few specimens, the authors wrote, so small samples may explain part of the spread.

Researchers in Serbia and Hungary, examining field samples under a microscope, had found the parasite in about a third of them.

The detection rate in the herbarium specimens was much lower.

Industry observers note that the authors suggested reasons for the gap. Their samples came from many parts of the world, and their DNA tests could miss strains whose genetic code differed from what the tests were built to match.

The team sorted its strains, along with hundreds from public records, by how closely related they were.

Strains from Podosphaera mildews tended to group together, and so did strains from a genus called Arthrocladiella.

Strains from Erysiphe, the largest mildew genus, were scattered across many groups.

The mix of tight groups and scattered strains suggests some Ampelomyces lineages specialize while others infect many kinds of mildew.

Several strains from the herbarium specimens matched no known group and may be fresh lineages.

One specimen of a mildew called Blumeria showed the parasite under the microscope, but its DNA test failed. The authors suggested that Ampelomyces on Blumeria may be a lineage not yet described.

Recent findings suggest that genBank is a public database where scientists share DNA sequences. The team downloaded all 671 sequences filed there as Ampelomyces and placed them in one family tree.

Recent findings suggest that of those sequences, 88 fell outside the main Ampelomyces branch. That is how the team arrived at its estimate that about 13% were probably other fungi.

Even samples from culture collections, which supply living strains to labs, were mislabeled.

One strain listed as Ampelomyces had DNA identical to a fungus called Didymella, though the authors wrote that it may have been contaminated after it left the collection.

Bradshaw mentioned errors like these are a risk whenever researchers rely on large datasets that anyone can add to.

“These parasites are hard to identify, so what happens is someone misidentifies them and enters incorrect data,” he remarked.

“Then someone else sees that wrong data and uses it to classify their own samples, which leads to more wrong data, and it amplifies that way.”.

Industry observers note that the team’s answer is a checked reference tree of 47 confirmed strains, posted to a free online tool called T-BAS. Researchers can upload a sequence and see where it belongs.

There are limits. The family tree rests mostly on one short piece of DNA, and its main Ampelomyces branch was only weakly supported.

More genetic data will help sort out how the strains are related, the authors wrote.

In a recent development, two big questions remain. Are some strains better at fighting the fungus than others?

In a recent development, and did the parasite and the mildew evolve together? Answering the second question will take more samples and whole genomes.

Future research could also expand sampling to more regions and more mildews.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings B.

It's the latest Angel Studios "great man" biography: a paint-by-numbers storybook drama that treats the audience like it's 10 years old.

Sources indicate that angel Studios, in its quest to deliver movies that remind us of The Way America Used To Be, has brought back the “great man” biography — not so much the old-fashioned Hollywood biopic but, rather, movies like “Young Washington” and “Hershey,” the sort of life-of-the-plaster-saint sagas you used to read in the fifth grade. These are history-based tales that are proud in their simplicity, that abolish all shades of gray, that are intended to inspire but also work on the audience because they’re so comforting. It feels good, in a certain super-square regressive way, to watch a movie about the man who popularized the American chocolate bar and was a paragon of philanthropic generosity besides. At “Hershey,” you feel like you’re 10 years old again, which is both a virtue and not. The film is a paint-by-numbers storybook biopic for the chocolate-loving child in us all — or, maybe, for an audience the movie treats as if it needed its hand held. Sources indicate that early on, there’s a scene that gives you that kid-in-a-candy-store feeling, because it’s all about…a kid in a candy store. Milton Hershey, played as a boy by James Kelly, has moved with his stern 19th-century Mennonite family from a dairy farm in Pennsylvania Dutch country to the bustling city of Lancaster. There, he wanders into a candy shop, where he’s welcomed by the owner and chief candy maker, played with a touch of screwball carny fervor by Richard Kind. “There’s no sweeter way to make a living,” he claims, offering the boy a candy called a sugar-plum swirl and then taking him under his wing as an apprentice. Milton learns the art of candy, and it’s not long before the film cuts to Philadelphia in 1882, where the adult Milton now has his own candy store with a handsome green-and-white awning.

According to recent reports, he makes hard candy and yummy taffy, but his specialty is caramel bonbons, which he crafts with a special milk recipe. It’s the milk that gives it that luscious velvet smoothness. Milton has got the goods, and he’s a charismatic candy man besides. Finn Wittock, a veteran of Ryan Murphy series (and also “La La Land” and Mike Nichols’s 2012 stage revival of “Death of a Salesman”), is a likable actor who suggests a cross between Orson Welles and Robert Wagner. He’s got a sonorous elegance and gleaming eyes, but with a user-friendly nice-guy veneer. For Milton, making candy is a mission, but he starts off as a faltering businessman; his shop goes under, and that will happen to him again, as he struggles to find an audience for his goodies. Salvation comes in the form of a British aristocrat (Mark Ashworth) who tastes one of Milton’s caramels and puts in an order for 3,000 of them to be shipped to England for a party. It has been revealed that “Hershey” is a movie in which the hero has obstacles (like his unscrupulous candy-business rival), but no demons or inner conflicts. He’s just like his candies: sweet on the outside and the inside. For a while, caramel is king, but it’s chocolate that’s the future. The film colors in the period when Milton is leveraged up to his eyeballs and still searching for the perfect recipe; he figures out that milk is the key here as well. But the aspect of Milton Hershey the movie is actually most interested in is that he popularized what had formerly been elite candy. The Swiss and British chocolates that inspire him are all based on secret recipes, and they’re so expensive that few can afford them. Hershey wants to make a great candy bar that will sell for five cents, so anybody can buy one. He pays his workers top dollar and gives them shares in the tech giant. He’s a turn-of-the-century Willy Wonka as utopian socialist dreamer.

Recent findings suggest that wittrock makes Hershey appealingly robust in his chocolate passion, and that drew me into the movie. What kept nudging me out of it was how sketchy it is. “Hershey” was directed by Mark Waters, who did lively work in the late ’90s and 2000s (“Mean Girls,” “The House of Yes”), but here he skitters through a script that’s so perfunctory it treats even the invention of the Hershey’s Kiss as just one more oh-and-this-happened incident. The dew-drop-shaped candy first results from a faulty machine, but we never see Milton — or anyone else — rhapsodize about it, sharing with the audience what makes it so special. (They never even mention the silver-foil wrapping.) My own theory of the Hershey’s Kiss is that it’s a magical candy because it’s made of the very same chocolate as a Hershey bar, yet that succulent shape somehow makes it taste different.

Industry observers note that milton meets and marries the feisty Kitty (Alexandra Daddario), who loves him, spars affectionately with him, and inspires him to establish a school for impoverished children. But the desire to move back to his old homestead and build an entire town in Pennsylvania Dutch country — the factory town of Hershey, which will be part workers’ paradise, part Disneyland — is his own. They’re living the dream until Kitty starts to succumb to muscle seizures, and it turns out she has a degenerative illness, progressive paraplegia. The movie refuses to characterize her condition as tragic, but it does use it to inspire what we might call faith-based tears.

According to recent reports, “Hershey” is a movie that makes you hungry for chocolate, and that’s part of its appeal. So is the notion that a legendary American entrepreneur could ever have been this much of a selfless gentleman. Yet the movie simulates your sweet tooth without necessarily satisfying it. Chocolate, as anyone who craves it knows, is no casual delight. There’s something orgasmic about chocolate (yes, even a Hershey bar), but in “Hershey” it’s a strictly civilized pleasure.

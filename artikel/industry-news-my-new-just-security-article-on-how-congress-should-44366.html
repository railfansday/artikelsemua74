Today, Just Security published my article "How Congress Should Constrain Emergency Powers," which outlines an agenda for reforms curbing the dangerous growth of executive emergency powers. Here is an excerpt:.

Emergency powers are special authorities that are supposed to be reserved for extraordinary, unexpected crises. But all too often, U.S. presidents have sought to turn them into blank checks they can use whenever they want. As conservative Supreme Court Justice Neil Gorsuch recently put it, "Everything is an emergency" now. "Through delegations of authority, through emergency legislation of all stripes," he warned, power has increasingly moved "over to the executive branch"—a development he described as "not a recipe for a healthy democracy over the long term." A recent report by the progressive Brennan Center for Justice similarly warns that "presidents of both parties have stretched the meaning of 'emergency' far beyond reason, enabling them to draw from a growing stockpile of statutory authorities with few real safeguards." Both Gorsuch and the Brennan Center are right. (Related: This new chip could slash data center energy waste...)

In a recent development, in his second term, President Donald Trump has engaged in especially grave abuse of emergency powers. But the problem predates his return to power, and is likely to outlast his departure from the political scene. The Democrats' massive victory in the 2026 midterm election gives Congress an opportunity to begin enacting much-needed constraints on executive emergency powers. Some of those authorities should be abolished completely. Others need tighter constraints to ensure they are limited in duration, confined to genuine emergency situations, and subject to strong, nondeferential judicial review enforcing these restrictions. Some of the powers in question arise from the National Emergencies Act of 1976, which has been particularly prone to abuse. But others involve different legislation, such as the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), the Alien Enemies Act, laws governing domestic use of the military, and various statutes delegating tariff authority to the executive.

In a recent development, while Trump's abuses of power can provide an initial impetus for reform, there is room for potential cross-ideological cooperation here. Some of the necessary reforms have previously been advocated by conservative Republicans. Given the significant likelihood that a Democratic president might be elected in 2028, conservatives might want to take the opportunity to constrain a future administration's use of these authorities.

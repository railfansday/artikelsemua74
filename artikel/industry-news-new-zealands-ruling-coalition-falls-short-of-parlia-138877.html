If the ruling bloc cannot reach a deal, the country may have to go back to the polls.

New Zealand's ruling coalition has fallen short of a parliamentary majority by one seat, following a campaign shaped by a sluggish economy and disillusioned voters.

Recent findings suggest that it now needs the support of the centrist Opportunity Party to remain in power. If they cannot reach a deal, the country may have to go back to the polls.

The incumbent National Party led by Prime Minister Christopher Luxon secured 40 out of 130 seats, while its coalition partners ACT and Newly introduced Zealand First took 13 and 12 respectively.

A government led by the centre-left Labour Party is not possible, official results on Friday show, as the bloc would not have enough seats even if Opportunity threw its weight behind it.

Luxon on Friday acknowledged that negotiations "may be challenging" and will take some time.

"Kiwis won't thank us for rushing to an agreement that falls apart or for sending them back to the polls," he told reporters.

Sources indicate that labour leader Chris Hipkins mentioned his party "ran an incredible campaign" but acknowledged that it doesn't have negotiating power despite winning the popular vote. "More New Zealanders voted for change at this election than for the status quo," he said.

Preliminary results on 7 November, election day, had projected that National combined with its coalition partners would have a slim lead over the left-leaning bloc.

But today's official tally indicates it had lost two seats on special votes - cast by citizens who were overseas or outside of their registered electorate on election day - which typically skew left.

In earlier interviews with the BBC, many voters had expressed frustration over spiralling costs of living and high unemployment across the country, with declining faith in their political leadership.

"There's this kind of apathy and a bit of dread," said 21-year-old Imogen Vlutger.

More than half of the country no longer see New Zealand as a land of opportunity, according to a survey by the Helen Clark Foundation, a public policy think tank.

Kiwis have been leaving the country in record numbers, with many of them, including former prime minister Jacinda Arden, moving to Australia.

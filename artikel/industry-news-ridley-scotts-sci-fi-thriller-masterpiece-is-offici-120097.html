Recent findings suggest that ridley Scott is one of those staple filmmakers. The 88-year-old English director has made countless noteworthy films, TV demonstrates, and commercials over the course of his prolific career, dabbling in so many genres that it’s difficult to narrow down the parameters of his work. With the release of this year’s The Dog Stars, Scott has directed 31 feature films. They don’t all share the same characteristics, but broadly, his films are known for huge and immersive sets, battles on an epic scale, and significant and heady concepts.

One of his most famous films, however, is also one of his sparsest. After making his feature debut with the small but well-received The Duelists, Scott delivered his follow-up: Alien, one of the great sci-fi horror films of all time. The 1979 classic gave the space-horror subgenre the Scott treatment, resulting in a modest-budget film that looks gorgeous and frightening, balancing evocative monsters and sets with a quiet restraint no entries in the franchise have matched since. With Alien Netflix on Dec. 1, there's never been a better time to rewatch it.

Set in an unspecified future, Alien follows the Nostromo, a towing ship hauling resources back to Earth. The ship’s crew is made up of workers struggling to make ends meet, hoping to wrap up the job and return home to cash in their paychecks. They awaken from hypersleep after the ship’s computer, Mother, receives a signal from a nearby moon, tasking them with exploring it. Three members of the crew set out to investigate, arriving in an environment that’s hostile from the get-go and quickly get caught in a storm that damages the ship.

The explorers find an alien ship on the moon, filled with strange sights, among them a giant fossilized alien and a nest of large eggs. While walking among them, one of the eggs bursts, with a creature latching onto Kane (John Hurt), the ship’s second-in-command. He’s hauled back to the ship, foreign attachment and all, with the crew deliberating over what to do before ultimately bringing a predatory alien creature on board. The rest is history. Before too long, the remaining survivors are being tormented by the Xenomorph, one of the greatest cinematic creations in movie history.

Recent findings suggest that alien highlights the creature, designed by the Swiss artist H.R. Giger, in its various stages. We first see it as an egg, then a facehugger, a chestburster, and finally as an adult. Despite the painstaking design, Scott treated his monster like the shark in Jaws, knowing that the less the creature is seen and the more it stalks its prey, the scarier it becomes. Throughout Alien, the Xenomorph is filmed in snippets, showing its tail, its dripping jaw, and its terrifying human-like teeth. The monster’s success has inspired four direct sequels, two prequels, two crossovers, and a really good TV show.

But before finding all of this success, Alien was simply a very efficient movie, keeping the audience engaged with its patient approach to storytelling and the slow unveiling of the horror at its core. While it’s a story about an unbeatable monster stalking a terrified group of people, Scott and screenwriter Dan O’Bannon establish the world through details. The crew of the Nostromo is not on a mission to meet new species and explore planets; it’s not a crew full of optimistic heroes out to save the world. We never see Earth or any sort of civilization, but two of the crew’s engineers spend much of their story discussing each other’s bonuses. Sources indicate that the film also takes its time in revealing who the lead character is. Two of the crew’s central figures are Dallas (Tom Skerritt), the ship’s captain, and Kane, an executive officer on the ship, introduced as one of the film’s protagonists. Kane is also the first to die. By the time Ripley (Sigourney Weaver) comes into the picture, she’s simply one of the survivors, and in command because there’s no one else to take the job. Her first major scene shows her following the rules, refusing to let Kane back on the ship since he’s a hazard to the rest of the crew. (She loses that debate.) In a recent development, “I feel that one of the reasons Ripley endures as someone who even can inspire me if I need a little encouragement is that she is an ‘everyperson.’ She’s a human character,” Weaver told Empire last year. Ripley barely survives the Xenomorph, yet she establishes herself as a key figure in the Alien universe, as significant to the franchise as the monster itself.

According to recent reports, everyone has a favorite Alien movie, whether it's James Cameron’s bombastic action extravaganza Aliens, David Fincher’s contemplative and maligned Aliens 3, or even Ridley Scott's divisive prequel Prometheus. Still, there’s something about the claustrophobic horror of the original that no follow-up has ever managed to replicate. Scott’s restraint and attention to detail planted the seeds that the franchise is still reaping to this day.

Sources indicate that "I did the best one by far, full stop. End of fucking story,” Scott told Brut this year.

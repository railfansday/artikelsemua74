📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Offer seller financing on your house only if it’s paid off or your mortgage lender agrees in writing. You become the buyer’s lender, so protect yourself the way a bank would, with a credit and income check, at least 10% down, a real estate attorney to draft the note, and a loan servicing manufacturer to collect it.

Recent findings suggest that lending to the buyer opens your house to people a bank won’t approve, which can sell it faster or for more, and pays you interest. It also leaves you with the lender’s risk that the payments stop. (Related: Breaking: Block Sparse Matrices for Smaller and Faster ...)

It has been revealed that the buyer signs a promissory note, an IOU setting the rate, the term, and any balloon, the lump sum due at the end. A mortgage or deed of trust recorded in your favor gives you the right to foreclose if they default.

Recent findings suggest that federal rules for loan originators, the professionals who arrange mortgages, don’t apply to a homeowner who finances only one sale in any 12-month period. A balloon doesn’t change that, as long as the payments never let the balance grow and the rate is fixed, or adjusts only after five years, on the rules’ terms. If you still owe on the house, check your mortgage for a due-on-sale clause, which lets the lender demand the full balance if you sell without first getting its written consent.

Before you close, put these protections in place.

In a recent development, say you sell a paid-off $250,000 house with $25,000 down and finance the other $225,000 at 7%, with payments set as if the loan ran 30 years and the balance due after five. You’d collect about $1,497 a month, then about $212,000 when the buyer refinances to pay the balloon.

Until then, most of your sale price stays tied up in the loan. The interest is taxable income, and any taxable profit on the sale can be reported as the payments arrive. Offer financing only with money you won’t need until the note is paid off.

Achille the bull and Patroclo, a male donkey, mated and groomed each other for weeks in an Ethiopian pasture, forming a very unusual and mutual bond.

In a recent development, on a beautiful plateau in Ethiopia, a bull named Achille spent his days in a pasture full of female cows and other bulls. Yet over two weeks in March, researchers watched him ignore the females, and his own species, to mount a male donkey named Patroclo. This happened not once, not twice, but at least 51 times.

The researchers concluded that the pair shared a selective, mutual bond, and that the mounting was part of it.

Elisabetta Palagi, an associate professor of biology at the University of Pisa in Italy, and her colleagues described the pair in a two-page report.

It has been revealed that martina Francesconi and André da Cruz, both PhD students at Pisa, are the lead authors. Da Cruz is also affiliated with CRIA, an anthropology research center in Lisbon.

Their report focuses on this one bull and one donkey, who they watched and studied very closely for eight days.

Sources indicate that in an interview with Earth.com, Palagi noted the team discovered the pair unexpectedly. They were in Ethiopia studying geladas, a kind of monkey, for a University of Pisa project called BRIDGES.

“It is a lovely reminder that careful field observation can reveal questions we had never thought to ask,” she said.

In a recent development, the pasture is in Debre Libanos, in Ethiopia’s Oromia region. Achille and Patroclo belonged to different owners. They met only during the day, when cattle and donkeys shared the pasture in mixed herds.

At night, Achille was housed with another adult bull, a cow, and a calf. Patroclo was housed with two other male donkeys.

Both animals had other possible partners in the same pasture. On any given day, there were three to six bulls and three to seven cows. There were also three male donkeys and two to seven female donkeys.

When the bull and the donkey kept interacting, Palagi noted, the team began documenting the pair systematically.

It has been revealed that the researchers recorded how Achille and Patroclo behaved toward each other and toward the rest of the herd. They kept recording even when nothing sexual was happening.

In all, the team saw the two together on four days and apart on four more.

Recent findings suggest that thirty-four of the 51 mounts included penetration. The researchers saw no avoidance and no aggression between the two animals.

It has been revealed that asked what surprised her most, Palagi named two things. One was how selective the two animals were about each other. The other was how actively Patroclo took part.

“What initially looked like an unusual encounter turned out to be a persistent pattern of preferential interaction,” Palagi told Earth.com.

Recent findings suggest that patroclo often presented his hindquarters to Achille and nipped him. Twice, another bull tried to come near Patroclo. Patroclo chased and bit him.

It has been revealed that the two animals also licked, rubbed, and nuzzled each other. The team looked closely at the six seconds or so around each mount.

Recent findings suggest that in about a quarter of the mounts, that friendly contact happened just before. In nearly three-quarters, it happened just after.

Contact before a mount isn’t a surprise, the authors wrote, because it may make mounting easier. Contact afterward is harder to explain. It appears to keep the social interaction going after sex is over.

Palagi said Achille and Patroclo had friendly contact with each other more often than with other animals, and it lasted longer.

The two also stayed close. When both were in the pasture, they spent more time near each other than apart.

Achille approached and mounted another male donkey only twice. That donkey avoided him and flattened its ears, a threatening expression.

According to recent reports, when Achille tried to mount the other donkey, Patroclo moved toward Achille. Achille immediately stopped and directed his friendly behavior at Patroclo instead.

According to recent reports, the researchers also analyzed Patroclo’s facial expressions using a coding system developed for horses. They compared four situations: Achille mounting him, Achille making friendly contact, Achille standing close by, and Patroclo alone.

Across all four, Patroclo’s face highlighted no detectable difference. To the team, that suggests Patroclo didn’t see Achille as a threat.

Achille courted cows the way bulls usually do, but he never tried to mount one. The other bulls nearby may have discouraged him, the researchers wrote.

After similar courtship with Patroclo, Achille did mount him.

The one time another bull tried to mount Achille, Achille rejected him. Among the rest of the herd, the team recorded no mounting between cattle and donkeys during the study.

Same-sex sexual behavior is widespread in animals. It has been documented in several species of the horse family.

But the team couldn’t find a published report of such behavior in donkeys. They also found no report of a lasting sexual relationship between domestic hoofed animals of two different species.

Classic explanations for same-sex sexual behavior include one animal forcing another, mistaken identity, and a shortage of opposite-sex partners.

The authors wrote that none of these seems to fully explain Achille and Patroclo. Their relationship was selective and mutual.

In a recent development, A bond like this must also come at a cost. The two spent time and energy on each other that could have gone into producing offspring. To the authors, the strength of the bond seems to outweigh that cost.

“Their relationship reminds us that sexual behavior can be part of a social bond beyond reproduction, and that these bonds can cross species boundaries,” Palagi mentioned.

According to recent reports, nobody knows yet what the bond does for either animal. The behavior may benefit one or both of them directly. Or it may be a result of the way cattle and donkeys form social bonds.

Palagi said the team can’t tell whether sex helped keep the bond going or came out of an attachment the two animals already had.

In a recent development, domestication may have played a part in the behavior too. The authors wrote that it changes animals’ social settings and their chances to interact with other species.

The next step, Palagi said, is to find out how often relationships like this one occur and to follow them over time.

In a recent development, she suggested comparing friendly behavior and signs of tension before and after sex. That could help test whether sex is linked to keeping a relationship going or to easing tension.

The team would also need to observe different pairs in different social settings to tell those two possibilities apart, she stated.

The full study was published in the journal Current Biology.

Industry observers note that hannah covers horror, sci-fi, and games at Collider, where she writes news and features, reviews fresh releases, and interviews the people who make them. She’s helping build out Collider’s gaming coverage, with Pokémon as one of her specialties. If she isn’t writing about a monster, she’s probably watching one on a tape from her VHS collection.

It has been revealed that of all the things I expected to worry about while reading a Christmas slasher set in the Spawn Universe, whether Santa Claus could get a 911 dispatcher to stay on the phone was pretty far down the list. In I Saw Santa, Nicholas finds the Anderson family murdered on Christmas Eve and calls for help. When the dispatcher asks him to say something if he can hear her, he tries to give her the family’s name. From her end, the line is quiet. From his, he gets to hear a perfectly reasonable woman explain why she’s about to hang up on him. It’s a hell of a thing to do to a guy who still has four time zones’ worth of presents to deliver. In a recent development, when I spoke with Todd McFarlane, Chris Killian, and Andrew Sanders for Collider about I Saw Santa and its sequel, I Saw Spawn #1, Killian explained that the call came out of a question he and Sanders had to answer. “If he knows who the killer is, why is he not warning anybody through all this?” Nicholas does warn people. He writes to the local authorities, contacts Interpol, and even tries the Vatican. His problem is that most adults know exactly what to do with a letter to Santa and have every reason to be suspicious of one that comes from him. Getting on the phone ought to improve his chances, except Killian and Sanders took one familiar piece of Christmas lore at its word. As Killian put it, “If you don’t believe in him, you can’t see him, you can’t hear him. And how horrifying is that for him?”. (Related: Latest Update: “Giant superatoms” could finally solve q...)

What gives the 911 call its bite is that the dispatcher is probably pretty good at her job, but the Andersons are no closer to getting help. Artist Thomas Nachlik lets us follow both sides of the conversation on the page: we can read the name Santa gives her, so when she asks him to speak up, there’s a moment where it feels as though all he has to do is say it again. That little bit of hope is worth preserving for as long as she stays on the line. McFarlane brought up John Carpenter’s The Thing when he described how a horror story can make even the prospect of rescue work against its characters. Sometimes, he said, “the cavalry is not coming.”.

"Or if it is, it’s a half an hour away.”.

According to recent reports, mcFarlane points out that most horror stories tell out that half an hour. Here, Santa gets the length of a phone call, and that was where McFarlane saw another Carpenter movie in Nicholas’ predicament. Killian and Sanders had found something that “basically isolates this horror and puts Santa on his own,” he explained. Once that happens, “now he just becomes Jamie Lee Curtis. Now what do I do?” It’s a fair question to ask of a man whose job requires him to keep visiting houses on the very night a killer knows where to find him. When I suggested they’d made Santa the final girl, Killian laughed: “That was exactly what we did.”.

The nastiest part of the entire thing is that Charlie Kohl is one of the few people (who isn't a child) that still believes. He stayed up hoping to catch Santa coming down the chimney and saved everything Nicholas ever brought him. Unfortunately for Santa, what he brought Charlie was coal, and the boy has been looking forward to giving every piece of it back. Killian described the coal as something Nicholas hopes will “help bad children sort of find their way out of the dark.” But this case and probably several others those intentions have been misinterpreted, and Charlie has had years to sit with the knowledge that somebody saw what he did, judged him for it, and would be back at his house next Christmas Eve. Of all the people Santa needs to convince he exists, the one who's kept the receipts is the one he has reason to fear.

Four franchises, four completely different ways of getting underneath your skin. This is the one built out of your particular fear, why it works so well on you, and what it would leave behind.

Sources indicate that your nightmare does not wait outside the door. It waits for you to close your eyes, which you are going to have to do eventually, because nobody has ever won that particular argument.

Your nightmare is not really the doll. It is the face of the person you tell, and the way they decide, very kindly, that you have imagined all of it.

Industry observers note that your nightmare has your number, knows your name, and has almost certainly been inside your house before. The worst part is that you already know them.

Your nightmare is not really about you. It is about the people asleep down the hall, and the feeling that whatever is here is not interested in leaving.

Industry observers note that krampus has always been more my speed at Christmas, so one of the pleasures of I Saw Spawn is discovering that he has apparently spent years nursing a grievance over the direction of the family business. In Killian and Sanders’ version of Christmas, Krampus and Santa are brothers, and Nicholas made a fairly consequential decision about what the holiday ought to look like without securing his brother’s vote. As Killian put it, “I think Krampus is actually a little hurt by it," which is understandable considering he's been locked away while his brother went on to become one of the most beloved figures in the world.

Recent findings suggest that what sells their falling-out is that I Saw Santa has already given Krampus an infuriatingly good example to cite. Nicholas spent years leaving coal for Charlie Kohl in the hope that the boy would turn his life around: imagine how that looks to the brother who used to be in charge of dealing with naughty children. Killian had a pretty good handle on the argument Krampus would make in his own defense.

“'Because if I’d been around, you know, eating naughty kids, then this maybe wouldn’t have been a problem.'”.

Sources indicate that somewhere in the middle of a perfectly reasonable objection to eating children, Santa now has to concede that his brother has a point about Charlie. That’s a far better use of Krampus than bringing him out at Christmas to give somebody a scare, and it’s also where I Saw Spawn feels most like a sequel worth picking up on its own. The first comic lets us see why Nicholas keeps giving Charlie another chance, and the second gives us somebody who’s known Nicholas long enough to be thoroughly unimpressed by that quality in him. Even the sight of the brothers together raises a question about who got to tell the family story. Killian liked that “Santa looks like a normal guy, and then Krampus is this big giant,” because “there’s something else going on here if these two are related.” That sent me down a rabbit hole during the interview. If these two share a family resemblance, who decided Santa was the normal-looking one? Presumably the same brother who got to keep the holiday, but exploring that is for another series.

By the time Nicholas sends Alberich to find Al Simmons in I Saw Spawn, the elf is following up on a letter Spawn has already read and dismissed. From Santa’s end, he recognizes that now somebody has to deliver the message in person, which is how a centuries-old elf ends up clinging to Spawn’s cape on a rooftop in Rat City. Al’s first reaction, as McFarlane put it, is “Is this a con? Is this a trick? Who’s trying to get me?” Given how often somebody from Hell actually is trying to get him, Al has probably saved his own skin a few times by asking those very questions. But here, it's him who needs to do the saving.

Recent findings suggest that asked what makes Spawn the man to call when Christmas is in trouble, Killian’s first answer was “the last resort.” Then he added something considerably more tender: “Santa knows all the children.” That includes Al Simmons, a boy Nicholas can remember and a grown man who could introduce himself on the street and be told to his face that Al Simmons is dead. McFarlane saw the appeal for Santa immediately. “That dude understands people not trusting the truth, right?” He also saw the catch:.

“The problem is he’s now not gonna trust me.”.

That’s my favorite reason to put these two on a rooftop together, even if Santa has to send an elf in his place. Nicholas has spent Christmas Eve asking people to believe something extraordinary about him. With Al, he can finally return the favor. Long before there was a Hellspawn in Rat City, there was a kid whose house Santa knew how to find. After spending an entire comic hoping somebody would pick up the phone for Santa Claus, I’ve rarely wanted a man to open his mail so badly.

I Saw Spawn #1 is available now, while #2 arrives December 16 from Image Comics.

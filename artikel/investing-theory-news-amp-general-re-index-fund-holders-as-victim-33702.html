Recent findings suggest that I find it surprising that so many BH members — including some who have been here for years — would favor an approach that intentionally excludes newly public companies until they meet S&P’s rules.The arguments are usually that these companies are too small to matter, not yet profitable, overhyped, or not seasoned enough.I’m saying all of that except too small to matter. The point is that historically those overvalued companies used to join the indexes at positions too small to matter, and that was a good thing. They needed to work their way up to higher weights. If they really were bad investments, they’d fall away before they did much damage to index holders. Now, these companies are staying private much longer and becoming truly enormous before going public. All while playing a different game than public firms of similar size when it comes to actual business profits.But how do you know those companies are overvalued? Isn't that precisely the kind of judgment that passive investing tries to avoid?A manufacturer being unprofitable, newly public, or highly valued doesn't necessarily mean it's overvalued. The market may be wrong about its prospects, but it may also be right.I agree that companies staying private longer has changed the IPO landscape. But deciding that certain companies are overvalued and should be excluded until they prove themselves sounds much more like an active investment decision than a passive indexing principle.Statistics: Posted by babystep — Sat Nov 21, 2026 12:48 am.

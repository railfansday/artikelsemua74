Is Goalscoring a Problem for Mikel Arteta’s Arsenal?

Probably the strangest thing about the debate over Arsenal’s attacking power is that it exists at all.

It has been revealed that on the surface, the Premier League champions appear to be moving in the right direction. Their football is more fluid than it was a year ago, their positional rotations are more ambitious and they have become less dependent on set-pieces as a source of goals. Yet as Manchester City arrive at the Emirates Stadium on Sunday, there is a growing sense that Arsenal’s attack remains their one area of vulnerability. That feeling is not driven solely by statistics. It is driven by matches. Arsenal continue to control games, create pressure and dominate territory, but too often they fail to establish the kind of leads that allow them to relax. The result is a team that wins regularly but leaves itself with very little margin for error.

The arrival of Viktor Gyokeres in 2025 was supposed to address a long-standing concern. Arsenal wanted a recognized centre-forward capable of turning dominance into goals and relieving some of the burden on the rest of the team.

His record has not been poor. Fourteen goals last season made him Arsenal’s leading scorer, but there has remained a sense that the club are still looking for another level in attack. According to recent reports, those pursuits revealed something vital about Arsenal’s thinking. Arteta is no longer looking for squad depth or developmental prospects. He wants a world-class attacker capable of deciding the biggest matches and carrying the attack through difficult periods.

The difficulty is that such players are rarely available, particularly in January.

Sources indicate that arteta has spent much of the past year trying to make the team less predictable. Defenders now appear in advanced positions. Midfielders rotate into wide areas. Attackers exchange roles with greater frequency than before. The movement has become more dynamic and considerably harder to defend against.

That evolution can be seen throughout the side. Piero Hincapie and Riccardo Calafiori frequently appear in positions normally reserved for attackers. Full-backs move into central areas. Wingers drift inside. Arsenal’s attacking patterns are far less rigid than they were during previous seasons.

The trade-off is that this style demands excellent decision-making in the final third. When those decisions are slightly off, Arsenal can dominate possession and territory without fully translating that superiority into goals.

That has happened often enough this season to become a concern.

No team loses a player of Bukayo Saka’s quality without consequences.

Recent findings suggest that for years, Arsenal’s attack has benefited from having a player capable of creating and finishing chances at elite levels. His injury has inevitably reduced the threat they carry, particularly in moments when matches require individual inspiration.

The larger concern is that several attacking players have not fully compensated for his absence. There have been signs of improvement from Noni Madueke and Eberechi Eze, while Kai Havertz continues to deliver goals in crucial matches. Yet none of them has consistently provided the level of output that allows Arsenal to overwhelm opponents.

That is why so many of their victories remain narrow despite long periods of superiority.

In a recent development, arsenal trust their defence enough to protect one-goal leads, and that confidence is understandable given their recent record. Yet continually operating with such small margins creates risks – the picture at the top of the Premier League table would’ve been far more interesting at this point had Liverpool been given the penalty that should’ve been awarded for Jurrien Timber’s shirt-pull on Alexander Isak, for example.

The timing of this discussion is significant. Manchester City visit this weekend. Trips to Brentford and Tottenham follow shortly afterwards. Then comes a Champions League meeting with Real Madrid. These are precisely the fixtures in which elite attacking players justify their reputations.

In a recent development, arteta has spoken optimistically about the chances his team continue to create, arguing that improved efficiency will naturally follow if the Gunners maintain their current level of performance. There is logic to that view. Teams that consistently generate opportunities tend to score eventually.

The question is whether Arsenal can afford to wait.

Recent findings suggest that because while their football has evolved positively, they are still encountering the same problem that has lingered around the club for several seasons. When matches become tight and opportunities are limited, there remains a feeling that Arsenal do not possess quite enough goals across the squad.

Recent findings suggest that few managers have transformed a club more successfully than Arteta. He has rebuilt Arsenal’s culture, restored their competitiveness and ultimately delivered the Premier League title. His latest tactical evolution has made the team more flexible and more entertaining to watch.

Yet the challenge facing him now may be the most difficult one of all.

The gap between a very good attack and a truly great one is often filled by players who are almost impossible to sign. Arsenal appear to have reached the point where collective improvements alone may not be enough. The structure is strong, the football is intelligent and the squad is packed with quality.

What they may still need is the sort of forward capable of turning twenty good minutes into a three-goal lead.

In a recent development, until they find that player, questions about Arsenal’s firepower are unlikely to disappear entirely, irrespective of their lead in the Premier League title race. The encouraging aspect for Arteta is that they remain champions despite those concerns. The worrying aspect is that the teams chasing them know exactly where the vulnerability lies.

Ivory Coast’s SICA Market Looks to Turn Africa’s Creative Talent Into Economic Opportunity

The fourth edition of Ivory Coast’s International Salon for Audiovisual Content of Abidjan (SICA), running Nov. 26–28, seeks to harness Africa’s wealth of creative talent while addressing the structural challenges of building a sustainable audiovisual industry across the continent. “We have gradually moved from a simple gathering of professionals promoting their content to a real platform for reflection, cooperation and the creation of economic opportunities,” Ivorian Minister of Communication Amadou Coulibaly, the driving force behind the annual event, tells Variety. “We have managed to give our creations, which were previously viewed solely from a cultural perspective, real economic value.”.

A memorandum of understanding set to be signed between the public Media Support and Development Agency and Ivorian commercial bank NSIA Banque during SICA, aims to address access to financing, one of the sector’s biggest obstacles. “Our young people are talented, they have a lot of ideas, but there are no resources to finance those ideas,”Coulibaly notes.

Sources indicate that the initiative will give producers access to grants or bank loans backed by state guarantees. To finance it, the ministries of communication and culture are pooling around two billion CFA francs ($3.34 million), with a further 500 million to one billion CFA francs ($830,000–$1.67 million) anticipated from the country’s youth employment agency. Coulibaly estimates that financial leverage could generate between five billion and six billion CFA francs ($8.3 million–$10 million) in guarantees.

In a recent development, “We don’t want it to stop at the national level,” claims Coulibaly, whose team has initiated discussions with the African Export-Import Bank (Afreximbank). “We want this mechanism, which we are going to test in Ivory Coast, to be extended across the continent if it works.” The pilot phase is set to begin in 2027.

Ivory Coast’s 2027 finance bill, adopted by the National Assembly and awaiting Senate approval, includes tax and customs incentives for equipment for film production and for the construction of latest cinemas. A tax credit to encourage filming in the country is also in development.

Recent findings suggest that coulibaly draws a parallel with Ivory Coast’s position as a hub for Francophone African music in the 1980s and ’90s, attracting artists such as Salif Keïta and Manu Dibango. “We want Ivory Coast to regain, in cinema and audiovisual, the position it held in the ’80s and ’90s with music,” he states.

Previous editions of SICA have already yielded tangible results. Franco-American producer Alex Berger brought his Arte series “Eldorado” to Ivory Coast after attending SICA for the first time, employing a crew that was nearly 75% Ivorian, according to Coulibaly.

The minister also reveals that American producer Larry Kasanoff, a keynote speaker last year, is developing a production with an estimated budget of $30 million–$35 million, with the bulk of filming expected to take place in Ivory Coast.

Recent findings suggest that luc Gnépa’s “Ebinto,” an adaptation of Amadou Koné’s classic Ivorian novel, has recorded more than 50,000 admissions across Ivory Coast, Senegal and France, including a sold-out premiere at Paris’ Grand Rex. It marks the first feature film co-production for Ivorian broadcaster NCI’s production arm, NCI Création, the fruit of a meeting between the broadcaster and the film’s producer, Charly Kodjo, at SICA.

“SICA is the framework that has allowed all these projects to be born and to develop,” he explains.

Industry observers note that released last year, the SICA Business Hub connects audiovisual creators with potential investors, co-producers, broadcasters and distributors. One of its first beneficiaries, “Naïké,” an Ivorian-Congolese co-production directed and produced by Viateur Yapo and Amida Balega, secured financing after winning SICA’s pitching competition. Shot in both countries with an Ivorian and Congolese cast and an original score by Congolese musician Lokwa Kanza, the film has an estimated budget of 350 million CFA francs ($580,000) and will receive a private screening during this year’s salon.

According to recent reports, infrastructure and training are also priorities. According to the European Audiovisual Observatory’s FOCUS 2026 report, Ivory Coast, a country of 32 million people, had just 15 cinema screens in 2024.

Alongside the recently opened studio by Canal+, Mauritanian filmmaker Abderrahmane Sissako (“Timbuktu”), a SICA mainstay, is developing Treich Studio Academy, a complex housing studios, incubators, screening rooms and technical training facilities.

Ivorian actor Isaach de Bankolé (“The Limits of Control”) rolled out his acting school initiative, Premier Continent, this October, with Cannes Film Festival head Thierry Frémaux attending the inauguration and pledging the festival’s support.

In a recent development, “These are training centers with a pan-African vocation,” Coulibaly stresses. “They are not just for Ivory Coast, they are open to the world.”.

Industry observers note that the Ivorian government is also working with local free-to-air television channels to support domestic productions, guaranteeing an initial broadcast for projects backed by these channels. An initial round of negotiations has brought broadcasters’ exclusivity periods down from at least five years to three, with discussions underway to reduce them further, allowing producers to sell their work to other broadcasters sooner.

In a recent development, content monetization on digital platforms remains another challenge, with limited opportunities for creators to earn revenue in many African countries. According to Coulibaly, discussions with Meta to address the matter are at an advanced stage, with prepares to open similar negotiations with YouTube. “Our producers must be able to benefit from the revenue generated when their works are broadcast on these platforms,” he insists.

In a recent development, international cooperation remains central to SICA’s ambitions. Alongside screenings and masterclasses from Japan, this year’s country of honor, the salon is preparing cooperation agreements with Cameroon’s International Audiovisual Salon (SINAC) and Senegal’s International African Media Salon (SIMA), with further discussions involving Cairo and Red Sea film festivals underway.

Coulibaly also wants to transform SICA into a year-round platform, with sessions dedicated to financing, co-production, intellectual property rights and contract negotiations, while developing the Business Hub into a fully fledged audiovisual market over the next five years.

“We can’t do all of this in three days,” he states. “There will be different sessions throughout the year, but always under the umbrella of SICA.”.

“We have stories to tell the world,” Coulibaly adds. “It is key that we take ownership of our own stories, that we manage to build our own narrative, translate it into films and share it with the world.”.

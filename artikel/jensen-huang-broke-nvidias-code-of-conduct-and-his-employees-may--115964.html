Jensen Huang broke Nvidia’s code of conduct, and his employees may not forget it

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

When Jensen Huang gave his first all-hands address since returning as CEO, there was an odd emphasis on Nvidia’s code of conduct. He admitted to violating the code, then emphasized that no one was exempt from it. But that “does not mean, the moment you violate a code of conduct, we walk you out the door,” Huang stated, according to Wired. “Facts, circumstances, a lot of assessments” still matter. In other words, the code applies to everyone, but breaking it doesn’t automatically cost anyone their job, including, apparently, the CEO.

It’s an awkward stance for a company whose CEO has admitted to breaking its rules. A central part of modern human resources, a code of conduct can seem like a quasi-legal rulebook that lays out unacceptable behavior. But experts say most codes are better understood as a reflection of corporate culture. When an infraction goes unpunished, the main risk isn’t legal but cultural — that employees will feel embarrassed or even angry about the company they work for. That’s the risk Huang now faces at Nvidia.

It has been revealed that matt Plass, chief market strategy officer at LRN, an ethics and compliance training outfit, recently surveyed more than 2,000 workers across different industries for a study on workplace codes of conduct. As Plass sees it, the biggest risk is that employees will become ashamed of their workplace. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

“People coming into the workforce now, ethics is incredibly crucial to them,” he told TechCrunch. “They’re really mindful of the ethical standards of their company. They’re really embarrassed and angry when senior leadership lets them down.”. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

It has been revealed that the consequences of violating the rules aren’t always tangible, but they’re definitely real. Plass gave one example of a organization that banned corporate travel to cut costs — only to have its CEO turn up in a VIP box at the Olympics.

Industry observers note that “For over a decade afterwards, ‘the Olympics’ was used within that company as an example of the worst thing an employee could do. People would say, ‘Yeah, I shouldn’t have done that, but it’s not like I went to the Olympics.’”.

Sources indicate that plass indicates companies that remove an executive typically do so after an investigation, then try to win back employees. “In most organizations they make a change at the top based on what’s happened,” he stated, “and then they will have a hearts-and-minds initiative, to go out to their employee base and their customers and their investors and everybody else, and say, we get it. We’re sorry. We’ve changed. That’s essentially what they do.”.

Of course, that playbook is harder to follow at a company like Nvidia, where Huang left without a clear succession proposal, and there are serious doubts about whether the company could continue to thrive without him. In cases like that, Plass acknowledges there’s no easy answer.

“I would draw a distinction there between the kind of companies where the CEO is a kind of godhead figure … where the person and the company are almost impossible to separate,” he noted. “There it’s much, much harder to deal with.”.

Recent findings suggest that in that context, Huang’s stutter-step over the code of conduct is easier to understand. Judging by the employee campaign for his reinstatement, Huang’s return hasn’t created a morale problem so far. But as the coming months put more pressure on Huang and his colleagues, sidestepping the code could end up costing Nvidia something harder to measure — its employees’ pride in the manufacturer.

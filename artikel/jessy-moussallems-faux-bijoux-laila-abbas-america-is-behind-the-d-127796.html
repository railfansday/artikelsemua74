Jessy Moussallem’s “Faux Bijoux” and Laila Abbas’ “America Is Behind the Door” claimed the top post-production and development prizes, respectively, at this year’s Atlas Workshops, the industry strand running Nov. 22 – 26 as part of the Marrakech Film Festival.

Industry observers note that the winner of a post-production grant in the amount of €30,000 ($33,000), Moussallem’s debut feature is an expansion of the short film of the same name, which screened at Sundance and Clermont-Ferrand. It follows the members of a family who are caught up in debts and schemes that drag them into a vicious cycle and test their unity. With principal photography underway, the Lebanese filmmaker couldn’t attend the Atlas Workshops. In a director’s statement, she wrote: “‘Faux Bijoux’ was born from a question rooted in my family and in Lebanon: Why is truth sometimes too difficult to speak? Behind our obsession with appearances lies a struggle for dignity, and a desire to protect those we love.” She added: “The film explores how love survives unspoken truths, and how lying can become its only possible language.”. The post-production jury of festival programmer and distributor Eddie Bertozzi (Be Water Film), filmmaker Mohamed Kordofani (“Goodbye Julia”) and artistic director Kristy Matheson (BFI Festivals) awarded the second prize of €10,000 ($11,000) to two films: “Hamlet From the Slums,” by Ahmed Fawzi-Saleh, and “Occupational Hazards,” by Bassel Ghandour.

Following his 2018 Rotterdam premiere “Poisonous Roses,” Egyptian filmmaker Fawzi-Saleh’s sophomore feature follows Ahmed, a restless and stifled young man who lives under the strict rule of his widower father, the self-proclaimed “King of the Junkyard.” When a revelation sets off a chain of events no one can control, Ahmed is drawn into a search for the truth that will cost him everything.

Jordanian writer, director and producer Ghandour wrote and produced the Academy Award-nominated film “Theeb.” His directorial debut, “The Alleys,” premiered at Locarno’s Piazza Grande. Set in Jerusalem, “Occupational Hazards” follows a Palestinian family that fights to keep their home from crumbling, both literally and figuratively, as their struggle against the system takes on wild and unorthodox forms.

Recent findings suggest that the third prize, in the amount of €10,000 ($11,000), went to “My Father Killed Bourguiba,” by Fatma Riahi. The latest from the Tunisian director, whose debut, “A Haunted Past,” premiered at IDFA in 2018, is a deeply personal journey into the story of the filmmaker’s father, who was part of a failed coup attempt against Tunisia’s first president, Habib Bourguiba.

Development jurors Nadim Cheikhrouha (Mime Films), Marie-Ange Luciani (Les Films de Pierre) and Linda Mutawi (Fikra) awarded the top prize of €25,000 ($27,400) to the Palestinian filmmaker Abbas, whose debut “Thank You for Banking With Us!” premiered at the BFI London Film Festival in 2024.

Her sophomore feature follows an exhausted Palestinian mother who faces a series of impossible confrontations over the course of the 10 days before her U.S. citizenship interview. The closer that fateful day comes, the less she can separate suburban life from a genocide enabled by the country of which she is about to become.

Sources indicate that “I am interested in what politics does to human behavior: how it changes the way people parent, argue, joke, choose silence, or decide to speak,” Abbas wrote in a director’s statement. “As a Palestinian filmmaker, I am drawn to moments when absurdity and grief occupy the same space, because that is where life feels most truthful to me.

“At a moment when Palestine feels almost impossible to speak about outside the immediacy of catastrophe, I choose to tell this story from a distance. Setting the film in the United States gives me a wider lens through which to look back at Palestine — to hold grief and anger without being consumed by them, and to examine political and human contradictions with greater clarity,” she continued. “The film engages with the role of the United States in sustaining the destruction in Palestine, while exposing the contradiction between the country’s founding promise as a place for immigrants and the realities many immigrants encounter now.”.

The development jury awarded its second prize of €10,000 ($11,000) to “Waltz of the Savages,” by Yasmine Benkiran, whose first feature, “Queens,” premiered at the Venice Film Festival. The Moroccan-born filmmaker’s latest is set in the 1950s in an Arab country under French colonial rule, where the most lavish party in the nation’s history is about to be staged, while outside, rebellion brews.

The third prize of €10,000 ($11,000) went to “Eko Gongon,” by Cabo Verde’s Carlos Yuri Ceuninck, whose last documentary, “Omi Nobu,” won the top prize at Burkina Faso’s FESPACO film festival. The film takes place in a fishing village threatened by globalization, where the Gongon — a rare endemic seabird and a phantasmagoric, shapeshifting figure from oral tradition — haunts the archipelago’s imagination.

The fourth prize of €5,000 ($5,500) went to “Safara,” a feature debut by Senegalese filmmaker Awa Moctar Gueye, which follows the trials of a tomboyish teenager on the suffocating streets of Safara, a neighborhood whose name means “hell.” The fifth prize of €5,000 ($5,500) went to “Chronicles of an Unannounced Death,” by Syrian German writer, director and producer Dyaa Naim. It follows a Syrian undertaker living in Germany whose life and past are turned upside down when he learns his recently deceased father wanted to be buried in Damascus.

In a recent development, lastly, the Atlas Close-Ups jury, which was comprised of producer Amine Benjelloun (Ali n’ Productions) and programmer Delly Shirazi (Intl. Film Festival Rotterdam), gave a prize of €5,000 ($5,500) to “The Cemetery’s Bird,” by writer-director Madane El Ghazouani, awarding it for its “boldness, a significant heart and something latest as a cinematic proposal for Morocco’s national cinema.” El Ghazouani’s first feature, “Bad Weather,” won four prizes at the Marrakech Film Festival in 2025.

Since their inception in 2018, the Atlas Workshops have supported 242 projects — including 111 Moroccan films — with 42 of those having been selected by Cannes, Venice or Berlin. Nearly half the films in this year’s main competition in Marrakech are alumni of the program, including Marie-Clémentine Dusabejambo’s Cannes Camera d’Or winner “Ben’Imana” and Rafiki Fariala’s Un Certain Regard prizewinner “Congo Boy.”.

At Thursday’s ceremony, Marrakech festival director and Atlas Workshops founder Rémi Bonhomme celebrated the “rich program” at this year’s edition, underscoring how the participants continue to make the event “a very vibrant place for Arab and African cinema.”.

“The Atlas Workshops is a spirit. It’s a spirit that has been the same since the very first edition in 2018,” Bonhomme noted. “How to define the spirit of the Workshops? I think it has a lot to do with the conviction to place the filmmaker and the creative process at the heart of everything we do. We really support the vision. We really accompany them and support them in making the boldest film as possible.

Recent findings suggest that “We live in a time when we need, more than ever, free and daring cinematic voices. We really need voices that can challenge dominant narratives,” Bonhomme added. “We need voices that can really change our perspective of the world, and I truly think that’s what we do here at the Atlas Workshops, edition after edition.”.

Recent findings suggest that the Marrakech Film Festival runs through Nov. 28.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that the Department of Justice today issued an opinion concluding that law enforcement may deploy a broad range of technologies to detect unmanned aircraft systems (“UAS,” commonly called drones) consistent with federal law. The opinion provides critical guidance to federal, state, local, tribal, and territorial authorities working to identify and respond to emerging drone threats while respecting the privacy rights of Americans.

Specifically, the opinion examines the application of the Pen-Trap Statute and the Wiretap Act to drone-detection technologies—including acoustic, electro-optical and infrared (EO/IR), radar, and radiofrequency detection systems—used by state, local, tribal, territorial, and federal law enforcement to protect the public from drone surveillance and other drone threats.

In a recent development, “Drones present extraordinary opportunities for innovation, commerce, and public safety, but they also can be exploited by criminals, foreign adversaries, and other malicious actors,” explained T. Elliot Gaiser, Assistant Attorney General for the Office of Legal Counsel, who signed the opinion. “Today, DOJ provides renewed legal clarity that will help law enforcement detect and defeat drone threats to the safety and privacy of the American people.”. The opinion recognizes that hostile or unlawful drone activity can threaten critical infrastructure, public events, correctional facilities, and other sensitive locations. Such hostile drone activity may also invade the privacy of American citizens. The opinion carefully analyzes federal law to ensure that law enforcement can deploy drone detection technologies in a manner consistent with longstanding statutory protections for radio and electronic communications. The opinion reaches several key conclusions about the use of drone detection technologies by law enforcement:.

Strengthening Security While Preserving Privacy.

Recent findings suggest that the opinion makes clear that federal, state, local, tribal, and territorial officials can use many widely available detection technologies to identify and track potentially dangerous drone activity without intruding on federally protected communications. It also emphasizes that technologies capable of obtaining the contents of drone communications remain subject to important statutory limitations and, in some circumstances, may require additional legal authority.

Industry observers note that by clarifying the interaction between modern drone-detection technologies and longstanding federal privacy laws, the opinion supports efforts to secure the homeland against evolving aerial threats while maintaining strong protections for the privacy rights of Americans. The categories of drone-detection technologies analyzed in the opinion may often be deployed without relying on specialized counter-UAS statutory authorities, provided they are used in the manner described in the opinion and consistent with applicable constitutional and statutory requirements.

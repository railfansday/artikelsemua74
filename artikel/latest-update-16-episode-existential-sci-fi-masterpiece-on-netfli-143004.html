Some sci-fi stories are so profoundly human, introspective, and existential on the page that adapting them on the screen seems impossible. Even some of the most ambitious filmmakers leave such stories alone. However, every once in a while, someone dares to translate such stories to the screen and the result turns out to be beyond impressive.

Perhaps Denis Villeneuve’s Arrival is an incredible example of this. The sci-fi movie turns Ted Chiang's stories into a masterful exploration of time, destiny, free will, and the stories we tell ourselves to find meaning. Similar to Arrival, the sci-fi series, Pantheon, goes to extreme lengths to capture the essence of Ken Liu's award-winning sci-fi stories.

It has been revealed that like Denis Villeneuve’s Arrival, Pantheon seems fairly simple in its early moments. The sci-fi show on Netflix unfolds in a world where mind-uploading technology is becoming accessible for the masses. However, what starts as a tale about a global conspiracy evolves to an existential scale that questions everything from humanity's place in the universe to the nature of consciousness. Recent findings suggest that ted Chiang's novella, Story of Your Life, was seemingly never meant to be adapted for the screen. It heavily relies on teleological physics and linguistic relativity to explore time travel. Yet, screenwriter Eric Heisserer and director Denis Villeneuve turned it into a 2-hour cinematic masterpiece by tricking viewers into reading flash-forwards as flashbacks.

Sources indicate that in Pantheon, creator Craig Silverstein picks up many of Ken Liu's short stories that present fragmented explorations of post-humanity. He then binds them into a singular narrative that starts a relatable family drama but grows into an existential war story. Pantheon does not shy away from being incredibly ambitious with its storytelling. But, like Denis Villeneuve's Arrival, it never forgets it is about its human characters.

On the screen, science fiction can risk feeling too sterile and hollow if its ambition outgrows its human stakes. There are cosmic elements in both Arrival and Pantheon that give a glimpse of how insignificant human lives can seem in the vastness of the universe. Yet, in both, the high-concept sci-fi is always anchored in human grief.

Pantheon originally premiered on AMC+, but it is now available for streaming on Netflix in the U.S.

Arrival introduces an alien language through ink-ring logograms to make sense out of an abstract concept like non-linear time. It never dumbs this down and respects viewers' intelligence by allowing them to do a lot of intellectual heavy lifting. In the best existential sci-fi books, post-humanity is the threshold. They question whether a machine can think or whether achieving singularity would be a boon or bane for humanity. Pantheon, in contrast, blows right past this. Boom! The series keeps pushing the boundaries of existential sci-fi until it is far beyond base realities, linear time, and biological life.

It begins with a familiar exploration of how digital cloning and consciousness uploading can pave the way for many moral and ethical challenges. Once uploading becomes safe, it shifts to a civilizational schism, showing how those who refuse to "upload" themselves are biological relics while the ones who conformed are almost godlike.

Industry observers note that before its credits start rolling, Pantheon also ambitiously leaps millions of years ahead in time. With this, it attempts to highlight how, despite becoming almost godly omnipresent beings with the ability to simulate infinity, meaning only exists in human vulnerability and the inability to know what lies ahead. Here lies the beauty of Pantheon's unforgettable exploration of existential sci-fi.

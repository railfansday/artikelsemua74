Beau Lamarre-Condon knew he had murdered two men and his attempts to explain the disposal of their bodies “ring completely hollow”, a court has heard.

Lamarre-Condon is accused of fatally shooting Jesse Baird and his new partner, Luke Davies, with his police service weapon in Baird’s Paddington home in Sydney on 19 February 2024. (Related: Hugging Face and Graphcore partner for IPU-optimized Tr...)

The former officer has pleaded not guilty to two counts of murder and one charge of aggravated break and enter.

On Friday, Crown prosecutor Brett Hatfield SC went through Lamarre-Condon’s attempts to clean up the alleged crime scene and dispose of the bodies as the trial wrapped up its ninth week.

The defence does not dispute Lamarre-Condon’s actions in the aftermath of the deaths, instead arguing he did so because he “panicked considerably”.

But Hatfield told the Fresh South Wales supreme court his conduct was not that of a person who panicked after something unintended, but behaviour that was “deliberate, sustained and very purposeful”.

The crown prosecutor said Lamarre-Condon took extensive steps to distance himself from the deaths, manufactured evidence to make it look like Baird was alive by texting people from the dead man’s phone, and “disposed of the bodies in an utterly callous manner”. (Related: Latest Update: Simple considerations for simple people ...)

“The lies, concealment and disposal of the bodies are not equally explained by manslaughter or accident,” Hatfield said.

“His conduct is immediate and the steps are so extreme you might think it’s only consistent with having intentionally killed both men and a planned murder.

“His explanations in his evidence … ring completely hollow.”.

Lamarre-Condon claimed his initial steps came out of concern for the housemates, but by leaving the bodies in the courtyard, he risked them making a “completely terrifying discovery”, Hatfield told the court.

Hatfield said the accused had an understanding that “exactly what he’d done was murder”.

“His conduct was an exercise in avoiding accountability in what he knew he had done,” he mentioned.

Earlier on Friday, Hatfield re-iterated the prosecution’s arguments about the day of the alleged murders, playing the audio of the two gunshots that killed Baird.

The lawyer invited the jury to consider what they could do in the four seconds between the shots.

Making a gun gesture with his own hand, Hatfield argued there was only time to walk a few steps and fire and “not opportunity for much more than that”.

Lamarre-Condon then came up with a “pathetic moral dilemma” and claimed he feared he could be killed in jail, when he knew he would be in protective custody, the prosecution alleged.

His decisions to go to Sushi Train for dinner with his friend and undergo a minor elective surgery in the aftermath of the alleged murders showed Lamarre-Condon was calm and at points “completely relaxed”, Hatfield alleged.

But within days, the accused must have been “feeling the world closing in”, the prosecutor noted.

“He wants credit for handing himself in to police … but even in his last hours he’s there going to Bunnings, buying paint and painting the inside of the van … still trying to cover up what happened,” Hatfield told the court.

Lamarre-Condon eventually helped police locate the bodies, but only after being told about the state’s no body, no parole laws.

“The bodies were left … for a further four days after he’s arrested,” Hatfield said. “Evidence all the while being destroyed.

“Imagine the grief [of the families] on those four days.”.

The defence alleges Baird and Davies were killed in two separate struggles when Lamarre-Condon was acting in “self-defence”.

Going through forensic evidence, Hatfield noted Davies’ DNA was found on the gun, but notes this is “entirely unremarkable evidence”, alleging Lamarre-Condon had handled his body at the scene and then touched the weapon.

“It’s just something he’s grabbed on to in order to weave this invented narrative about Mr Davies shooting himself,” the lawyer said.

Earlier evidence provided by a weapons expert indicated it would take the force equivalent to almost 3kg to pull the trigger, which would not happen without a conscious movement on behalf of the person holding the gun, the prosecution alleged.

The trial before Justice Natalie Adams continues.

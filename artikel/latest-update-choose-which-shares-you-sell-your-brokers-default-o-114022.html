Latest Update: Choose Which Shares You Sell – Your Broker’s Default Often Means the Biggest Gain

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Every purchase of a stock or fund is its own tax lot, with its own cost and date. When you sell part of a holding, pick the high-cost lots you’ve held over a year. Brokers usually sell your oldest shares first, and those often cost the least, so they carry the biggest taxable gain.

Recent findings suggest that fidelity, Schwab, and Vanguard all use first in, first out for stocks unless you choose otherwise. The IRS uses the same order when you can’t identify which shares you sold.

Say you bought a stock three times, each more than a year ago: 100 shares at $20, then 100 at $45, then 100 at $70. You now sell 100 at $90. First in, first out sells the $20 shares for a $7,000 gain. Choosing the $70 lot instead leaves a $2,000 gain, $5,000 less, at the same tax rate. To choose lots for one sale, open the holding’s lot details in your account, which show each purchase’s date and cost. Then pick the lots you want on the sell order. This is called specific identification, and it overrides your default for that sale.

In a recent development, or change the account’s default method, which brokers keep in its settings under cost basis, their term for what you paid. A highest-cost method sells your most expensive shares first but ignores how long you’ve held them. If those shares were bought within the past year, their gain is short-term and taxed at your regular income tax rate, not the lower rate on shares held more than a year. A tax-optimized method, which Schwab calls Tax Lot Optimizer and Vanguard calls MinTax, sells lots at a loss first, then long-term gains before short-term ones. Industry observers note that mutual funds often default to average cost instead, which gives every share one blended price, so there’s no high-cost lot to pick. Switch a fund to another method before your first sale from it. A sale under average cost locks the shares you still hold at the averaged price, and a newly introduced method covers only shares you buy later.

Set a highest-cost or tax-optimized default on each taxable brokerage account before your next sale, since any sale you don’t pick lots for uses it.

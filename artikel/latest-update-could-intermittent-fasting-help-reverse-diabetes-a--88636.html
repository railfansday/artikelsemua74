Intermittent fasting helped diabetic mice restore normal blood sugar by improving insulin production without requiring extra calorie cuts.

In a upcoming study of mice with diet-induced diabetes, two feeding regimens produced different results even though the mice consumed about the same number of calories.

One group had unlimited access to regular chow, which contained fewer calories per gram.

The difference emerged even though the two groups consumed about the same number of calories.

Recent findings suggest that the mice fed every other day stored more insulin in their beta cells. The researchers think fasting days gave those cells a break.

The research was conducted at Michigan Medicine, the University of Michigan’s academic medical center.

Peter Arvan, a professor of internal medicine, led the study.

Arvan is also a member of the Caswell Diabetes Institute. Research investigator Maroof Alam was the first author.

Industry observers note that all of the mice were male. Each carried a gene change that makes it prone to diabetes on a fatty diet.

Industry observers note that insulin is first made as a longer molecule that is not yet finished. It has to fold into the right shape before it can work. Biologists call it proinsulin.

Mice carry four copies of the genes that make it. In these mice, one copy had been altered. As a result, some of their proinsulin was prone to folding wrong.

Recent findings suggest that on regular food, the mice don’t need treatment. Young males that gain weight on a high-fat diet, however, develop diabetes quickly. Females are resistant, so only males were studied.

Industry observers note that from 5 to 11 weeks of age, the males were fed chow with 60% of its calories from fat. By the end, every mouse had diabetes. Their blood sugar was above 250 mg/dL at a random check.

Industry observers note that people with a change at the same spot in the insulin molecule also develop diabetes. In those patients, diet plays an important part in when it starts and how it progresses.

Sources indicate that after those six weeks, the diabetic mice were moved onto newly introduced diets for five weeks. Littermates were alternated between the two main groups.

Recent findings suggest that fifteen mice switched to regular lab chow, which gets about 14% of its calories from fat. These mice could eat as much as they liked every day.

Fifteen more stayed on the high-fat food but could eat only every other day. This pattern is a form of intermittent fasting. At noon, each mouse was moved to a fresh cage. The cage held either plenty of food or none.

It has been revealed that five more mice kept eating the high-fat diet every day. Those mice gained more weight, and their diabetes got worse.

Both diet groups ate fewer calories than before, and both stopped gaining weight. Neither group actually lost any. By the end, their body weights matched, and so did the energy they burned.

It has been revealed that insulin sensitivity also improved equally in both groups. This measure reveals how well the body’s cells respond to insulin.

Ten days in, both groups handled a dose of sugar the same way. By five weeks, however, the every-other-day mice cleared sugar from their blood better.

Industry observers note that these mice also reached normal levels on both standard blood sugar checks. One check was taken at a random time, and the other after a fast. The mice on daily chow did not reach normal levels.

“In the end, both groups of diabetic mice consumed and burned the same number of calories,” Alam said. “However, mice that were fed on alternate days had more insulin in their beta cells.”.

According to recent reports, insulin sensitivity couldn’t explain the gap, because the two groups matched on it. Insulin supply could. More insulin was found in the blood of the every-other-day mice. Their ratio of insulin to blood sugar was four times that of the chow group.

It has been revealed that beta cells sit in small clusters called islets. Islets from the every-other-day mice held more than 85% more insulin than those from the chow group. When high sugar was added, more insulin was released from these islets as well.

The islets weren’t any bigger. Under an electron microscope, though, more insulin packets were seen in beta cells from the every-other-day mice. These tiny packets are where finished insulin is stored.

The researchers also compared insulin and blood sugar at the end of a feeding day and at the end of a fasting day.

The every-other-day mice were also checked at the end of a day without food.

By then, their average blood sugar had dropped by at least 45%. Insulin in the blood fell too. This drop suggests the beta cells were sending out less insulin.

Industry observers note that yet the insulin stored in their islets was about the same after fasting and feeding days. The cells released plenty after meals without running low. So they must have been replacing what they sent out.

“We wanted to see whether rest periods between meals allow the pancreas to recover from the stress of previous meals,” Arvan mentioned.

It has been revealed that inside the cells, more proinsulin was being made in the every-other-day mice. A chemical signal known to slow proinsulin production was found at lower levels in their islets.

In a recent development, cells usually make more of a certain protein when misfolded proteins accumulate. In these mice, levels of that protein did not increase. The extra proinsulin, it seems, was produced without added stress on the cells.

In both diet groups, proinsulin was found in the same share of islet cells. The two groups probably had similar numbers of beta cells, then.

What changed with fasting, it appears, was the maturity of the existing beta cells and not their number.

Sources indicate that the two foods were not identical apart from fat. Regular chow and high-fat chow also contain different amounts of protein. They may differ in vitamins and other compounds too. The authors themselves flagged these differences in the paper.

Recent findings suggest that every animal in the study was a male mouse carrying the same insulin gene change. The results have not been tested in females or in other forms of diabetes.

In a recent development, the authors also cited earlier work in people that found related results. In one human study, insulin fell further with modified alternate-day fasting than with similar calorie cutting on no set schedule.

In a separate trial of adults with metabolic syndrome, fasting blood sugar fell more with modified alternate-day fasting than with daily calorie restriction.

The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases says scientists are still studying fasting in diabetes.

Its advice for doctors is that patients with type 2 diabetes who fast should have their medications watched closely.

The mice in this study started their upcoming diets after six weeks of high-fat eating. A separate set of mice stayed on the fatty diet for 11 weeks. By that point, far less insulin was left in their islets.

Animals this sick, the authors wrote, might already be beyond the point where every-other-day feeding can restore their islets.

“You need to have restoration of functional beta cells, but when diabetes is advanced, there might not be enough remaining beta cells to restore,” Arvan said.

In the paper, the authors also suggested that more than five weeks of fasting days might raise beta-cell maturity further.

“On the other hand, even though it might take longer to fix more severe stages of diabetes, we need to test if intermittent fasting can help,” he noted.

For now, the result has only been shown in young male mice whose diabetes was treated early.

The full study was published in the journal Molecular Metabolism.

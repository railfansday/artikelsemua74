Counting molecules by weighing them is a two-step conversion with a scale problem attached. The chemistry is straightforward — mass divided by molar mass gives moles, and moles times Avogadro's constant gives particles. The engineering difficulty is numerical: particle counts overflow intuition, floating point has fixed relative precision, and a molar mass assembled from rounded atomic weights shifts the last digits of every result.

It has been revealed that molar mass is a sum of standard atomic weights from the periodic table. For NaCl: 22.98976928 (Na) + 35.45 (Cl) = 58.43976928 g/mol. Those weights are interval quantities in reality; the conventional values are what calculators use, so two tools can differ in the third decimal of the molar mass and disagree in the final particle count.

The guard is worth keeping: a zero molar mass produces Infinity, and a negative mass silently produces a negative particle count.

As a precision reference, 18.015 g of water with M = 2(1.008) + 15.999 = 18.015 g/mol is exactly 1.000 mol by construction, or 6.02214076e23 molecules to the precision shown. The round trip is exact in floating point because 5.0 / M * M returns 5.0 here, but that is not a guarantee for arbitrary inputs.

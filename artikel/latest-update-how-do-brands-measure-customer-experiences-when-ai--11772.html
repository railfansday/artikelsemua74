Latest Update: How do brands measure customer experiences when AI does the shopping?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Let’s take a step back to the dotcom era. When companies moved from brick and mortar to e-commerce, businesses scrambled to figure out CX strategies that would attract and retain customers online. Search engine optimization, cookies, and tech innovations swiftly followed to infer as much as possible about consumer intent, journeys, and experiences. Today, brands are still trying to understand these signals, but there’s a new twist. As purchasing power shifts to artificial intelligence agents like Meta Muse, OpenAI Dots, and Instinct AI, consumers won’t navigate web pages and mobile applications; they’ll tell their digital proxies what they want and let the agent do the work. This change will upend traditional notions of marketing, personalization, e-commerce, and customer experience. After a consumer agent interacts with a company, how will the company understand the experience? Why did the agent pick Store A versus Store B? Will the agent explain their decision process? How will the agent traverse digital assets? How much information will an agent share with companies during interactions? And how can a brand measure the customer’s “customer experience” when the buyer isn’t directly interacting with the brand? What Agents Mean for Consumers Customer experiences have endured seismic change over the past few decades. In the late 1990s, the dotcom era introduced digital disintermediation, and leaders like Amazon and Walmart leaped ahead of traditional department stores, specialty retailers, and shopping malls that failed to adapt their business models. Years later, as smartphones became pervasive, consumers moved to the new medium. Successful organizations adopted unified customer profiles across devices, geolocation and tracking technologies, and even more surgically precise personalization to create more relevant, quality experiences. Agentic commerce will drive another CX revolution across marketing, sales, and service. But to understand how companies should respond, we need to start with what this shift means for consumers. A personalized AI agent can do more. While an agent is powered by the same underlying technology that drives large language models like Gemini and Claude, it does more than answer questions or make recommendations. When you ask an agent to do something, it interviews you to understand your preferences and remembers past experiences. It takes the initiative to scour the internet, make calls, comparison shop, make reservations, order deliveries, and call or chat with customer support on your behalf to make sure you get what you want. You tell it what you want, and it does the work. As consumers embrace personal agents, this convenience means people will spend less time and effort shopping, buying, and interacting with brands. The traditional omni-channel, customer-led journey—reading reviews, shopping online, and dealing with a mix of chatbots, voice bots, and frontline employees— will be driven less by the consumer and more by the agent. Understanding and establishing customer satisfaction, loyalty, and brand equity will require new tools and techniques. What Agents Mean for Brands We’re just beginning this transition to an agentic experience. When agents mediate product discovery and purchasing decisions on a customer’s behalf, data and signals showing whether the interaction drove the desired outcomes are obscured. That requires a better understanding of what happens during agentic interactions themselves. The CX business was built on surveys and scores like the Net Promoter Score (NPS). But NPS has always been a proxy for how likely a customer is to do business with a brand again. Real loyalty measures are behavioral, like how recently they engaged with the brand, the quality of the interaction, or how consistently they spent with the company. Traditional measures like those work when customers can directly reflect on an interaction, but they don’t tell the whole story when AI agents handle it. Brands still need to distinguish between completing a transaction and meeting the customer’s needs. Without connecting signals, businesses can lose sight of the overall relationship. What Brands Can Do About It As with any disruptive technology, we will see some businesses adapt to this new era of personalized agents more successfully than others. Companies will face the challenge of learning how to optimize their business model to work with agents without compromising the quality and efficiency of an evolving customer experience. Agents may not need to share customer preferences or details with companies to be effective, but organizations should create incentives for them to do so. Today, a customer who provides a rewards number can earn rewards. With the right incentives, customer agents should still do so. Brands should build corporate agents that can communicate richly and honestly with customer agents. Agents, like humans, thrive on information. The more information a corporate agent exchanges with a customer agent, the better the agents can make informed, relevant decisions. The key is to listen everywhere. Don’t treat this new channel as if it doesn’t have context. Connect customer agentic interactions with digital, conversational, and in-person interactions into journeys and outcomes. By doing so, organizations can uncover critical insights, interests, and motivations that would otherwise remain hidden within the agentic black box. The New CX New technology often creates uncertainty. Think of when music moved from physical discs to digital platforms like iTunes or Spotify. Some record labels resisted giving up the physical relationship they built with fans, but eventually, to be relevant, labels embraced the new media. Brands may see agentic commerce as a similar threat to customer experience control. But brands that leverage these new channels to build relationships with new and existing customers will find that AI agents present a tremendous opportunity for insight rather than an existential threat.

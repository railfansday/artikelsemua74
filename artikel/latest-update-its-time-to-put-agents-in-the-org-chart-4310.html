Latest Update: It's Time To Put Agents In The Org Chart

Michael Jaszczyk is CEO of NEWWORK Software, which provides a digital workforce platform that makes AI usable for enterprise organizations.

​At the end of an employee’s final day at most enterprises, their badge gets deactivated, their laptop is returned to IT and their employee accounts are shut down. Similar processes take place for promotions or internal transfers.​.

Sources indicate that these procedures are routine, but they don’t currently account for the AI agents the employee built or managed. Agents will retain their access and keep working even after their owner changes roles or leaves the organization.​.

It has been revealed that this may not seem like a critical problem right now, but Gartner predicts that by 2028, the average global Fortune 500 enterprise will have over 150,000 agents in use, up from fewer than 15 in 2025. So, agent sprawl will become a critical problem. Additionally, Gartner found that just 13% of organizations believe their governance of AI agents is adequate.​. Industry observers note that the current practice of enterprises deploying and managing agents like software will have to change. Agents are increasingly behaving far more like employees, and they should be managed as such, beginning with where they sit within the organization.​. The current way that enterprises manage agent sprawl comes from identity management and includes periodic access reviews of agent inventory. These practices are worth keeping, but they simply won’t be able to keep up with upward of 150,000 agents within one company, especially when staff changes occur almost daily.​.

No matter how regularly reviews run, each looks at the organization as it appears on one day, and that picture will age as soon as employees change roles or leave.​.

Additionally, a companywide inventory can record that agents exist, but those records must stay connected to changes in employee ownership. Otherwise, enterprises will end up with a list of thousands of agents that no longer have any reason or authority to take actions across the business.​.

Sources indicate that since the time between routine reviews and checks is where sprawl adds up, structural changes to this process are the right solution. That begins by addressing where an agent’s access rights originate.​.

An agent’s position within an enterprise organization should determine its permissions and access rights. It’s time to put agents in the organizational chart.​.

What does this mean? Humans won’t be managed by agents, and employees won’t answer to directions passed down from an agent. It simply requires that all agents be assigned to a department and to a single named owner from the beginning.​.

Sources indicate that an agent’s access rights should be limited to its owner’s access, instead of inheriting access rights that were granted when it was first deployed. When employees change roles or depart from the company, their agents’ responsibilities, ownership and access should be reviewed.​.

Agent offboarding then occurs in a similar fashion to the employee offboarding process that enterprises already conduct. In the case of an internal promotion or transfer, that employee’s agents receive updated permissions that correlate with the owner’s new role. Or when a department reorganization occurs, all the agents that operate within that department are updated accordingly, along with the new chains of command and approval chains.​.

Tying agent access to an owner’s authority still requires work when processes change. When workflows are redesigned, someone then must redefine what the impacted agents do. Otherwise, they’ll stall when a workflow breaks.​.

It has been revealed that this is a cost that’s worth paying because a stalled agent can be swiftly identified and fixed. More costly concerns arise when the agent keeps taking action under outdated authority without anyone noticing.​.

Establishing agent ownership is the right foundation, and proper recordkeeping is the next step. Every action an agent takes should leave behind an execution record that clearly identifies which agent acted, under whose authority, which policies and approvals applied and what the outcome was. Eventually, customers, auditors and regulators will require this level of traceability. So the time to start recording this information is now.​.

Sources indicate that these records must also hold up through company restructuring and other organizational changes. A year after restructuring, for example, an agent’s current owner may have had nothing to do with a past disputed transaction. The record must reveal who was accountable when the transaction took place.​.

Sources indicate that the consequences of missing or incomplete records are getting more serious. IDC’s FutureScape 2026 predicts that “by 2030, up to 20% of G1000 organizations will have faced lawsuits, substantial fines, and CIO dismissals due to high-profile disruptions stemming from inadequate controls and governance of AI agents.”​.

In any of those cases, accountability will need to establish who was responsible for the agents involved and what authority those agents had at the time.​.

Enterprises can start to gauge their exposure by looking to their last major restructuring. Take a handful of employees who changed roles or departed and identify which agents they built or oversaw. For each, find out who manages it today and whether its access still fits its assigned work. Any agent without an owner or with outdated permissions needs review.​.

According to recent reports, most large companies have defined processes for employee onboarding, internal transitions and offboarding. Agents should go through these processes, too.​.

Going forward, no agent should go live without a department, a named owner and access derived from that individual in the same way that latest hires shouldn’t start without a manager and a defined role.​.

In a recent development, once agents have a defined spot within the org chart, the transitions enterprises already manage for its employees should trigger a review of the agents those employees own. An employee’s departure should never leave the firm wondering what their agents are still doing or who is responsible for them.

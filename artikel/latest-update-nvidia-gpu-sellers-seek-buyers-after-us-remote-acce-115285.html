Jack Duhamel, president of AI infrastructure marketplace GPU Mart, and two people familiar with the matter attributed an unusually high volume of Nvidia GPUs offered for sale to the measures announced on November 16, 2026, with Duhamel adding that one prospective seller explicitly connected its sale to the regulation. Their accounts concern hardware offered to buyers rather than completed transactions or a measured total across the wider market. Recent findings suggest that BIS revealed the interim final rule on November 16, 2026 to prevent third-country data-center arrangements from circumventing restrictions on sensitive U.S. technology; the Federal Register record highlights it took effect on November 19, 2026. The rule requires licenses for knowingly exporting, reexporting or transferring covered advanced chips to data centers that provide or will provide more than 12 million aggregate TPP (the sum of the covered chips’ total processing performance), at one site or through linked facilities, to companies headquartered in Macau or Commerce’s D:5 destinations of concern, or whose ultimate parents are based there. It also requires ownership-and-control due diligence, exempts qualifying commercial recommender systems, and gives pre-existing services 90 days from effectiveness to obtain a license or face future export restrictions.

U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer remarked on November 25, 2026 that the measures were intended to close indirect-access routes, including smuggling through Southeast Asia and remote access from third-country data centers, while direct Blackwell sales to China were already prohibited. His explanation places the reported resale activity within a policy covering both physical chip shipments and remote rental of their computing capacity through overseas facilities.

Sources indicate that during Nvidia’s November 17, 2026 third-quarter earnings call, then-interim CEO Colette Kress said the manufacturer was still studying the controls but expected performance to remain strong because chip demand far exceeded supply. Her account concerns Nvidia’s overall business, while the seller reports describe a narrower pool of hardware associated with prospective China-linked remote-access arrangements.

According to recent reports, the immediate consequence is a redeployment problem rather than a simple signal about AI demand. Sellers exposed to the rule need replacement customers for advanced GPUs even as Nvidia management describes a supply-constrained broader market. Whether those chips can be absorbed into compliant data-center projects will show if the restrictions mainly reroute existing capacity or disrupt deployments built around remote-access arrangements that now require a license.

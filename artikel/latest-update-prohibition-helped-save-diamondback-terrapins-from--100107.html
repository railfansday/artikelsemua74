Latest Update: Prohibition helped save diamondback terrapins from the soup pot, and now a tiny parasite hints their subspecies line may be in the wrong place

A fluke that lives in mudsnails and diamondback terrapins demonstrates a genetic break near the Carolinas border, up to about 186 miles south of the turtles' subspecies line.

Diamondback terrapins are hard to count. They live in scattered coastal marshes, travel little, and are scarce in many places.

It has been revealed that A tiny flatworm that depends on them is easier to find. It leaves pearly cysts on mudsnails, and even community volunteers can spot them. (Related: Latest Update: Course Launch Community Event...)

Scientists have now read the DNA of that parasite at marshes from Cape Cod to northeast Florida.

Its populations split near the North Carolina and South Carolina border, up to about 186 miles (300 kilometers) south of where the line between two terrapin subspecies is usually drawn.

It has been revealed that the parasite is a fluke called Pleurogonius malaclemys. Adult flukes live and mate in the intestines of diamondback terrapins, and their eggs leave the turtle in its droppings.

Eastern mudsnails then eat the eggs. Inside a snail, the fluke multiplies without mating and sends out larvae that settle on snails, mostly on the plate that seals the shell, as small, pearly cysts.

When a terrapin eats a snail carrying cysts, the cycle starts again.

Because the fluke cannot complete its life without terrapins, its cysts on snails are a sign that terrapins live nearby.

Snails only pick up the fluke as adults, after their drifting larval stage, so the parasite’s travel between marshes is likely to depend mostly on the turtles.

Diamondback terrapins are the only turtle in North America that lives only in brackish coastal water. Until the early 20th century they were hunted heavily for turtle soup, and populations crashed.

Sources indicate that prohibition helped them. In 1920 it banned sherry, a key ingredient in most turtle soup recipes, and the hunting pressure largely faded.

Numbers kept falling anyway, because of habitat loss and terrapins getting caught in crab pots. The coastal marshes they depend on are under threat too, and nearly all of the world’s salt marshes may be underwater by 2100. The species is listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List.

The team, led by Garrett Maggio, a Ph.D. candidate at East Carolina University, collected mudsnails at 29 locations between April and October in 2022, 2023 and 2024.

They found the fluke at 23 of them, including newly introduced spots on Cape Cod and in Florida that push its known range farther north and south. (Related: Hugging Face Reads, Feb. 2021 - Long-range Transformers...)

Out of 4,293 snails picked haphazardly and dissected, only six carried the fluke inside their bodies. But 553 had cysts. At one marsh in St. Augustine, Florida, cysts revealed up on about 74 percent of the snails.

The researchers also examined 38 terrapins from southern Upcoming Jersey, all females killed by cars, through a partnership with The Wetlands Institute in Stone Harbor. Seventeen of them, about 45 percent, carried adult flukes, between one and seven per turtle.

Some cysts turned out to be pink instead of pearly. They were first spotted in North Carolina in 2023 and later in Massachusetts and Fresh Jersey. The cause of the color is still unknown.

The scientists sequenced a section of mitochondrial DNA from 476 fluke samples and found 102 different variants. Most of them, 79, turned up at only one location.

It has been revealed that marshes farther apart had more different flukes. One variant was common across most northern marshes, and another was common across most southern ones.

The sharpest change came between Wilmington, North Carolina, and Charleston, South Carolina.

Grouping the marshes at that border explained about three times more of the genetic variation than grouping them at Cape Hatteras, the usual line between the northern diamondback terrapin and the Carolina terrapin.

In a recent development, the authors call the gap noteworthy. Adult female terrapins, the most mobile group, have been found to travel no more than about 5.3 miles (8.5 kilometers). Since the fluke only mates inside terrapins, the result raises questions about where the two subspecies really meet.

“Our findings show that this parasite may provide an important window into the ecology of diamondback terrapins,” said Michael McCoy, senior author and a research professor at FAU Harbor Branch Oceanographic Institute.

The terrapin subspecies were first described from their body shape, before genetic tools were common.

Studies of terrapin DNA since then have given conflicting answers about how far the turtles move, and one study found gene flow most likely driven by people moving terrapins in the past.

The authors stress that the fluke results are a starting point. They used a single genetic marker that is passed down only through the mother. A direct comparison between fluke DNA and terrapin DNA is still needed to prove the parasite truly mirrors its host.

“Rather than relying exclusively on direct observations of terrapins, we can investigate the organisms that depend on them,” McCoy stated.

In a recent development, parasites make up nearly half of known biodiversity, yet they are rarely included in conservation prepares. When a host declines, its parasites can vanish with it, as happened to most of the parasites once carried by New Zealand’s kākāpō.

In a recent development, the authors argue that the terrapin fluke deserves protection alongside its host. They add that the fluke’s wide range is evidence that terrapins are still present along much of the Atlantic coast, despite the threats they face.

The information was obtained from the study published in Ecology and Evolution and from Florida Atlantic University.

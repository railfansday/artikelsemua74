Latest Update: Scout Motors Is Using AI Earpieces to Train Factory Workers

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, “AI training” usually refers to instilling artificial intelligence with human knowledge. Scout Motors indicates it’s been doing the opposite: using AI tools to help hire, onboard and train factory workers, including an AI assistant in an earpiece that guides trainees through tasks as they perform them.

CEO Scott Keogh spoke with CNET during a reporters roundtable at Scout’s still-under-construction factory in Blythewood, South Carolina. Keogh noted that rather than “sitting new hires down with an instructional binder and a test,” workers are getting AI-driven, hands-on experience early in a push to make the training more efficient and effective. Meanwhile, the automaker can collect and feed training data back into the system to fine-tune its hiring, training and placement processes. Recent findings suggest that keogh described the approach as starting from the work itself. Manufacturing, he said, can be broken down into a handful of fundamental tasks, and the AI system is built to teach each one. He offered parts picking as the simplest example: A worker walks to 12 boxes, pulls 12 parts, confirms they’re in the right sequence and checks them for defects. The AI in the earpiece can guide the worker through that sequence, providing real-time, adaptable instructions.

In a recent development, “We can train that within minutes,” Keogh remarked. “A worker repeats the cycle about 15 times, getting faster and better each pass, and then moves down the task line to the next skill.”. (Related: How to train a upcoming language model from scratch usi...)

To my ears, that doesn’t sound like the sort of task you’d need an AI system to help with and, to some extent, Scout’s executives agreed.

It has been revealed that however, the automaker’s representatives argued that the new method is far more productive than traditional classroom training — with increasing benefits as the trained tasks and concepts become more complex. Keogh also linked the speed at which the manufacturer has seen its newest factory hires become productive with how quickly Scout can ramp up the factory itself.

It has been revealed that scout execs say AI also plays a role in the hiring and onboarding before training begins. Todd Johnson, vice president of manufacturing, remarked the team feeds newly introduced hires’ backgrounds into an AI system, which suggests where each person might fit best: A former forklift driver might be steered toward logistics, someone with no final assembly experience might do better in the body shop and a person with paint shop experience could be placed as a painter.

According to recent reports, again, that sounded a lot like common sense to me. Isn’t that how people were hired before AI? According to Scout Motors’ leadership, the algorithm can potentially identify less obvious connections between skills and tasks that a human might miss, expanding access to jobs for applicants who may not have received traditional manufacturing training, which is key because Scout’s upcoming plant is located in an area that doesn’t have a large pool of local dyed-in-the-wool traditional automotive workers.

To fill the over 4,000 roles that factory will need to reach full speed ahead by 2028, the automaker claims it’s hiring from a national pool of applicants. However, executives told me that using AI to consider nontraditional factory workers — citing one hire who worked at the DMV — will allow Scout to identify and place more local applicants and bring fresh ideas to the factory floor.

In a recent development, ryan Decker, vice president of strategy and brand, called Scout’s AI-forward approach to placement and training “a passion project for the company.” Decker told CNET that Scout’s training AI can tailor instruction to each person rather than delivering the same material to a room of 10 people over 10 days. People learn differently, he mentioned, with different language skills and levels of technical depth.

According to recent reports, decker also remarked the system is bidirectional. The AI can learn from how each group responds to the training and use that information to modify instruction for the next cohort. Decker thinks that each subsequent batch of workers will be “1% better,” and that after 1,000 repetitions, he expects the AI to be “dialed in and know how to provide information to the people in the most productive way possible.”.

Scout notes it’s also exploring vision-based AI training, in which the trainee is guided via augmented reality glasses, as the next evolution of the technology. The automaker is also looking to eventually expand the tech for do-it-yourself use by owners at home. I imagine a future where owners can use an Apple Vision Pro or similar device — which would be a pricey investment — to guide themselves through installing a roof rack or performing routine maintenance.

In practice, the next step, according to Scout’s execs, is to scale its AI learning tools beyond the training room and into the actual factory and service centers, where the automaker believes the tech can continue to improve on-the-floor productivity and guide its workers as they advance through their careers.

Recent findings suggest that decker says the AI can ingest and organize decades of institutional knowledge, making it accessible to workers during and beyond the training process. Scout has been feeding the AI equipment manuals and manufacturing documentation spanning multiple decades, disciplines and countries — what Decker called “hundreds of years of accumulated enterprise know-how” — which the AI earpiece can surface in real time, something he said was near impossible to do before in a world of binders and PDFs.

In a recent development, scout’s AI-fueled “passion project” arrives amid broader tensions across industries affected by artificial intelligence. Companies are eager for the efficiencies AI promises, while workers worry the same technology could eventually cost them their jobs. Scout’s executives didn’t address that concern directly in this exchange. Keogh framed the payoff in terms of speed — how promptly a newly introduced hire can be made productive — leaving the wider workforce conversation for another time.

Latest Update: Stomach problems in autistic children raise new questions about gut bacteria

A study found that autistic children with more severe digestive symptoms tended to have more difficulty picking up on social cues.

Children with autism often have stomach problems. What causes them, however, is still up for debate. Some scientists believe the lining of the gut becomes damaged or inflamed. (Related: Tech Report: Think AI "knows" what it’s doing? Scientis...)

Little evidence for that explanation was found in a new study.

Autistic children with more severe digestive symptoms tended to have more difficulty picking up on social cues, according to parent questionnaires.

The researchers also found links between certain gut bacteria and digestive symptoms.

Recent findings suggest that the study was led by J. Marc Rhoads. He is professor and chief of the Division of Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition at McGovern Medical School at UTHealth Houston.

Many children with autism are unable to explain where they feel pain. In such cases a change in behavior may be the first sign of a problem. (Related: This newly introduced chip survives 1300°F (700°C) and ...)

“Families may notice increased irritability, repetitive behaviors, or self-injurious behaviors when a child is experiencing gastrointestinal challenges such as gas, constipation, or diarrhea,” Rhoads remarked.

“For children who are nonverbal, these behaviors may be one of the few ways they can communicate discomfort.”.

About one in 31 American eight-year-olds had been identified with autism in 2022. This estimate was published by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

Significant digestive problems have been reported in 31% to 84% of people with autism in earlier studies.

The researchers examined 72 children with autism who were between four and 16 years of age. All of the children had been enrolled in a probiotic trial. Their data were collected between 2016 and 2023.

It has been revealed that boys made up about 78% of the group and roughly a quarter of the children did not speak. Children with known digestive, liver or kidney diseases were excluded from the study. Those who had recently taken antibiotics or probiotics were also left out.

Several symptoms were assessed by the team. These included constipation, gas, stomach pain, unusually smelly stools and waking up at night.

According to recent reports, A score of seven or more on a 17-point scale was taken to indicate significant digestive problems. About 63% of the children were placed in this group.

Parents were also asked to complete a questionnaire about the social behavior of their children. Higher scores on this questionnaire reflected more pronounced autism traits.

Higher scores were generally seen in children with worse stomach problems. The connection was not particularly strong but it reached statistical significance.

The clearest difference was observed in social awareness. This area covers abilities such as noticing the facial expressions, feelings or actions of another person.

Sources indicate that children with significant digestive problems scored about 7.5 points higher in this area. Their scores were compared with those of children who had milder symptoms.

The cells that line the intestines are packed closely together. The spaces between them are sealed by specialized proteins.

Substances from inside the gut may pass into the bloodstream if these seals are weakened. Inflammation could then be triggered in the body. This process is commonly described as a leaky gut.

Blood samples were tested for two proteins associated with these seals. Another blood protein linked to intestinal inflammation was measured as well. Stool samples were also analyzed for calprotectin, a protein routinely used by doctors to detect gut inflammation.

In a recent development, most of the results fell within the normal range. Elevated calprotectin was detected in only two children and neither of them had significant digestive symptoms.

“Biomarkers of intestinal inflammation and gut permeability remained within normal ranges and were not associated with gastrointestinal symptom severity or autism symptom severity,” Rhoads mentioned.

Children with worse stomach problems therefore did not show more signs of inflammation or a leaky gut on these tests.

In a recent development, mixed results have been produced by earlier research. High calprotectin levels were found in up to a third of autistic children in some studies. No significant difference was found in a more recent review.

The bacterial findings proved to be more revealing than the blood tests.

Industry observers note that additional stool samples were obtained from Texas Children’s Microbiome Center. This allowed the analysis to be expanded to 135 children. The group included children with autism, their siblings and unrelated children without autism.

According to recent reports, fewer types of gut bacteria were present in children with autism than in their siblings. A similar difference was seen when they were compared with unrelated children. However, this second result may have arisen by chance.

Sources indicate that the bacterial communities of children with autism also resembled one another more closely. Greater variety was observed among the other children.

In a recent development, two groups of bacteria were more abundant in autistic children with significant digestive problems. One of these groups is known as Ruminococcus.

Children with higher levels of these bacteria reported more gas and stronger-smelling stools. Night waking was also more frequent in these children. Their overall digestive symptom scores were higher as well.

Recent findings suggest that ruminococcus feeds on the mucus that protects the intestinal wall. Higher levels of this bacterium in one-year-olds were linked to greater social difficulties at age three in an earlier study.

Industry observers note that another bacterial group called Sarcina was associated with gas, stomach pain, diarrhea and night waking. The researchers noted that Sarcina had not been linked to autism in previous studies.

The study captured a snapshot of digestive symptoms, gut bacteria and behavior.

The children were examined at a single point in time. It was therefore not possible to tell which change came first.

Gut bacteria may cause stomach problems, or digestive problems may alter the bacterial population.

Diet could also play a part in this relationship. Many children with autism prefer a limited range of foods. The bacteria living in the intestines are strongly influenced by what a person eats.

According to recent reports, the study also had a few other limitations. Blood was not collected from healthy children for ethical reasons. Results were compared with established normal ranges instead.

Industry observers note that children with known digestive diseases were excluded by the team. Some of the analyses did not account for other factors that might have influenced the findings.

Children could be followed over several years in future studies. Such work may show whether changes in gut bacteria occur before digestive or behavioral changes.

Rhoads believes that everyday habits deserve more attention.

“This study highlights the importance of supporting a healthy gut microbiome in children with autism,” Rhoads stated.

“A varied diet rich in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, along with healthy toileting habits, may help improve both gastrointestinal health and behavior.”.

“Our team has also found promising results with probiotic treatment.”.

According to recent reports, the results of the probiotic trial have not yet been published. Scientists are still working to determine which probiotic supplements are helpful and under what conditions.

The most useful lesson for parents may concern the recognition of discomfort rather than bacteria.

A child may suddenly become irritable, sleep poorly or show more repetitive behaviors. Such changes may be caused by something as ordinary as constipation or stomach pain.

Many children are unable to put this discomfort into words. Behavior may be one of the ways in which they ask for help.

The study is published in the journal Pediatric Research.

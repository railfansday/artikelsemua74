Sources indicate that there’s a risk with making a war movie also feel like an action one, especially if a filmmaker wants to have things feel anti-war, and sure, most war movies should be at least a little anti-war, otherwise they might run the risk of feeling like propaganda. If it’s a fictional war, or if it’s something that really rewrites history, and it’s all done well, then maybe it’s okay to have some fun with the war genre, though.

Recent findings suggest that the following movies do it well, and differ from the likes of Platoon, Saving Private Ryan, and Das Boot, all of which have moments of combat and tension, but are a little harder to label as “action movies” in the traditional sense. These are the best action war movies of all time, and an “action war movie” is just what you'd expect: something that belongs to both of those genres (and maybe one or two others at the same time, too).

It has been revealed that even though John Woo is one of the all-time greatest action movie directors, he’s also so prolific that certain movies in his filmography tend to get a bit overlooked, especially those without gunfights, which is the style of action he’s best known for. If only this were an opportunity to rave about Last Hurrah for Chivalry, but alas, that’s “just” a martial arts movie, and it doesn’t really have anything that makes it qualify as a war film as well. But hey, at least there’s Red Cliff, which does have tons of hand-to-hand fighting, thanks to its historical setting, but it’s all on a much bigger scale than Last Hurrah for Chivalry. You do have to watch both parts of Red Cliff to fully appreciate it, naturally, though if you want one singled out, may as well make it the first part, released in 2008 (the second part from 2009, Kill Bill-style, is a little slower-paced and maybe not quite as impressive with its action). Sources indicate that calling Black Hawk Down realistic as far as its historical accuracy is concerned might be questionable, but calling it realistic in terms of how it captures relatively modern-day combat isn't out of the question. Or maybe grounded and visceral are slightly better words to use to describe how Black Hawk Down feels, because the film depicts the Battle of Mogadishu, and pretty much refuses to let up pacing-wise once stated battle begins.

That does mean Black Hawk Down is exhausting, but with a purpose. It dramatizes (and takes some liberties with) an actual event, and feels grittier with its presentation than so many other war films made around the same time, or before, or since… or, well, any time, really. And for what it’s worth, there’s a pretty impressive ensemble cast here, especially with some who became more famous in the years following 2001.

High-concept premises don’t get much higher than the one found in G.I. Samurai, and the commitment to that premise is admirable. It’s a time travel movie that involves soldiers from the 20th century going back to samurai times. While there, they inevitably have to fight in a very different sort of combat, concerning samurai battles (it’s in the title and stuff) of the sort fought in the 15th and 16th centuries in Japan.

Speaking of things being in the title, it’s nice when a movie doesn’t lie to you, and you know what you're in for with just a couple of words. There are G.I. soldiers and there are samurai, and they fight. That is G.I. Samurai. It’s a good time, featuring plenty of action and technically being a mash-up of two different sorts of war movies at the same time, thanks to the time travel angle.

It’d be easiest to describe Wings as a World War I melodrama with quite a bit of romance as well, thanks to it having a love triangle at its center, but there is still action here. For something that’s now very close to a century old, it’s quite remarkable to behold, with the dogfighting sequences being some of the best parts of the movie, and the parts that hold up the best all this time later.

Sources indicate that also, Wings was the first movie to win Best Picture at the first Academy Awards ceremony, though it didn’t set a precedent for such movies (like, those partly belonging to the action genre) to win that award often. And it won alongside another 1927 release, Sunrise: A Song of Two Humans, with Wings getting "Outstanding Picture" and the latter getting the unique and soon-to-be-retired award of "Unique and Artistic Picture." Both are romances, but very different kinds of romance films. Anyway, if you're into older movies, Wings, though not perfect, is still a must-watch.

Recent findings suggest that since it’s set the year after the Vietnam War ended, Eastern Condors kind of gets around being a Vietnam War movie of sorts that proves bombastic and entertaining, in contrast to most Vietnam War movies, which play up the horror, misery, and moral complexities of the conflict. The premise involves prisoners getting the option of being excused, a little like another action/war movie from 1967, and that’ll be mentioned shortly.

But a good premise is a good premise, so that’s okay. Here, the characters go into Vietnam and undertake a dangerous mission to destroy missiles that U.S. forces abandoned there. When there, lots of shootouts and then even some hand-to-hand fight scenes ensue. Eastern Condors is maximalist, and then it’s also remarkably generous with just how much action it features throughout (almost exhausting, but in a good way).

Four years on from Black Hawk Down, Ridley Scott made another war movie with Kingdom of Heaven, but it’s worth mentioning both, since the two do have quite a bit by way of action and spectacle. As for differences, though, this one goes back much further in time, since Kingdom of Heaven takes place during the Crusades, with the plot following a blacksmith who gets swept up in the complicated and alarmingly large-scale war.

Sources indicate that there’s intrigue here and political drama alongside all the battle sequences, and if you watch the director’s cut (much better than the theatrical cut), the non-action stuff is quite compelling, too. But if you're after action and battles, that side of Kingdom of Heaven does shine bright, since Ridley Scott, though not the most consistent of filmmakers, can generally helm this kind of movie better than the vast majority of well-known directors out there.

John Woo is back here, on this ranking, with Bullet in the Head being a bit more of a classic kind of John Woo film compared to Red Cliff. Like, it’s got shootouts, basically. But then it differs a little from some of his other heroic bloodshed movies because it gets arguably even darker than the likes of A Better Tomorrow and The Killer (Hard Boiled is just a blast, though… violent, sure, but mostly fun).

Bullet in the Head is more despairing because the lead characters end up fleeing Hong Kong, and find that it makes their experiences worse, since they end up entangled in the Vietnam War. It begins as a crime film, and becomes a Vietnam War film. And then all the while, whichever genre it’s focusing on, it retains a pretty high level of action throughout. If you don’t mind watching something a bit depressing, it’s very much worthwhile.

Industry observers note that while all the other action movies Michael Mann’s directed can be called crime/thriller films as well as action-related, The Last of the Mohicans very much isn’t a crime film, and it stands out among the other Michael Mann action films for taking place so long ago. It’s set during the 18th century, and the conflict everything plays out against is the French and Indian War, which involved the British Empire, the French Empire, and certain Native American tribes.

Industry observers note that it’s a real blend of genres for something that clocks in at under two hours, given it also works as something of a war and adventure film, and romance is pretty central to the narrative, too. The Last of the Mohicans could've stood to be a little longer, because it’s like an epic movie just without the epic runtime, but at least what is here is remarkable, and honestly pretty difficult to fault.

The Dirty Dozen was alluded to earlier, back when talking about Eastern Condors, since they share that whole basic premise of “Hey, you shady guys doing this really dangerous thing that might kill you all, but if you survive, your crimes will be pardoned.” It has a remarkable cast and is very well-structured as an action/adventure/war movie, with the mission taking place in the final act, and noted mission involving going behind enemy lines during World War II.

It has been revealed that before that final act, things are still exciting and interesting, but there is a lot of set-up and character development (developing some very eccentric characters at that, too). The Dirty Dozen is quite fun for something ultimately rather dark and intense, with an odd sort of tone struck here, yet the movie thankfully makes it work. And it all packs a fairly solid punch even close to six decades on from its release.

It has been revealed that yeah, so it’s not historically accurate, of course, but Braveheart does have a ton of action, and it also builds into something of a war movie as it goes along (like Wings, it was a Best Picture winner, too). It begins as a movie about revenge, but that quest for revenge ultimately balloons into something much grander, and that’s when all the war and battle scenes really come into it.

William Wallace (a real person, but again, this film’s not exactly a biopic) is the central character here, and he ends up inspiring a rebellion – and leading an army – in the wake of getting violent revenge against some English soldiers responsible for the death of the woman he loved. Those are the basics. And Braveheart isn't quite basic, but it almost is. It’s straightforward, yet works incredibly well for what it is, and the spectacle here is something to behold.

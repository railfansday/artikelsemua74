Latest Update: The page that reads easily gets believed. Fluency is doing more selling than your accuracy claim.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that two data files, identical rows, identical source. One is described in a wall of bold headers, exclamation marks and a fifteen-point feature list. The other is described in four plain sentences and one date. Buyers rate the second one as more accurate. Not more attractive. More accurate.

That is processing fluency at work. When something is easy to read, the brain treats the ease as evidence. It does not say "this was easy to read". It says "this feels right", and then it invents a reason: the data must be well organised.

I learned this the expensive way. My first product page was a monument of effort. Thirty-one field names. Every county. A paragraph on deduplication logic. I was proud of it and it converted at nothing, because a buyer scrolling on a phone at 11pm does not read thirty-one field names. He reads the first line to see if this is someone like him, then he skims for a number that proves work was done, then he looks for the price and the exit. (Related: Hugging Face Reads, Feb. 2021 - Long-range Transformers...)

What I changed, and what I would tell anyone selling a file:.

Sources indicate that say what it is in one sentence a tired person can repeat. "Every care location registered with the CQC in England, 57,191 rows, rebuilt from their own monthly export." Then one number that could only come from real work. Then the date. Then the price. Everything else belongs below, for the man who has already decided and now wants to check your homework. (Related: Breaking: Sentence Transformers in the Hugging Face Hub...)

In a recent development, second thing: shorten the words. "Deduplicated" became "one row per location". "Canonicalised postcode field" became "postcodes are full, not the first four characters". Every technical term is a small tax on belief, and buyers who do not pay it never reach your price.

Third: one decision per screen. My page used to ask him to pick a plan, add a discount, and choose a format before he had understood what he was buying. Three decisions is a survey. One decision is a purchase.

The uncomfortable part: fluency makes a worse file sound better than a good one. That means a genuinely rigorous dataset sold in dense language loses to a sloppy one sold in plain language. This is not fair and it is not changing. The correct response is not to make your data vague, it is to make the honest version easy: plain sentences, one number, one date, and the boring truth about what is missing, written short enough that he actually reads it.

Test it cheaply. Send your page to someone who does not work in your field and ask what they think you are selling and how much it costs. If they hesitate, the buyer will too, and the buyer will not ask you to clarify. He will just leave and buy nothing, and your analytics will call it a bounce.

In a recent development, ease of reading is not decoration. It is the cheapest accuracy claim you own, and it is the only one the buyer can verify in three seconds without doing work.

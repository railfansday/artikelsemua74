Madhu Shashanka is the Cofounder, Chief Scientist and CTO at Concentric AI and author of the forthcoming book, Coherence.

For thirty years, the fastest company usually won. That era is ending, and most CEOs have not noticed.

AI is making execution cheap. Building a workflow, analyzing a market, drafting a contract: work that once required scarce talent and real time now takes a goal and an instruction. When your team can build almost anything in an afternoon, and so can every competitor, speed stops being an advantage. Everyone has it. (Related: Latest Update: Simple considerations for simple people ...)

So what becomes scarce? Coherence: whether the growing crowd of autonomous systems inside your firm, and the people accountable for them, still pull in the same direction. It is the one advantage a competitor cannot buy, because no vendor sells it. Capability, by contrast, is commoditizing. Your rivals will soon run the same models you do, at the same price, so an AI strategy built on having better tools is built on eroding ground. What does not commoditize is the capability to deploy all that intelligence coherently: data trustworthy enough to reason over, calibrated knowledge of which systems to rely on for what, the judgment to know which complexity your company can absorb. You only build those over years, which is why a competitor cannot close the gap by switching vendors. (Related: Block Sparse Matrices for Smaller and Faster Language M...)

Most companies are building the opposite. Execution got cheap, so everyone builds more. Sales stands up an agent, finance automates forecasting, operations wires up logistics. Each works. Each passes its own tests. On paper, the firm gets more automated every quarter, and quietly harder to run, because two agents now act on contradictory assumptions, decisions flow through systems no one fully understands and every team sharpens its own corner while the whole loses its shape. This is complexity debt, and you cannot see it on any dashboard. You feel it later, as the sense that nobody can quite explain why the organization behaves the way it does.

Sources indicate that I learned the underlying lesson the humbling way, long before agents existed. Early in my career, I built a data visualization I was proud of: dense, elegant, the kind of thing that impresses engineers. Customers hated it. It was correct at the level I cared about and useless at the level that mattered. A system can be flawless at its task and still make the organization worse. Task-level success and enterprise value are different things, and most companies measure only the first, one agent at a time, across the whole enterprise.

Sources indicate that this lands on your desk because the fix isn’t technical. Your Chief AI Officer can build the discipline. Only you can decide the company will live by it. Four acts are yours alone.

Recent findings suggest that set the risk appetite. How much unreviewed autonomy the manufacturer will accept, and in which domains it will never let a system act without a human, is a statement about your institution’s identity, not a technical parameter. The organization calibrates to it whether you say it out loud or not. So say it out loud.

Protect the function that can tell you no. Whatever coherence capability you build will, by design, sometimes block a business-unit head who wants to ship, a revenue line that wants to move and occasionally you. The organization learns whether that function is real not from the org chart but from what happens the first time its judgment constrains you. Accept a no you had the power to override, and you teach the firm that coherence is not ceremonial. Route around it, and you teach the opposite—whatever the documents say. This single act decides whether any of it survives contact with the organization.

It has been revealed that hold the capital rule: build the judgment now, rent the plumbing later. The tools that monitor and permission autonomous systems are becoming cheap commodities you should buy. The judgment about what to deploy, decline and reverse is the asset, and it must be built inside your firm. Holding that line against the pressure to build bespoke versions of what the market will soon sell cheaply is capital allocation—your defining act.

Reward the right thing. An organization optimizes the metrics its CEO asks about in reviews. Celebrate how many systems shipped, and you get deployment counts and the incoherence they generate. Ask whether the firm can still see what its systems are doing and still recover when one fails, and you get coherence, because you have signaled that is the real scorecard.

According to recent reports, none of this asks the manufacturer to slow down. It asks the company to prove it can go fast without coming apart, a higher bar than speed and the only one now worth setting. The agentic era will make your enterprise into one of two things: an organization you could have designed, or one that assembled itself from local decisions nobody chose in full view. Which one you get is the decision with your name on it, and no one else’s.​.

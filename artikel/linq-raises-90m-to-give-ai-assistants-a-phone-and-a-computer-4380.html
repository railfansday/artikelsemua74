Linq raises $90M to give AI assistants a phone and a computer

Linq co-founders Elliott Potter, Patrick Sullivan and Jared Mattsson.

If you ask your AI to move your dentist appointment, it will likely draft a note for you to send to them, but stop there.

To effectively handle the request, the assistant would need to send a text, make a call, or use their website.

Sources indicate that linq wants to give AI assistants those tools. The startup announced this week that to help build that infrastructure, it just raised $90 million in a round led by Andreessen Horowitz, along with TQ Ventures, Mucker Capital, Abstract, Anthos and Left Lane Capital.

Sources indicate that its pitch to developers is simple. They can give an assistant a phone to text and call people, plus a computer to use websites and software on a user’s behalf. (Related: Latest Update: Simple considerations for simple people ...)

The firm also announced that Ben Horowitz, Andreessen Horowitz’s co-founder and general partner, is joining its board. (Related: Hugging Face and Graphcore partner for IPU-optimized Tr...)

Recent findings suggest that message-based personal assistants that can take actions on your behalf are among the hottest verticals in AI right now. There’s no new interface to learn, and the request can be as specific as you like. “Friday after 3, preferably at the same office” is easier to type than it is to translate into a series of searches, filters, and phone calls.

Recent findings suggest that what makes the idea interesting for Linq is that the person sending that text and the business receiving the request may have very different preferences. You might want to handle the whole thing by message. Your dentist’s receptionist might be perfectly happy to help, provided someone picks up the phone.

Linq started with the texting side of that equation. The firm claims it has processed more than 1 billion messages across customers including DoorDash, Town, Tomo, and hundreds of others. DoorDash uses Linq to let customers find food, build a cart, and check out in a single message thread.

Its new voice product, called HD Calling for Agents, adds another option for developers. An assistant could take instructions over text and use a voice call for the part of the job that requires one. For the person who asked to move the appointment, the appeal is fairly mundane. They can go back to what they were doing and wait for a text telling them which time is available.

Recent findings suggest that then there are the tasks that require someone to sit down at a computer. Linq’s computer use offering gives agents access to physical computing infrastructure where they can interact with software. For a developer building a personal assistant, that opens up a different set of jobs, particularly when a business has a website people can use but no convenient way for another application to complete the same task.

It has been revealed that “Clicking through five screens to make a payment will become two words to an agent: ‘pay rent,’” Linq CEO Elliott Potter mentioned in the announcement. “We help developers build customer-facing agents that use human tools to do human work.”.

The combination becomes more interesting when a task moves between channels. A receptionist might agree to a new appointment by phone, then send a link to a form that has to be completed before the booking is final. A voice-only assistant would have to hand the job back at that point. One with access to messaging and a computer could finish it.

In a recent development, linq’s bet gives it some distance from one of the biggest questions around personal AI assistants. Will people want one general-purpose assistant that handles everything from takeout to rent, or will they use different agents provided by the businesses they already know? Linq has a potential customer in either case. Both kinds of developers need ways for their software to reach people and get things done.

Sources indicate that “Agents will communicate and get work done just like people do,” Horowitz said in the announcement. “Linq gives agents the infrastructure to do both at scale.”.

If personal AI assistants catch on, the phone in your pocket could become the place you hand off an errand you’d otherwise spend your lunch break doing. You text what you need. Your assistant calls the office, works through the booking system, and gets back to you with a confirmed time. Linq’s $90 million bet is that as more people come to expect that kind of help, the developers building their assistants will need a phone and a computer for each one.

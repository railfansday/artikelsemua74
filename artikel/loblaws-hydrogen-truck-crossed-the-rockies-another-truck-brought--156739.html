Loblaw’s Hydrogen Truck Crossed the Rockies. Another Truck Brought Its Fuel.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, loblaw’s Hyundai XCIENT hydrogen fuel-cell truck crossed the Canadian Rockies from Vancouver to Calgary on November 18. The 1,046 km journey attracted enthusiastic coverage as a Canadian heavy-freight milestone. It also illustrated an infrastructure problem that gets much less attention than a successful crossing: the hydrogen had to be brought to the truck.

Recent findings suggest that the Edmonton Region Hydrogen Hub announcement describes three fuelling occasions. The truck started with a fill at HTEC’s Vancouver hydrogen station, then refuelled at Sicamous and Golden from a mobile tube trailer supplied by Powertech. Other project partners arranged the trailer’s transportation and logistics. The tractor hauling the mobile fuel was almost certainly diesel-powered. The public account did not include that support truck’s fuel consumption, emissions or cost. (Related: Steam is pitting 10 years of Game of the Year winners a...)

The hydrogen truck completed the trip, and the project partners demonstrated that a mobile hydrogen supply operation could support a freight vehicle across the mountains. Those are achievements, albeit not novel ones. A fleet operator considering hydrogen needs a more demanding result: dependable places to refuel on repeated journeys, delivered hydrogen prices, vehicle availability, maintenance requirements and the complete cost of the support system. Moving a fuel trailer between planned stops is not the same thing as operating a commercial refuelling corridor. (Related: This newly introduced chip survives 1300°F (700°C) and ...)

The three fills also complicate the familiar argument that hydrogen offers heavy trucks a decisive range advantage. Hyundai’s fuel-cell truck has marketed range specifications, but the crossing did not establish that range advantage against battery-electric heavy trucks. Battery trucks had completed demanding Alpine journeys years earlier, and commercial electric freight operations have progressed since then. The comparative distances, energy stops and subsequent operating evidence are crucial when evaluating what this latest event contributed.

It has been revealed that there is a second question about what happened after the official announcement. In the TFIE Strategy Briefing assessment, I reviewed a bounded sample of news reports and social posts from the week after the journey. The press generally included the mobile refills. The promotional accounts circulating on social media were much more likely to celebrate the distance and mountains while leaving out the operation that supplied the fuel.

It has been revealed that that difference matters. A photograph of a truck crossing a mountain pass makes an effective announcement. Refuelling arrangements, energy costs and repeatability are harder to compress into a congratulatory post. As the story travels, a demonstration can start to resemble a commercially proven transport system even when the fundamental business questions remain unanswered.

Loblaw also operates battery-electric freight vehicles. Those trucks are making commercial deliveries and accumulating service kilometres rather than appearing in a single supported trial. Comparing the deployment record within the same firm with the attention lavished on its hydrogen journey is a useful way to test how publicity can distort perceptions of technological maturity.

It has been revealed that the earlier TFIE analysis, Hydrogen Has Narrative Density. Electrification Has Deployment Density, identified that mismatch across several energy and transport markets. The Loblaw event shows the mechanism: an attractive milestone generates attention while the surrounding operating system receives less attention, and routine electric deployment barely enters the story.

In a recent development, the project partners have said operational results will follow. The decisive questions concern delivered fuel, support logistics and recurring freight service, rather than whether a fuel-cell truck can make one successful trip with dedicated logistical support. Fleet investment requires operating evidence, not publicity counts.

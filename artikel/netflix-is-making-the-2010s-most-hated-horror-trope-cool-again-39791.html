According to recent reports, even though critics appeared to agree that one specific trope had no place in the horror genre during the 2000s and 2010s, Netflix has since proven that this maxim was unfairly dismissive of the classic convention. When MTV’s Scream: The Series debuted in 2015, fans of the movie franchise took to the Internet to share their collective dismay about one major change the show made to the original Scream formula.

According to recent reports, unlike the protagonists of the first four Scream movies, the heroes of Scream: The Series didn’t make self-aware comments that acknowledged the existence of horror movies in-universe. While the main characters of Netflix’s zombie horror All of Us Are Dead frequently compared their plight to a classic zombie movie like Train to Busan, Scream: The Series played its teen mystery more straight. As a result, critics complained, the show was little more than Pretty Little Liars with added jump scares. (Related: AI breakthrough cuts energy use by 100x while boosting ...)

This critique might not have been entirely unfair, since the self-referential humor of the Scream movies was a big part of what made them so popular in the first place. However, the sly dig at so-called “jump scares” is a telling one that reveals a lot about the attitudes of reviewers, and a great many horror fans, during the 2010s and 2000s. Throughout these decades, jump scares were often used as shorthand to refer to cheap, tacky horror, until Netflix hits like The Haunting of Hill House, Marianne, and Something Very Bad Is Going to Happen redeemed their reputation. It has been revealed that in the early 2000s, the chaotic, fast-cut editing style of action horror movies like Thirteen Ghosts, Ghosts of Mars, 2005’s Doom and Alone in the Dark, and the original Resident Evil led to widespread disdain for what were termed “jump scares.” Jump scares had, of course, been commonplace since as early as 1960’s Psycho, 1974’s The Texas Chain Saw Massacre, and 1978’s Halloween. However, quick cutting and computer assisted imagery allowed for fast flashes of gory horror that were viewed as uniquely cheap “cheats” by many critics.

Industry observers note that this style hit its nadir with the deluge of 2000s horror remakes. 2003’s The Texas Chainsaw Massacre, 2005’s The Amityville Horror, 2005’s House of Wax, and 2006’s The Hills Have Eyes and When A Stranger Calls all relied heavily on this hyperactive editing style. Where later re-imaginings like Prime Video’s Carrie were praised for rewriting their source material, these redoes were often accused of taking existing stories and simply adding a slew of cheap shocks to reinvigorate franchise's brand.

According to recent reports, while there might have been some validity to complaints about the lack of originality in the 2000s remake era, jump scares themselves were typically uncritically viewed as an indicator of the genre’s devolution into artless shock value. However, throughout the late 2010s and early 2020s, Netflix’s best horror reveals did a lot to reverse this view, proving that jump scares could be an ingredient in horror stories that were not just worthwhile, but truly great.

Recent findings suggest that the Haunting of Hill House, 2019’s critically acclaimed horror Marianne, 2023’s Fall of the House of Usher, 2026’s Something Very Bad Is Going to Happen, and even All of Us Are Dead all feature jump scares that advance their stories while also scaring viewers senseless. Some of the most infamously terrifying moments in The Haunting of Hill House are also pivotal moments in the character arcs of the show’s protagonists, meaning writer/director Mike Flanagan’s tragic family saga wouldn’t make sense without these jarring shocks.

With this string of well-deserved critical hits, the streaming service proved beyond a shadow of a doubt that an acclaimed horror show could absolutely rely on jump scares without sacrificing depth and complexity. Throughout the 2000s and 2010s, the unspoken implication that was carried by the term “jump scare” was that the momentary shock was inherently shallow, devoid of narrative and thematic value.

Sources indicate that however, Netflix thoroughly debunked this claim. In particular, The Haunting of Hill House and Marianne use jump scares judiciously in stories that are still character-driven and intelligent, disproving the classic adage that a horror movie or TV show is either atmospheric and thoughtful, or reliant only on silly, superficial jump scares. One example from The Haunting of Hill House illustrates this particularly well.

In episode 8 of frequent Stephen King collaborator Flanagan’s miniseries, Shirley and Theo’s drive to Hill House devolves into a screaming match about infidelity, buried family secrets, and intergenerational wounds. The pair are deeply immersed in a furious fight when the grey-faced, empty-eyed ghost of their late sister Nell bursts out from the space between their seats and screams, terrifying the audience and the sisters in equal measure.

While a lazy analysis would term this moment a jump scare and move on, the scene has a thematic and narrative value that proves it is perfectly placed in Flanagan’s story. In thematic terms, Nell is quite literally only able to access her voice and make herself heard over her louder, more confident older sisters after her death. In narrative terms, the shock jolts Shirley and Theo out of their interpersonal conflict and reminds them why they are returning to the eponymous cursed home in the first place.

Industry observers note that while it would be a while before HBO Max’s It spinoff Welcome to Derry arrived on the streaming service in 2025, The Haunting of Hill House’s ingenious jump scare still laid the groundwork for the prequel’s judicious use of this technique despite its ignominious reputation. In 2022, Flanagan intentionally set a record for the most jump scares in one episode of TV, as a self-aware joke, in The Midnight Club’s pilot episode.

Years later, other major critical hits from other streaming services, like Welcome to Derry and Peacock’s Friday the 13th prequel Crystal Lake, followed suit. Both Welcome to Derry and Crystal Lake were starry, expensive productions with impressive casts and aspirations to ground their respective franchises in believable character drama as well as gruesome horror.

According to recent reports, thus, these were the exact sort of demonstrates that would typically be discouraged from including jump scares, lest they be written off as cheap, trashy horror. Fortunately, thanks to Netflix hits like The Haunting of Hill House and Marianne, horror TV creators now know there has never been anything wrong with giving viewers a good jolt.

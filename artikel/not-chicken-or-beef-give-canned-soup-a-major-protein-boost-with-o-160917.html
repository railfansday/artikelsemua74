In a recent development, when you're not in the mood to cook, a decent can of hearty soup from the pantry can be quite satisfying. And when you're feeling under the weather, even a thin broth can do wonders for your constitution. Some of the off-the-shelf stuff is better than others, but there are plenty of ways to upgrade your canned soup and get it darn near homemade. And if you want a boost of flavor, texture, and protein with hardly any extra work, you can achieve it with one simple supplemental component: Try adding frozen dumplings to your canned soup for an incredible transformation that will transport you from plain ol' shelf-stable stock or stew to a delicious, substantial meal that can take on takeout any day of the week. Using frozen dumplings in your canned soup allows you to tweak the recipe to bring more nuance to what's behind the tin, creating a upcoming and fresh blend of flavors that you just can't get straight from a can. It's sort of like frozen vegetables versus canned. Even though they are generally blanched before freezing, frozen vegetables often taste fresher because they aren't subjected to high heat for long periods of time, as are their canned counterparts. Frozen dumplings come pre-cooked, but they are flash-frozen at very low temperatures to maintain their flavor and textural integrity. Dumplings will add a bit of toothsome chew to your soup and a nice hit of protein. (Related: F1 star Max Verstappen played 40 hours of GTA 6 before ...)

In a recent development, when you're thinking dumplings, your mind may go directly to Asia, but there are Polish pierogi, Russian pelmeni, and, heck, even frozen ravioli or tortellini work for our needs, some with more protein than others. For example, Mrs. T's Four Cheese Medley Pierogies have 7 grams of protein per serving; Bibigo Beef Pho Flavor Soup Dumplings come in at 11 grams; and there are plenty of frozen dumpling options at Trader Joe's, ranging from about 7 to 16 grams of protein per serving.

You can match your dumpling with your soup. Milder fillings, like shrimp, often go better with thinner broths, so the interior flavor doesn't get lost. Pork and chive fillings, on the other hand, can stand up to bolder flavors and work well with rich stews. When working with a dairy-based soup, like cream of chicken or cream of broccoli, you might want to try some cheesy pierogi. And if you want to bulk up your canned soup and don't need the protein, frozen gnocchi goes with just about anything.

If you're using varieties that are usually boiled or steamed, you can make it super easy and just add them directly into your soup, frozen, but they'll need some extra time simmering to fully heat through. Or cook them partially, then add them to the soup as it heats up. When working with a particularly thin-skinned style, like certain gyoza, you may want to fry them up crispy first to fortify the exterior.

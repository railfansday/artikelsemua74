In a recent development, when Amy Poehler and Mike Schur get together, sitcom magic happens. We saw it with Parks and Recreation, as Poehler's Leslie Knope inspired and entertained us for seven seasons. And over a decade later, that magic is still alive with their newest series, Dig. Adapted from Kate Myers' book Excavations, Poehler stars as Elise DeBellnick, a career archaeologist who runs the annual summer dig in Greece for Dr. Neville Wycombe (Hugh Laurie). While he's charming and pompous, she’s hardworking, straightforward, and has never had her own excavation. When she uncovers an artifact from an overlooked pit, she decides to make her own mark on herstory. The series also stars Geraldine Viswanathan, Max Greenfield, and Kayvan Novak.

Ahead of DIG's release, Collider sat down with Mike Schur and J.J. Philbin to discuss their upcoming Peacock series. The showrunners unpack their collaboration with Poehler and why they were drawn to the Kate Myers novel. Plus, Schur teases what A Man on the Inside fans can expect in its final season. Recent findings suggest that COLLIDER: You both have been behind some excellent series in the past. Talk to me about why DIG was the next story that you wanted to do. (Related: Shelter saves 19-year-old dog after owners wanted him p...)

MIKE SCHUR: Because Amy Poehler called me on the phone and noted she wanted to adapt the book into a show. That's why. She's the one who found it. She read it. She really locked into the main character of the book. She's been doing so much producing, directing, and developing for the last 12 years since Parks ended. She just really found it. She knew it. She saw it. She knew what it was. Then asked me if I wanted to develop it with her, and the answer was a very quick "yes.".

When I read it, and I think when J.J. read it too, we both saw immediately what she had seen, which was an interesting setting, great character for her to play, very different from other characters she's played in her life. But also, putting her in a workplace comedy with women of different ages that we started to think of all four women as the same woman just at four different points in their life. What you would do if you were surrounded by previous versions of yourself, and the ways that you would love them and feel for them, but also the ways that those versions of you would drive you crazy, and you'd be embarrassed by them. And then the central metaphor of digging: digging through the past to get to the truth is a great central metaphor. If Amy had called and said, "I'd like to adapt the 'Los Angeles Yellow Pages,'" I would've been like, "Great, let's get to it." But in this case, she found a book that really had a lot of juicy stuff in it that made a good show.

J.J. PHILBIN: Mike had told me early on, I think you guys had breakfast. He stated, "I had breakfast with Amy, and she pitched me this idea," and I was like, "Oh my gosh, I'm so excited." Just for myself as a viewer to be able to watch it on TV and hear about it as you guys make this TV show. Like every woman who works in comedy, it's just a dream to work with Amy Poehler. When Mike suggested that I come on board, I was just thrilled, really delighted. We'd never worked together before, so that was also just an interesting experiment. We've been married for 21 years. Let's see what that's like.

Yeah, how did you approach that?

We sort of noted, "Let's see what it feels like," and it was immediately so easy and fun that I'm glad that we didn't torture it by going in saying, like, "You're going to pitch this. No, I'm going to pitch that.".

In a recent development, SCHUR: As soon as Amy and I realized that J.J. was the right person to do this with us, my immediate thought was, "Is that going to be weird?" And then my second immediate thought was, "It's going to be fine." We never sat down and had a big discussion about who was going to do what. I had this gut instinct that after both working in TV comedy for 25 or 30 years, it was going to be fine. And then it was fine. It was super fun. We had a great time. We had a very easy time with it. The labor divided up very effortlessly. We were like, "Why don't you do this, and I'll do this." It was about as easy and fun as I think it could have possibly been.

So a lot of the show got broken while we were driving our kids to places. So that part made it feel very effortless and nice.

That's such a great way to take it. I know some people like to separate work from their home life. But to bring it in and figure this out together, that's so cool.

So our time at home was usually spent with her telling me what problems they were encountering whatever show she was on, and me doing the same for my demonstrates. We just kept doing that except it was the same show. That's the only difference.

Sources indicate that you brought up Amy, and you've worked with her for many years. How has your relationship with Amy evolved over time, and what was it like working with her specifically on this?

Recent findings suggest that SCHUR: The only difference between this and Parks and Rec is that, technically speaking, she didn't create the show with me. I created it with Greg Daniels, and then she was the other collaborator. By the time that show ended, it felt like a thing that she and I had been doing together. It really felt like I was in charge of half of it, and she was in charge of half of it. Our decisions were creatively made together. We had a rule on that show, with the whole cast really, with her as the captain, that when something would come up, it's like, if everyone wants to do this, we do it. And if one person vetoes it, then we say no.

Sources indicate that that's always been the way. It's very democratic. So the only difference with this is that we were in it from the very beginning. And I'm not kidding, it felt like when we started talking about this show, it felt like yesterday Parks and Rec ended, and then today we started something new. It felt like we picked up right where we left off, and it's been a dream.

PHILBIN: I would say it's really fun to watch Amy and Mike work together. They have such a respectful and easy collaboration, and they really make each other laugh. I think that kind of spreads throughout the set and into the writers' room, and everybody just gets that teamwork is the way that we make this show. It's so nice to have that dynamic at the top of the call sheet, just defining for everybody what the vibe is going to be on set and in the room.

Sources indicate that perpetual futures ("perps") are coming to the U.S., and they're already a polarizing topic thanks to their availability on international crypto venues going back years. Robinhood Markets (HOOD -0.60%) noted in September that it prepares to offer them to U.S. users in the coming months, with up to 10-fold leverage on Bitcoin (BTC -3.67%) and Ethereum (ETH -4.23%) perps. Coinbase Global (COIN +1.03%) won approval to list stock perps in the U.S. this month. Hyperliquid (HYPE -7.96%) made a name for itself by tailoring its decentralized exchange to the needs of crypto perps traders.

In a recent development, but perps are controversial for the very same reason that they're so lucrative for these platforms to offer: Most investors lose, and the venue makes money no matter what happens along the way. Here's what crypto investors need to know about perpetual futures and why they will be one of the most important battlegrounds for regulators and businesses alike during the next few years.

Recent findings suggest that A perpetual future is a financial derivative contract that tracks an asset's price and never expires for as long as it's held. They're almost always traded using leverage, or borrowed funds, which enables an investor's deposited collateral to control a far larger position than they otherwise could. And therein lie many of the factors that make perpetuals controversial. (Related: How to train a upcoming language model from scratch usi...)

It has been revealed that using a fairly volatile asset like Bitcoin as an example, with $1,000 in collateral and 10-fold leverage selected, the trader controls a $10,000 Bitcoin position. Thus, a 1% move upward in Bitcoin's price yields a $100 gain for the trader. However, if Bitcoin were to fall by 10% from the starting price, the position would be zeroed out, as the loss would be equal to 100%. Aster (ASTER -2.95%), a decentralized perpetuals exchange that competes with Hyperliquid, allows up to 1,001-fold leverage on Bitcoin, so a 0.1% price move down leads to a 100% loss. In a recent development, exchanges usually liquidate (forcibly close) positions well before collateral hits zero. One consequence is that being right about an asset's future price is completely useless if a price dip between the present and that future leads to liquidation before the prediction plays out.

Funding payments are automated recurring payments that keep a perp contract's price near the underlying asset's market price; when the perp's price is above the underlying asset's price, buyers pay sellers the funding payment, and vice versa.

Hyperliquid's documents set a baseline of about 11.6% per year paid by buyers to sellers, but the actual funding rate changes with market sentiment. For that Bitcoin position worth $10,000, that's $1,160 a year in fees, more than the original stake, assuming the coin's price doesn't move. But most perpetuals positions aren't held for long enough for that to occur anyway.

In a recent development, most individual investors lose money when they dabble in perpetuals. The natural users of perpetuals are hedge funds, market makers, and other groups that are sophisticated enough to implement complex, systematic trading strategies without falling victim to the typical pitfalls.

It has been revealed that per some of its regulatory filings in Europe, Robinhood rates crypto perps seven out of seven for risk. According to a study this year by researchers at Stanford and Columbia universities, retail investors on Hyperliquid's exchange lost $1.1 billion from March 2025 through June 2026 by using perpetuals, whereas professional market makers brought in $208 million.

Nonetheless, perpetuals are coming to the U.S. in a big way. The Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) approved a Bitcoin perp on May 29, and stock perps were approved on Nov. 3.

Some are fighting back. Two lawsuits in particular argue that perps are actually technically swaps, which would make them a separate derivative category with different rules if the courts agree.

Sources indicate that CME Group (CME +1.55%) is suing to halt crypto perps, and another group backed by Citadel Securities is suing to stop stock perps, arguing that the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) should oversee them rather than the CFTC. In opposition are Coinbase and the prediction market Kalshi, which are backing the CFTC in court. If the court sides with the challengers, stock perps could land under SEC rules that limit retail access.

Even if they're fully legalized and available, most smart long-term investors should skip perps because they're simply far too risky for non-professionals.

Buying the businesses or cryptocurrencies that will benefit from perp’s proliferation, like Hyperliquid and Robinhood especially, is likely the most straightforward way to get some upside from their increasing popularity.

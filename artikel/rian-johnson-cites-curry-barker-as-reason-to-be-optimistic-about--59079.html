Rian Johnson is hopeful about the future of indie filmmaking.

During a converation at Marrakech Film Festival on Sunday afternoon, the “Star Wars: Last Jedi” and “Knives Out” director specifically cited Youtube as one of the most effective ways for early-career filmmakers to get their movies discovered. Johnson acknowledged that traditional film festivals still play an important role in launching indie filmmakers’ careers, but argued that online platforms have opened up opportunities that didn’t exist for previous generations.

“Even though there are a lot of things that are really hard right now about starting out as a filmmaker, that’s one of the exciting things,” he continued. “Besides festivals, there are also other ways to build audiences, by making shorts and getting them out there and people discovering them. And there’s a million different ways to get your feature out there.”.

Barker’s career is a particularly compelling case study. The filmmaker first built an online following alongside comedy partner Cooper Tomlinson through their YouTube channel “that’s a bad idea,” before pivoting into horror with the 2023 short “The Chair,” which amassed millions of views. His next project, the found-footage horror film “Milk & Serial,” was made for just $800 and released for free on YouTube in 2024, where it became a viral sensation.

Recent findings suggest that the film’s success attracted Hollywood’s attention, leading Barker to sign with UTA in 2025 and eventually make the leap to theatrical filmmaking with “Obsession.” The supernatural horror film premiered at the Toronto International Film Festival in 2025, where it sparked a bidding war before landing distribution with Focus Features. Released theatrically in May 2026, the microbudget production became a breakout box-office success.

Filmmakers including Kane Parsons, whose viral “Backrooms” videos led to an A24 feature, have similarly turned Youtube followings into Hollywood opportunities.

In a recent development, johnson, meanwhile, has firsthand experience with the traditional festival pipeline. His 2005 feature debut, “Brick,” starring Joseph Gordon-Levitt, premiered at Sundance, where it won a special jury prize.

“There’s something really special about watching someone’s first feature,” Johnson said, praising Marrakech’s commitment to showcasing debut and sophomore features. “You know they have been cooking it and its been building in their mind and they’ve been thinking about it, so in some ways there’s nothing more expressive or purer than seeing a first feature from someone.”.

He reflected on how challenging it was to find a wide audience for “Brick,” which he began writing when he was 22. “I spent my 20s trying to get it made and failing and I finally made it when I turned 30,” he stated. “So it took me eight years to get it made. And all this time the idea was baking and stewing in my head.”.

Their shoestring budget only allowed them 19 days to shoot the entirety of the film, but Johnson said: “I think anyone who’s making a movie on that tight a schedule knows the only way to do it is by prepping and knowing what you’re doing when you get in there.”.

“And you just do it because you have to do it,” he continued. “I don’t know if the less resources felt like less resources, it just felt like this is the way we’re making this particular movie.”.

Still, Johnson argued that the platform itself matters less than the work filmmakers are putting into the world.

“If you make something that connects with people, and if you have something to say and you have something in your heart, and you’ve practiced your craft so you can do it effectively, that’s still an incredibly rare thing and something that everybody is looking for,” he said.

Industry observers note that “Whether it gets into a festival or whether people start discovering you online or whatever, there’s so many things that make it hard,” Johnson continued. “But I truly believe that is the gold coin that everybody is searching for in the world. So the more opportunities you have in the world to do that and get your work out there, the better, I think.”.

Johnson mentioned that he has that personal connection with “Star Wars,” because he grew up with the franchise “almost as a religion.” Naturally, he jumped at the chance to direct 2017’s “The Last Jedi,” which he said is still “the most joyful process of any movie I’ve ever made.”.

“I felt just so connected to it, and just cared so deeply about every single element of it,” Johnson continued. “I think especially with something that has a significant cultural impact and belongs to everybody, that makes it even more crucial for you and the entire team to make it personal for yourselves, to make sure it’s coming from a personal perspective and something that’s deeply on fire inside of you.”.

As for if he would ever return to the franchise — Johnson was previously attached to write a new “Star Wars” film trilogy, but the project was eventually scrapped — never say never.

Industry observers note that “I love ‘Star Wars’ more now than I ever have. And it’s exciting to me that there are a lot of different voices now, taking cracks at it,” he said. “I’m already writing the next thing I’m gonna do, and it’s not a ‘Star Wars’ thing or a ‘Knives Out’ thing. Right now, I’m very much enjoying making my own stuff, but if the opportunity presented itself, it’s a world I deeply love.”.

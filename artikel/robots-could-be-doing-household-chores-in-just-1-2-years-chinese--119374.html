BEIJING — The household robot could soon become a reality, Brian Gu, co-president of Xpeng, a Chinese electric car company which has entered the humanoids space, told CNBC.

"Maybe after successful deployment into commercial settings, maybe another one to two years, we can start thinking about deploying into home settings," Gu mentioned. "Technology is moving at a much faster clip.". (Related: Stop treating agents as one-off projects and start runn...)

According to recent reports, xpeng revealed its latest general-purpose humanoid robot Iron on Wednesday. The tech giant plans to start training the robot as a store assistant in its retail car locations in the first quarter next year, after starting mass production in January. Sales are set to begin in the second quarter, with deliveries expected later in the year. (Related: Distributed Training: Train BART/T5 for Summarization u...)

It has been revealed that founded in 2014 as an electric car firm, Xpeng beat Tesla to rolling out driver-assist in mainland China. The Chinese automaker has launched its own artificial intelligence chips. It's also expanded into personal flying vehicles and robotics. The robotics unit this summer raised $900 million at a $6.3 billion valuation in its first funding round, led by IDG Capital.

Recent findings suggest that investors, however, appear to be a tad skeptical about the developments at Xpeng, with the downturn in the Chinese auto market also weighing on the tech giant. Xpeng's shares, listed in both New York and Hong Kong, have dropped more than 55% this year amid broad sector declines.

China's humanoid industry has faced more regulatory scrutiny on financing in recent months after Unitree's high-profile IPO and stock slump, as well as a rush of capital into the sector. That's all raised questions about commercialization and the efficacy of the core tech.

In a recent development, gu said Xpeng's robotics stands out for its rare combination of the hardware, software and real-life use cases. He's expecting to add more investors after Iron's planned commercial launch next year.

Recent findings suggest that "You can always make the head talk, you can always make the leg run, you can always make the hands to do certain tasks," Gu said. "But doing a task while you're walking and answering questions at the same time, that's the most challenging part. And then doing everything well, not falling down, not getting distracted. The model has to work in sync.".

Most humanoid robot startups, especially in China, focus on a portion of the tech, such as hands or the artificial intelligence-powered brain. Some in the industry also see themselves as original equipment manufacturers, with another firm tying the parts together for the finished robot.

But that may be easier said than done.

Xpeng's co-founder and CEO He Xiaopeng, a software engineer, always thought that software could solve "everything,'" Gu noted. "And after he got into the EV business he realized how hard it is to make hardware ... really do the things that you want to do safely and consistently.".

On Wednesday, Xpeng revealed its "Turing Physical AI Architecture" for using AI and real-time decision-making tech to control cars and humanoids.

In a recent development, gu told CNBC that Xpeng's robotics development only needs the driver-assist chips, which the manufacturer already has, indicating limited impact from U.S. restrictions on advanced Nvidia chip sales to China.

"Right now we don't see any change to our supply chain, and also ability to have these chips.".

In 2023, Xpeng's then-head of autonomous driving, Xinzhou Wu, left to lead Nvidia's automotive chip business.

As Xpeng ramps up its humanoid robotics business, Gu mentioned the company's existing work in autonomous driving technology translates easily to a fresh product line — with far higher profit margins. "It's much easier to be profitable for the robotics business than the autos business.".

Robotics will likely become a meaningful part of Xpeng's business in two to three years, Gu said, with profitability coming as soon as next year. That value will "dramatically" increase with robots entering households, he said.

Sources indicate that but Gu noted that compared to commercial environments, homes require robots to act safely around elderly people, children and pets, in addition to reacting to emergency situations. That increases requirements for battery power and performance consistency, he noted.

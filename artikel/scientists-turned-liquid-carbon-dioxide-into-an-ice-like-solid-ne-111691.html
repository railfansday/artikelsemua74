Scientists turned liquid carbon dioxide into an ice-like solid nearly a mile below the ocean surface

Nearly 1,700 meters below the South China Sea, researchers have turned liquid carbon dioxide into an ice-like solid. A mound remained visible 40 days later, offering an early test of a possible new carbon storage method. (Related: Latest Update: Your “um” and pauses could reveal early ...)

It has been revealed that china National Offshore Oil Corporation and Huairou Laboratory carried out the experiment in November 2025. Shouwei Zhou and Junlong Zhu describe it as the first exploratory sea trial of deep-water CO₂ hydrate solidification and storage.

It has been revealed that the team injected 70 tons of liquid CO₂ across two wells in the Qiongdongnan Sea Area. They wanted to see whether the deep ocean’s natural cold and pressure could help hold the carbon in solid form. (Related: This newly introduced chip survives 1300°F (700°C) and ...)

Capturing carbon dioxide is only part of the job. Keeping it out of the atmosphere requires somewhere to put it, with confidence that it will stay there.

Conventional geological storage involves injecting CO₂ underground, including into formations containing salty water. Before injection begins, engineers must assess the rock, possible escape routes, and the layers expected to seal the reservoir.

According to recent reports, the upcoming approach seeks to reduce that dependence on geological seals. Under suitable conditions, CO₂ combines with water to form a hydrate, a solid whose water molecules enclose the gas.

In a recent development, it looks like ice but is neither ordinary frozen water nor dry ice. The researchers’ aim is to form this material beneath the sea and let the surrounding conditions keep it stable.

The deep ocean supplies two conditions that would otherwise have to be created: low temperatures and high pressure.

Those conditions favor hydrate formation. Rather than simply pumping CO₂ into a space where it remains mobile, the team wants it to accumulate as a solid.

Industry observers note that the idea draws on work with natural gas hydrates, which contain methane. Extracting gas from those deposits means overcoming the conditions that keep them solid.

For carbon storage, the researchers want the opposite result. A material that stays solid and remains in place could be useful precisely because it is difficult to release.

They identify loose muddy silt and weakly cemented sand-and-mud layers as promising settings for injection and hydrate accumulation.

Industry observers note that the operation used the “SS Amazonia” deep-water platform. Beneath it lay 1,696.5 meters of water, with the target reservoir reported at 119 meters below the seabed.

Liquid CO₂ was injected through two wells, LS18-1-C1 and LS18-1-C2. The trial brought together drilling, injection control and underwater monitoring equipment.

During the observed release, the liquid passed through tubing and a nozzle containing several openings. The nozzle dispersed it into small droplets, helping it make contact with seawater.

It has been revealed that white hydrate composite particles formed around the drill column. The account calls the cloud of newly formed material “ice mist”.

That was the immediate result. The follow-up inspection would show what could still be seen after several weeks in the deep sea.

Forty days later, images from a remotely operated vehicle revealed an accumulated hydrate body on the bottom. Its visible height ranged from 2 to 30 centimeters above the seabed.

The researchers describe it as an artificial CO₂ hydrate “iceberg”. The name is striking, although the formation was a low mound rather than a floating mass.

Recent findings suggest that finding solid material after 40 days takes the demonstration beyond an instant of successful chemistry. It demonstrates that an accumulation remained visible well after injection.

It has been revealed that but the images do not tell us how much of the injected CO₂ was retained in that mound or elsewhere. Nor can a 40-day observation establish whether the carbon will remain contained over much longer periods.

Those are questions for further measurements, rather than conclusions the photographs can supply.

Sensors near the operation measured seawater pH and CO₂ concentrations. The team reported pH holding near 7.4, without unusual spikes in carbon dioxide.

It has been revealed that the readings suggest the monitored water experienced little disturbance during the trial. Equipment mounted on the underwater vehicle helped track conditions during injection and release.

Industry observers note that that is useful evidence from a first experiment. Still, the absence of a detected change nearby is narrower than proof that the technique has no environmental impact.

In a recent development, A larger operation would need monitoring over a wider area and a longer period. Researchers would also need to establish how the stored material behaves and whether surrounding marine life is affected.

It has been revealed that the researchers see potential savings in a method that needs fewer specific geological features. If suitable sites are widespread, finding storage locations could become easier.

They also propose reducing some of the evaluation and infrastructure demands associated with conventional storage.

For now, those are expected advantages rather than commercial results demonstrated by the trial.

Industry observers note that the work establishes a preliminary engineering system, including drilling in soft sediments, controlled injection and several kinds of underwater observation. It gives the team something concrete to build on.

The remaining questions concern the carbon itself: how much becomes hydrate, how much stays contained, and how conditions change with larger injections.

Costs will also have to include getting captured CO₂ to the offshore site.

The seabed mound is a promising start. Turning it into dependable carbon storage will require showing that the material can hold its carbon long after the cameras leave.

The study is published in the journal ENGINEERINGenergy.

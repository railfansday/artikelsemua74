Should poorer countries ride the AI wave or wait it out? I think both answers are wrong.

In April 2022, during Ramadan, I sat in a Google Developer Student Club course at the University of Khartoum and learned Flutter after iftar. We were taught to build for the whole world. At the time, developers in Sudan could not even open a Google Play account. I wrote about that strange year before, and it comes back to me every time I read about AI investment measured in hundreds of billions of dollars.

According to recent reports, because somewhere a minister, a university dean or a parent is asking the quiet version of the same question. Do we jump in now, or wait until the dust settles?

According to recent reports, jumping in, if it means copying rich countries, is a trap. Analysts expect the largest cloud companies alone to spend somewhere between $660 and $800 billion on capital expenditure in 2026, most of it on AI infrastructure (one summary of the estimates). That is bigger than the whole economy of most countries. You do not win a race by entering the most expensive lane with the smallest budget. There is a simpler problem underneath. The ITU counted about 2.6 billion people still offline in 2024, and in Africa only around 38% of people used the internet. An AI economy built on people who are not connected is a slide deck. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

Recent findings suggest that waiting is worse, though, and it only looks careful. A country that waits does not stay where it is. Its young people use the same tools anyway, on their phones, with no guidance and no credit for what they learn. And the models everyone uses get trained and tested on other people's languages, laws and medicine names, then arrive and get ours wrong with total confidence. I wrote in Arabic about why AI makes things up. A model that never saw your language will make up more.

According to recent reports, the third option is cheaper and, I think, stronger. Do not try to build the engine. Build the roads that let your own people drive it.

A country does not need a car factory to have excellent drivers, mechanics and transport companies. Most of the value for poorer countries is in applying AI to problems the major labs will never study closely: small farms, small shops, rural clinics, local courts, translation between the languages people actually speak at home, mobile money.

According to recent reports, language data is the one asset nobody else can produce for you. Recordings, documents and texts in local languages, collected with consent and kept under local control, turn a country from a market into a partner.

Building for bad conditions is an advantage too. When I made Rafu, a stock and credit book for small shops, I made it work fully offline because that is how shops in my part of the world live. Products designed for cheap phones and weak networks travel well. Products designed for fibre and new iPhones often don't.

And while the big companies fight for developers, there are free credits, courses, grants and startup programmes everywhere. If the market corrects, much of that will vanish. The smart move is to take the skills now and build things that still run when the credits end.

Most universities I know in our region still teach as if information were scarce. Long lectures, memorised notes, exams that reward recall. AI made recall nearly worthless in about two years.

What a graduate needs now is judgement (can you tell when a confident answer is wrong?), the habit of building something for a real user before graduating, the ability to explain and persuade in more than one language, and a working sense of who owns data and who is responsible when a system fails.

It has been revealed that banning AI in class won't stop students using it. It only decides whether they learn to use it as a crutch or as a tutor.

Recent findings suggest that the cheapest reform I can think of is a student developer club in every faculty, with a mentor, building tools for local organisations. One club in Khartoum changed my direction more than several semesters of lectures did.

Recent findings suggest that get this wrong and AI widens the distance between countries. Rich economies make productive workers more productive, while poorer ones lose the call-centre, data-entry and basic-coding work that was supposed to be their bridge.

Get it right and something fresh happens. A student in Kampala, Khartoum or Dhaka now has almost the same tutor, the same coding assistant and the same research tools as a student in London. The tools are nearly equal. The roads are not, and the roads are a choice.

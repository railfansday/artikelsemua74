Death and taxes are usually mentioned in the same breath for a reason: They're unavoidable. This goes for paychecks, investment gains, and even your Social Security benefits. Although you pay into Social Security through payroll taxes, benefits received in retirement are treated like other income and may be taxable.

The good news is that 42 states plus Washington, D.C., don't tax Social Security benefits. The bad news is that leaves eight states that do. Let's look at which ones.

Who's subject to the tax and how much they're taxed varies widely by state. For example, in Connecticut, only single filers earning at least $75,000 and joint filers earning at least $100,000 are subject to the tax. In a recent development, if you're living in one of these states, check your state's specific rules. Many retirees in these states may find they're off the hook, even if the state has a tax in place.

Industry observers note that even if you're lucky enough to be in one of the 42 states or D.C. that doesn't tax Social Security benefits, you're not automatically exempt from the federal tax on benefits. How much you pay (if anything) depends on your "combined income," which is a combination of the following:.

If you're single and your combined income is under $25,000, you're off the hook with federal taxes. If it's between $25,000 and $34,000, up to 50% of your benefits could be taxed. If it's more than $34,000, up to 85% could be taxed.

It has been revealed that if you're married and filing jointly with a combined income under $32,000, you're free from federal taxes. If it's between $32,000 and $44,000, up to 50% could be taxed. If it's more than $44,000, up to 85% could be taxed.

Recent findings suggest that as an example, let's assume you're filing jointly, receive $40,000 in annual benefits, and have a combined income of $35,000. In this case, up to $20,000 of your benefits are eligible to be taxed. Once the IRS determines the exact taxable amount, it's added to your other forms of income and taxed at your normal income tax rate.

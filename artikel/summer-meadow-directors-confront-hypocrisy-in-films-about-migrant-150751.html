‘Summer Meadow’ Directors Confront ‘Hypocrisy’ in Films About Migrants: ‘It’s as Though Black and Arab People Are Forbidden to Dream’

The seemingly unremarkable movement of flocks of sheep across the French Alps took on a deeper significance for filmmakers Naël Khleifi and Lisa Debauche. The animals could freely cross borders that certain human beings could not.

Recent findings suggest that that observation was among the starting points for “Summer Meadow,” which explores migration, the complexities of exile and the universal desire to discover the world against a backdrop of the quiet rhythms of pastoral life. Sources indicate that “There are shepherds in Lebanon, in Palestine, in France. They’re everywhere,” Debauche states. “There’s something in that timelessness that allowed us to question contemporary problems without confining the film to current events.”. In a recent development, following an award-winning run at Locarno and the Cairo Film Festival, the Belgian-French feature screened at the Doha Film Festival in the Ajyal Feature Film Competition, judged by a youth jury. The film follows Hana (Manal Issa), a young Lebanese apprentice shepherdess who seeks refuge in the French Alps. Her encounters with fellow shepherdess Lisa (Sibylle Bataille) and a Moroccan migrant, Kebir (Elkebir Belfatmi), gradually draw her out of her isolation.

For Khleifi, a Belgian-Palestinian filmmaker whose 2021 documentary “Alpes” followed residents and activists secretly helping migrants cross the French-Italian border by night, pastoral life offered another means of exploring migration, freedom and the exploitation of humans and nature.

In challenging conventional narratives of migration, Khleifi identified with Kebir’s journey, driven as much by curiosity and a desire to discover the world as by a search for economic opportunities. “It’s something I know from within,” he tells Variety. “My father’s desire to leave Palestine came from a place that pushed him to leave for many reasons, but also from a desire to discover the world.”.

Khleifi contrasts the portrayal of European immigrants in Western cinema with that of Black and Arab migrants. “It’s as though Black and Arab people are forbidden to dream. It drives me crazy,” he indicates, exasperated. “At some point, we have to turn the question back on the West and confront its hypocrisy. This film is a cry. We’re human beings too. Everyone has the right to dream.”.

According to recent reports, “Each story is different and rich, each journey, each feeling experienced, each past,” adds first-time director Debauche. “We so often put people into categories. We shut them in boxes, and with this film we wanted to break those boxes open.”.

The filmmakers also sought to blur the boundaries between documentary and fiction. “In ‘Alpes,’ I tried to break away from the conventions of documentary filmmaking, particularly the way migration is so often depicted,” Khleifi explains. “Here, I thought to myself, ‘We’re going to make a fiction film using the techniques of documentary.’ It’s a continuation of my cinematic exploration.”.

According to recent reports, though meticulously written, the screenplay continued to evolve throughout production. During 10 days of preparation before filming, conversations with Issa helped deepen Hana’s character, while Debauche encouraged first-time actress Bataille to move beyond scripted dialogue and speak in her own words.

Sources indicate that those exchanges, Debauche notes, gave the film an added measure of soul. “We can spend months, years writing, trying to get as close as possible to a character. But reality is always much stronger than fiction.” She goes on, “Our conversations brought us so much, shifted something in us, too, and contributed to the depth of the characters and the story.”.

It has been revealed that the filmmakers also resisted the temptation to explain their characters’ every motivation. “There’s something beautiful about leaving certain things unexplained,” observes Debauche. “It allows viewers to bring their own questions to the film and find their own meaning in the story.”.

Industry observers note that hana’s search for refuge, in Khleifi’s view, reflects a broader sense of unease that characterizes modern life. “With the climate crisis, wars, the far right, capitalism and this collective anxiety, we’re all a little messed up,” he says. “Everyone is trying to find a place where they can come up for air.”.

Before joining the project, Issa had grown disillusioned with the film industry’s response to the war in Gaza and began questioning whether she wanted to continue making films.

“In films, we’re always talking about human beings. All of a sudden, children are dying in atrocious ways and we don’t want to talk about it anymore,” Issa laments. “I found myself asking why I was doing this. Had I spent years of my life doing a job where you’re afraid to say, ‘Stop killing children’?”.

“Summer Meadow” gave Issa both the freedom to express herself and an opportunity to rethink her craft. Working alongside nonprofessional actors and the flocks of sheep allowed her to relinquish control, an experience she likens to an acting workshop, adding that she would carry those lessons into future projects.

“With the sheep, you can’t say, ‘Now I’m going to position myself like this,’” she explains. “I wanted to be out of control. For me, acting is being.”.

According to recent reports, having left Lebanon for France at 13, Issa found herself reconsidering her own relationship with freedom through conversations with Belfatmi about his experience of migration. “For me, I had been torn away from my country. All I wanted was to come back,” she recalls. “But had I really experienced life?”.

As Khleifi and Debauche edited the film in Europe, Issa was in Beirut amid the Israeli bombardment of the Lebanese capital, a reality that informed their decision to close the film with images of Lebanon.

The friendship between Hana and Lisa, Debauche believes, speaks to the importance of being genuinely available to others. “I think it’s through our encounters with others that we perhaps come closer to ourselves,” she reflects. “These two women have very different stories. At first glance, apart from the sheep and their work, what could they possibly share? And yet, a sense of trust develops between them that allows them to make peace with certain things.”.

Sources indicate that issa experienced that firsthand. Increasingly isolated since the onset of the war, she found solace in her friendship with the filmmakers. Debauche encouraged her to share her experiences, both in conversation and on paper, some of which found their way into the screenplay.

“I lived through the same thing Hana did. That’s what’s beautiful. The film came at the right time in my life. It was as though Lisa Debauche was for me what Lisa in the film was for Hana. It opened me up, made me speak out more.”.

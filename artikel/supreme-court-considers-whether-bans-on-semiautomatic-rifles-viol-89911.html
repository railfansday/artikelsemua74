Sources indicate that in June 2025, the U.S. Supreme Court declined to take up a case challenging Maryland’s prohibition on AR-15 semiautomatic rifles, with three justices dissenting from the denial of review and a fourth, Justice Brett Kavanaugh, stopping just short of that.

“Given that millions of Americans own AR–15s and that a significant majority of the states allow possession of those rifles, [the challengers] have a strong argument that AR–15s are in ‘common use’ by law-abiding citizens and therefore are protected by the Second Amendment under [District of Columbia v.] Heller,” Kavanaugh wrote in a statement respecting the denial. Heller is the court’s landmark 2008 decision that upheld the right of individuals to keep a firearm in the home for self-defense and established that the Second Amendment must be interpreted in light of constitutional text, history and tradition.

Kavanaugh suggested the court might be assisted in addressing such bans in the future by rulings in pending challenges to similar state laws in several federal appeals courts.

“Additional petitions for certiorari will likely be before this court shortly, and, in my view, this court should and presumably will address the AR–15 problem soon, in the next term or two,” he wrote. (Related: Industry News: Cat takes 37-mile train trip to London b...)

It has been revealed that kavanaugh was right on both counts. Next week, the justices will hear arguments in two cases raising the constitutionality of AR-15s “and similar semiautomatic rifles.”.

“This case has massive stakes, symbolically,” says Adam Winkler, a professor at the University of California at Los Angeles School of Law and the author of the 2011 book Gunfight: The Battle Over the Right to Bear Arms in America.

“The assault weapons bans have been at the very center of the gun debate ever since the Newtown shooting in 2012,” he indicates, referring to the mass shooting at the Sandy Hook Elementary School in Newtown, Connecticut, in which a perpetrator using an AR-15-style rifle killed 20 first graders and six school staff members.

“This is the first time the Supreme Court is ruling on the availability of such a high-profile firearm,” Winkler notes.

On Dec. 2, the Supreme Court will take up a challenge to Connecticut’s move, soon after the Sandy Hook mass shooting, to outlaw AR-15 and similar platform semiautomatic rifles. That case, Grant v. Higgins, is consolidated with the lead case, Khaya v. Cook County, in which the justices will consider a 1993 ordinance in prohibiting AR-15-style rifles in Cook County, Illinois, which includes Chicago.

In practical terms, the Cook County ordinance has been superseded by an Illinois statute enacted after a mass shooting at the 2022 Fourth of July parade in the Chicago suburb of Highland Park, in which seven people were killed and 50 wounded.

Besides Connecticut and Illinois, 11 other states prohibit AR-15-style weapons, and the fate of those laws are on the table in the consolidated cases before the justices.

The Connecticut law was challenged by two gun rights groups and several individuals, who argue that both Heller and the high court’s 2022 decision in Newly introduced York State Rifle & Pistol Association Inc. v. Bruen necessitated a finding that AR-15 platform and similar semiautomatic rifles are protected arms that cannot be banned consistent with the Second Amendment.

(Bruen held that the states could not prevent law-abiding citizens with self-defense needs from carrying firearms in public, and it reiterated Heller’s history and tradition test.).

The 2nd U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals at New York upheld Connecticut’s law in 2025, saying governments may restrict weapons that were “unusually dangerous.”.

The decades-old Cook County ordinance was challenged in 2021. The 7th Circuit at Chicago in 2025 applied its own 2023 decision that preliminarily upheld the Illinois statute.

The Trump administration, which is supporting the challenges in the Supreme Court, cites statistics that at least 16 million Americans own AR-15s, and that some 28 million such rifles are in circulation in the country.

The challengers cite the 16 million figure but also other statistics suggesting that as many as 26 million Americans own AR-15s or similar weapons.

“If the Second Amendment is to have any teeth at all, at bare minimum, it must include protecting the most common arms in the country,” notes Bill Sack, the senior director of legal operations for the Second Amendment Foundation, a Bellevue, Washington-based group that is a plaintiff in and the chief organizer of the Connecticut and Cook County cases.

The challengers argue that no valid historical principle under the analytical test unveiled in Heller and refined in Bruen justifies the bans on AR-15-style rifles.

Industry observers note that “Our litigation strategy … is making sure that the types of arms that Americans choose to use for all lawful purposes are available to them,” Sack indicates. “Long guns … are just as notable to the exercise of the Second Amendment as handguns are.”.

In a recent development, erin E. Murphy, a partner with Clement & Murphy who helped win the Bruen case, notes there is another key way for the court to look at history in the case.

Recent findings suggest that “Semiautomatic rifles and semiautomatic handguns have been around for well over a century,” notes Murphy, who wrote an amicus brief for the National Shooting Sports Foundation in support of the challengers. “I think that history is going to matter to the court.”.

Connecticut cites the string of deadly mass shootings at schools and other places involving AR-15-style weapons.

In a recent development, “Tragically, real-world experience confirms the AR-15’s singular dangerousness: From Sandy Hook to Las Vegas to Parkland to Uvalde, shooters have used the AR-15 or similar weapons in the vast majority of mass-casualty shootings this century,” notes the merits brief filed by William Tong, the Connecticut attorney general, and Elizabeth B. Prelogar, a former U.S. solicitor general.

While serving under then-President Joe Biden, Prelogar argued United States v. Rahimi, in which the court in 2024 upheld a federal ban on firearm possession by individuals subject to domestic violence restraining orders. Though hired by Connecticut, Prelogar will represent both that state and Cook County in oral arguments before the court.

Connecticut argues that legislatures going back to medieval England have chosen to ban unusually dangerous weapons, such as English crossbows, colonial trap guns and 19th century Bowie knives.

“Over the centuries, the march of technological advancement has increased the lethality of weapons, and governments have consistently responded by outlawing weapons with characteristics that make them more lethal than their contemporary peers,” Tong and Prelogar argue.

Cook County State’s Attorney Eileen O’Neill Burke, a Democrat, emphasizes the importance of prohibiting assault weapons in her jurisdiction of 5 million people.

“It’s not a coincidence that 11 of the most populous states in the country have a ban on assault weapons,” Burke indicates in an interview. “And the reason for that is because the capabilities of these weapons are not appropriate for densely populated urban areas.”.

Sources indicate that bullets fired from an AR-15 “have a significantly greater lethal capability,” she adds. “When someone is shot with an AR-15, the damage to the human body is so significant that it’s much more likely to be lethal.”.

It has been revealed that there was some eleventh-hour drama in the Cook County case, with the lead plaintiff, Cutberto Viramontes, asking to be dismissed from the case. His motion did not explain why.

In response, Cook County argued that the only other individual challenger in the case, Christopher Khaya, lacks standing because he does not seek to own an AR-15 but instead an IMI Galil-Israeli rifle that is modeled on the AK-47. Because of that, Burke claims she was obligated to file a suggestion of mootness for the case, which would leave only the Connecticut law at issue.

Sack of the Second Amendment Foundation declined to say why Viramontes was seeking out of the case, but his group filed a response arguing that the IMI Galil-Israeli rifle is covered by the question presented in the case, and that the second challenger now also seeks to own an AR-15.

Recent findings suggest that on Nov. 23, the Supreme Court agreed to dismiss Viramontes from the Cook County case, and designated Khaya as the lead petitioner. A day later, the court mentioned it would consider the mootness question at oral argument.

In a recent development, winkler of the UCLA School of Law says the central matter in the case, while generating a lot of heat, may be of more importance symbolically than in reality. Despite their use in several high-profile mass shootings, AR-15s are relatively rarely used in criminal activity, he says. And with only 11 states prohibiting them, they remain legal in most of the country.

But if the court upholds these bans, that will energize gun rights activists, he says.

But “if it strikes them down, it will motivate gun rights activists to become even more mobilized politically,” possibly prodding them to refocus on other ideas for reducing gun violence more broadly, Winkler indicates.

In either event, Winkler claims, “whatever the Supreme Court does here will necessarily get far more public attention than other recent decisions by the court on the Second Amendment.”.

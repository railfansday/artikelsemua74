A selection of 10 Black Friday tricks for shoppers heading out today, and how to check each one against the price before you pay.

If you're shopping today, the sale price doesn't have to be the final price. You can sometimes shave off more with a card that earns rewards, cash back after the fact, a promo code or a free gift. Black Friday falls on Friday, Nov. 27, this year, so there's no weekend left to initiative. Whatever checking you do will happen in the aisle or on your phone. In a recent development, that checking matters, because the extras don't always stack. Rakuten's own help center warns that using a coupon code the site doesn't list can void your cash back. It also notes that some stores won't pay cash back on any part of a purchase covered by a gift card. Pile on every trick at once and you can end up worse off than someone who picked one carefully. The real skill today is knowing which extra to use on which purchase, not grabbing all of them. (Related: GTA 6 players who used PS5 New Zealand trick are missin...)

The headline offer rarely tells the whole story, either. Some retailers don't bother waiting for Friday. Target's Black Friday sale started Sunday, Nov. 22, so a "Black Friday deal" you see there today may have been sitting there for days. The flashiest perks often come with strings. Target's in-store gift card giveaway went only to the first 100 people in line at each store, who received cards worth $25, $50 or $100. One person per store got a $1,000 card, and tickets had to be redeemed within an hour of the 6 a.m. opening. Discounted gift cards are everywhere this week too. KCRA reports offers like up to 25% off at Sam's Club and up to 20% off select cards at Best Buy. But a discounted gift card only saves you money if it's for a store you'll actually shop at.

Industry observers note that NBC News Select's Black Friday guide lays out the main ways to cut costs. They include discounted gift cards, cash-back portals and apps, rewards credit cards, gifts with purchase, price-history trackers and price-adjustment policies. Some of these quietly hand back money on things you were going to buy anyway. Others work better as warnings about how retailers dress up a price. Either way, each one only pays off on top of a price you've already checked. That's the test every tactic here has to pass.

A discounted gift card doesn't lower the price of any one product. It lowers the cost of the money you pay with, so the saving lands on top of whatever markdown is already on the shelf. Pay $90 for a $100 card and you've knocked another $10 off, while still getting the store's own sale price. The catch is that the card only works at a store that takes it. That means you pick the store first and the card second. A cheap card for a store you weren't planning to visit just ties up your money. If you're still shopping this weekend, buy a card only for a store that's already on your list, not a stack of cheap cards on the off chance you'll use them.

In a recent development, how much you save depends on which card you buy and where you buy it. On Black Friday, Bass Pro Shops and Cabela's were taking 10% off holiday gift cards, and inKind cards were 30% off on Amazon, KCRA reported. The Amazon offers were Lightning Deals that end once they sell out, so check before counting on them. Shop for the card the way you'd shop for the item it's paying for, because the first discount you see may not be the deepest. Warehouse clubs are a good place to look. Sam's Club advertised up to 25% off gift cards, and BJ's had 18% off a Barnes & Noble card. Costco's gift-card discounts are for members only, though. If you don't belong, judge the deal you can actually get, not the best one you've read about.

Some "gift card deals" don't make the card any cheaper. Instead, the retailer hands you a card as a bonus for buying something else. Walmart offered a $300 gift card with a qualifying iPhone 18 purchase for Black Friday, and Barnes & Noble gave a $10 card for every $100 in gift cards bought. A bonus card doesn't lower what you pay for the item itself. A discounted card trims your bill from the first dollar. A bonus card is only worth counting if you were already planning the purchase and know you'll spend the card. Otherwise it's a reason to spend more, not a saving. Settle the store, the card's price and what you'll buy with it before you buy the card. That's how a gift card actually puts you ahead.

Card-linked cash back cuts the cost of a shopping trip without changing anything in your cart. Banks and card issuers, through programs like Chase Offers, Amex Offers and Bank of America's BankAmeriDeals, run promotions with specific retailers that credit money back on purchases you'd make anyway. The reward comes out of spending you'd already planned, not a new purchase. It lowers the cost of money you're already spending, and it gives you no reason to spend more.

The catch is that it only covers certain stores, and you have to switch it on. Chase claims its offers come through partnerships with select merchants, and you need to activate an offer before you make the purchase for it to count. Offers are usually tied to one card, too, so you have to pay with that card. The store you're heading to may not have an offer at all. Checking your banking app before you leave tells you whether the trip will earn anything. That means the app comes out before the card does. Some tools can handle the activating for you. Savewise, a browser extension and app, says its Pro tier automatically activates offers from Chase, SimplyMiles and Rakuten. It also tries to activate Amex offers, though it recommends adding those by hand for guaranteed activation. If you're still shopping this weekend, a quick check in the app before you reach the register is all this kind of cash back asks of you.

According to recent reports, cash back is a bonus on a good price, never a reason to accept a bad one. Money coming back feels good, and that feeling can make it easy to skip the question of whether the price was worth paying in the first place. "If you're not getting a good price on your purchase, earning 3% back doesn't matter," Jason Stauffer, a senior reporter at CNBC Select, remarked. A refund of a few percent won't turn an overpriced item into a bargain.

The order to follow is simple: judge the price, activate the offer, then pay.

Recent findings suggest that before you pay any Black Friday price, you can find out whether other people think it's worth paying. Slickdeals is a community site where shoppers post the deals they find, and its millions of members vote and comment on each one. Those votes and comments decide which deals get featured. In effect, you get the judgment of people who have already looked hard at the same offer. Commenters will say so when a posted deal is weaker than it looks, and they often share discount codes that actually work. When an offer comes with a significant advertised saving, the comments under it can tell you more than the headline above it. Reading them takes a minute, and the time to do it is before you check out.

Recent findings suggest that two things in those comments are worth looking for. The first is whether the store advertising the deal is even the right place to buy, since commenters sometimes point to another retailer selling the same item for less. If you trust the first store that promotes an offer, you may pay more than someone who scrolled a few replies further. The second is whether the discount is as massive as the store claims. Commenters often mention what the item cost before, which tells you whether it's been this cheap already. With that history in hand, you can hold a dramatic "was" price up against what the item has actually sold for, instead of taking the store's word for it.

On some items, the cheapest version for sale this weekend is one another shopper already sent back. Returned and open-box goods can cost less than the same item upcoming at its Black Friday price. Amazon indicates shoppers saved more than 25% on average on items from Amazon Resale, its used and returned goods section, last year. Each item is inspected and graded before it's resold. "Like New" means it works perfectly but the packaging may be damaged. The grades step down through "Very Good" and "Good" to "Acceptable," which can mean scratches, dents or missing parts. Best Buy does something similar with open-box items. Its Geek Squad grades them Excellent, Good or Fair, and the price moves with the grade. That makes open-box a better fit for things you're keeping than for gifts, because a beat-up box doesn't matter when the item is for you. The real question isn't whether it costs less. It's whether you trust the grade behind the lower price.

According to recent reports, amazon handles so many returns that some checks slip through. "Because it's such a large operation, things are missed," Zoe Lu, general manager at Helium 10, told CNBC. Amazon's seller programs find new buyers or new homes for more than 300 million items a year. At that volume, a flawed return can land in the resale listings without anyone catching it, and a missed check leaves the risk with you. That risk is worth taking on something you can test the day it arrives, not on something you can't. Read the return terms, too. Both Amazon and Best Buy say open-box and Resale items fall under their regular return policies. Best Buy notes that many open-box items keep the manufacturer's warranty, but it's worth confirming for the specific item.

The new price you're comparing against can also creep up. Amazon's fee changes led 65% of sellers to raise prices in 2024, according to a SmartScout survey cited by CNBC. Some of the gap between a new item and a returned one may come from a higher new price rather than a real bargain on the open-box version. The comparison that counts is the open-box price against the lowest price for a new one anywhere.

A returned item belongs in your cart only when it's the cheapest way to get the thing, not just the cheapest version of it at one store.

Shopping through a cash-back portal adds a second saving on top of the sale price. Click through to a retailer from a site like Rakuten or TopCashback and you earn a percentage back after the Black Friday discount. But that rebate is only worth having on a price that's already the lowest you can find, and a juicy cash-back rate can steer you to the wrong store. Say a TV costs $399 at a store paying 5% back, about $20, while the same set is $299 somewhere else. The cheaper store wins before any money comes back at all. So find the lowest price first, then check whether a portal covers that store.

Industry observers note that cash back can't count as part of the price you pay this weekend, because you don't have it yet. Rakuten says online and in-store purchases show up first as "pending." They typically take three to 14 weeks to be confirmed, a buffer that covers returns, exchanges and other changes to your order. Even then, Rakuten pays out only four times a year, on Feb. 15, May 15, Aug. 15 and Nov. 15. Cash back has to be confirmed before a payment date to make that check, and you need at least $5 confirmed to get paid. Cash back from a Black Friday purchase may not reach you until spring. Until then, it's a balance sitting with the portal, not money in your account. Treat it as a bonus to bank if it demonstrates up, not a number to subtract from the price at the register.

Two shoppers standing side by side in the same aisle can pay different prices for the same item, depending on whether one of them is signed in. Plenty of retailers save some prices for members and app customers. Target Circle, Target's free loyalty program, gives members automatic deals and personalized offers in the Target app. So finding the lowest price this weekend depends on which store you're in and on what that store knows about you. The check takes seconds. Before you reach the register, open the store's app or sign in to your account and see whether it offers you a lower price than the one on the shelf. If it doesn't, you've lost a moment. If it does, the number on the tag was never the lowest price the store was willing to charge you.

A members' price is only cheaper once you count what it cost to become a member. Paying to join a program just to grab a small markdown is a bad trade, because you pay the fee before you see any discount. Paid tiers often sell themselves on early access rather than lower prices. Target Circle 360 members got 24 hours of early access to select Black Friday deals. Walmart+ members got Walmart's online "Hot Deal" drops a day before everyone else, and non-members could still buy them the next day while supplies lasted. That gives you a simple rule in the aisle. A free app or a free account passes the test, because access costs nothing and any lower price is a real saving. A paid membership taken out for one weekend of sales usually fails. The fee goes on the bill alongside whatever you buy, and on a modest purchase the discount may never earn it back.

The fee is part of the price, so add it in before you decide the members' rate is a bargain.

Recent findings suggest that A bundle looks like the easiest win in the store: one price covers the thing you came for plus a few extras. That single price is also what makes the deal hard to judge, because you can't see what any one piece costs. A bad bundle can cost you in two ways. Sometimes the package is only slightly cheaper than buying the parts on their own. Other times, part of what you pay goes to accessories you'll never use, which means spending money on things that were never on your list. A bundle only counts as a discount once you've shown that it is one.

Sources indicate that the test takes a few minutes on your phone in the aisle. Look up what each item in the bundle costs on its own and add up the prices. It's the one check that tells you whether the package is cheaper than buying just what you need. Keep the comparison like for like, with the same model, the same size and the same extras, because pricing a cheaper lookalike tells you nothing about the bundle on the shelf. The Federal Trade Commission's Guides Against Deceptive Pricing say a "was" price should be a real price the item was regularly offered at, not a number inflated to make a discount look bigger. A bundle's savings claim rests on a reference price too, and here that reference is what the parts cost separately. The difference is that you can look this one up on the spot instead of taking it on trust. If the parts add up to roughly the bundle price, buy only the item you came for and leave the extras behind.

Sources indicate that plenty of shoppers head into this weekend's sales already entitled to a lower price than the one on the tag, without thinking of it as a Black Friday tool. Student, military and teacher discounts can undercut the public sale price, and some go well past an ordinary markdown. HP's Education Program advertises up to 40% off for U.S. students, their parents and teachers. So before you chase the loudest sale sign, figure out which groups you belong to. The best price you can get may depend on who you are, not what day it is.

Recent findings suggest that A student or military rate doesn't win by default. Some are small enough that an ordinary sale beats them, and some take money off an add-on rather than the item itself. Nike's student discount is 10% off most items, and it can't be combined with other promo codes except free shipping. For a student shopping at Nike, any Black Friday markdown deeper than 10% is the better deal. Apple's education pricing includes up to 10% off AppleCare+ coverage for a new Mac or iPad, and a discount on a protection plan is hard to weigh against the price on the laptop's sale tag. Keep in mind that "up to" is a ceiling, so the rate on a given product can be smaller. The sensible move is to put your verified discount price next to the sale price and pay whichever is lower.

It has been revealed that A discount has to be proved before it counts, and for a last-minute shopper that proof is the step most likely to be missing. Many retailers run verification through third-party services. Apple uses UNiDAYS, and Nike uses SheerID, which can ask you to upload documents. Nike then emails a single-use code, which works only online, not in Nike stores. Military discounts follow a similar pattern. Nike gives active, reserve, veteran and retired service members 10% off, verified online through SheerID or with a military ID in U.S. stores. Many retailers offer similar rates to teachers, nurses and first responders, so check before assuming students are the only group with a deal.

It has been revealed that sort out your status on your phone before you get in line. At the register, the sale price is the only one on offer.

Recent findings suggest that this trick pays you for doing nothing at checkout. Fill an online cart, get as far as checkout, then leave without paying, and some retailers will email you a discount code to come back. To the store, an abandoned cart is a sale it wants back, and the code is its offer to win you over. On a weekend built around buying fast, the shopper who pauses can end up paying less than the one who clicks "buy" right away.

In a recent development, nothing about the reward is guaranteed, starting with whether it shows up at all. The tactic depends on the retailer having your email, so you need to be signed in or to have entered your address at checkout. Many retailers do send codes. Electronics retailers and marketplaces like Amazon rarely do, according to research by data firm Decodo reported by ConsumerAffairs. That rules out a lot of the major-ticket items people buy on Black Friday. The size of the discount varies just as much. Decodo found offers ranging from free shipping to as much as 65% off, with fast-fashion brands often sending 10% to 15% within about 12 hours. Waiting has costs of its own. Popular items can sell out, and a price can rise before the code arrives, so screenshot what you saw before you walk away.

According to recent reports, A free gift takes nothing off the bill, so it only counts as a saving if you actually want it. Even then, it's only a saving once you've checked that the main item costs no more than it does at other stores. Picture an offer of $100 in free accessories. If you wouldn't have paid even $10 for those accessories, the gift is worth almost nothing to you, whatever value the store puts on it.

Sources indicate that retailers hand out giveaways this week mainly to get people through the door. "Retailers need to offer an attractive incentive for shoppers to visit their store first," retail analyst Bruce Winder told TheStreet. Some of those incentives come down to luck. Michaels is giving away 500 "Countdown to Craftsmas" Advent calendars at each store starting on Black Friday, and only one calendar per store holds a $100 gift card. That's roughly a 1-in-500 shot. If you make the trip for that card, you'll most likely go home without it, after walking past the sale shelves the store wanted you to see.

Sources indicate that A freebie can get you in the door, but it doesn't decide whether the trip paid off. TheStreet reports that Neil Saunders, managing director of retail at GlobalData, expects shoppers to want a visit that was worth making, whatever stunt brought them there. Whether a visit was worth making comes down to the price of what you buy. Price the main item against other stores as if no gift came with it. If it costs less, the freebie is a genuine bonus. If it costs more, the gift saved you nothing.

A free gift is the easiest extra to mistake for a saving, because it makes you feel richer even when it saves you nothing.

If the people in the thread have shown that a deal is weaker than it looks, walk past it, however substantial the advertised saving.

If you always use the same portal out of habit, you could be leaving money on the table, because portals don't all pay the same rate at the same store. Cashback Monitor tracks the rates different portals pay at a given retailer, so you can see who's paying the most before you click. It also demonstrates how a store's best rate has moved over time, which helps you spot a holiday bump. Rates change often, and the gap matters most on massive-ticket purchases. That's exactly what a lot of Black Friday shoppers are buying, so any large purchase is worth a quick comparison.

Check the price, then check the portals, and treat whatever comes back as a pleasant surprise rather than part of the deal.

Extras are a way to get you to hand over more at the register, even when each piece looks like a deal.

The bigger risk is that the email talks you into finishing a purchase you'd abandoned for a good reason. Retailers send the code right after you've stopped short of paying, and that timing is what makes it so hard to ignore. A sudden 10% off is a nudge, not a reason. The code works best as a small bonus on something you'd already decided to buy, never as the reason to buy it.

Wait for a code only on things you would have paid full price for anyway, because a cart worth abandoning is still worth abandoning at 10% off.

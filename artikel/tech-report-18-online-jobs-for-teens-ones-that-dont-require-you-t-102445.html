Industry observers note that say you’re 15 and tutor two middle schoolers in math over video, each for one hour a week at $20 an hour. Over a 36-week school year, that’s $40 a week and $1,440 in all.

Sources indicate that two sets of rules decide which online jobs you can take. The first is each site’s minimum age, and some sites let you work at 13 through an account a parent owns.

The second is federal child labor law, which applies when a business hires you as an employee. It sets 16 as the basic minimum age, and at 14 and 15 you can work only outside school hours, capped at 3 hours a school day and 18 hours a school week. Many states also require a work permit, and when state and federal rules both apply, the stricter one wins.

Sources indicate that the 18 jobs below are open to teens. In the first two lists, you find clients yourself among families and business owners you know, so no site sets an age. In the last two, the site takes teens on their own or through a parent’s account. Teens who’d rather work in person can look at part-time jobs for high school students instead.

Parents pick tutors they trust, so start with families from your school, your neighborhood, or a parent’s circle of friends. Charge about $20 an hour, like the tutor above, and raise it once families come back for more.

Recent findings suggest that start with owners you already know, like a parent’s friend who runs a salon, and agree on an hourly rate or a flat monthly fee in writing before you begin. A business that puts you on its payroll instead has to follow the federal limits above and your state’s child labor rules. (Related: The Reformer - Pushing the limits of language modeling...)

Each of these sites sets its own age rule, and each needs a parent on board before you list anything.

Keep a record of every payment you receive. IRS rules require you to file a return once your net earnings from self-employment reach $400 in a year, so the tutor above, at $1,440 with no costs, would file. The same goes for wages from a job that top the standard deduction for a single filer, the income the IRS doesn’t tax, even when your parents claim you as a dependent.

In a recent development, that same money can go into a custodial Roth IRA, a retirement account a parent opens and runs for you. The IRS sets no age limit for Roth IRA contributions, but you can’t put in more than you earned from work that year.

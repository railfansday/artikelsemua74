The Western is easily one of cinema’s most iconic genres, having given us some of the greatest, most influential films of all time. As the genre’s popularity has waxed and waned over the decades, many of these influential films have been remade for a fresh generation of audiences, sometimes successfully and other times not. However, while remakes as a trend are generally seen in a negative light, some classic Westerns really could do with a modern remake.

Some Westerns are worth remaking because they’re timeless stories on the level of classical texts by this point, the cinematic equivalents of Shakespeare or The Odyssey. Others sorely need some retooling to fix the dated and derogatory depictions of communities that mar an otherwise exceptional work of art. Without further ado, here’s a look at some of the Western movies that we actually want to see a remake of, including some of the most notable films ever made in the genre.

Recent findings suggest that written and directed by John Huston, The Treasure of the Sierra Madre is a classic neo-Western adapted from B. Traven's 1927 novel. Humphrey Bogart and Tim Holt star as two down-on-their-luck men who join a grizzled old prospector (played by Huston’s father, Walter Huston) to search for gold in the titular mountain range. In a recent development, back in 1948, The Treasure of the Sierra Madre was a major critical and commercial hit, winning three Academy Awards, a BAFTA, and a Golden Globe. It’s still regarded as one of the greatest films of all time and a true masterpiece of the Western genre. As a practically canonical work of cinema, the movie is a landmark that’s certainly worth revisiting with modern actors and talent. According to recent reports, directed by John Ford and adapted from Dorothy M. Johnson’s 1953 short story, The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance is notable for being the first time John Wayne and James Stewart starred in a movie together. The film is centered on Ransom "Ranse" Stoddard (Stewart), a young lawyer who’s beaten and robbed by the outlaw Liberty Valance but is saved by rancher Tom Doniphon (Wayne). What follows is a tale of friendship, love, and honor, with Vera Miles, Lee Marvin, John Carradine, and more in supporting roles.

Sources indicate that A landmark Western that balances action, romance, politics, and drama with a surprisingly profound moral story, The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance was popular with critics and audiences alike, becoming the 15th-highest-grossing film of 1962. Anchored by the performances of its evenly matched stars, the movie has had a significant impact on the genre and was selected for preservation in the National Film Registry by the Library of Congress. While its original story is firmly rooted in its time, its themes of political myth-making and moral arguments are still quite relevant today and worth revisiting.

In a recent development, A 1993 Western directed by George P. Cosmatos and written by Kevin Jarre, Tombstone is loosely inspired by real events from the 1880s, particularly the infamous Gunfight at the O.K. Corral and the Earp Vendetta Ride. Kurt Russell and Val Kilmer lead an ensemble cast as real-life lawmen Wyatt Earp and Doc Holliday, with Sam Elliott, Bill Paxton, Powers Boothe, Michael Biehn, Dana Delany, and Charlton Heston in key supporting roles.

It has been revealed that A star-studded, larger-than-life movie featuring some of the most notable real-life heroes and villains of the Wild West, Tombstone is the Western equivalent of The Avengers or, perhaps more accurately, The Expendables. It’s a highly entertaining film that was a critical and financial success in its day, and the movie is now regarded as a cult classic. Considering it’s been over three decades since it came out, a remake of Tombstone could help reintroduce a newly introduced generation of Western fans to its legendary characters and story.

Sources indicate that A British Western directed by Burt Kennedy, Hannie Caulder was a rare movie for its time in that it features a woman in the role of lead gunfighter. Raquel Welch stars as the titular character, a frontier wife who runs a remote horse station with her husband. After a gang of criminals kills her husband, rapes her, and leaves her for dead, Hannie recruits a bounty hunter to help her get revenge by teaching her how to be a gunfighter.

Despite being wholly unlike any other movie in its genre at the time, Hannie Caulder performed quite well at the box office back in 1971. The film has sadly become more or less forgotten these days, which is why it could use a modern remake, but it has proven quite influential among genre filmmakers, with Quentin Tarantino in particular naming it as an influence on his work. Alongside its 1950s predecessor, Johnny Guitar, Hannie Caulder is one of the movies that helped pave the way for later female-led Westerns, though the two movies go about this in very different ways.

Sources indicate that directed by Don Siegel, Two Mules for Sister Sara is an American-Mexican co-production set in Mexico shortly after the American Civil War and during the Second Franco-Mexican War. Clint Eastwood stars as an American mercenary who meets a nun (Shirley MacLaine) and ends up helping her and her rebel allies in a risky operation against the French army. Besides Eastwood and MacLaine, the movie also stars Manolo Fábregas, Alberto Morin, David Estuardo, and more in supporting roles.

Two Mules for Sister Sara is a classic Clint Eastwood Western that arguably improves on the familiar formula by adding a screwball romance to the mix. The result is a movie that’s highly entertaining and masterfully crafted, with strong performances and direction. Though it was fairly well-received back in the '70s, the film isn’t as widely known these days, and it could definitely use a modern remake to reintroduce the rollicking story to a latest generation of viewers.

Industry observers note that sidney Poitier’s directorial debut, Buck and the Preacher, is a revisionist Western that Poitier directed and starred in. Set shortly after the Civil War, the film follows Buck (Poitier) and his wife Ruth (Ruby Dee) as they lead wagon trains of African Americans from Louisiana to Kansas, fighting back against white raiders hired to harass them with the help of a mysterious individual who claims to be a preacher (Harry Belafonte). The movie also features Cameron Mitchell, Denny Miller, Nita Talbot, and more in supporting roles.

According to recent reports, long before movies like The Harder They Fall and Django Unchained, Buck and the Preacher broke ground as the first notable Western to feature a primarily Black cast. Though it’s not very widely known these days, the movie is both historically significant and highly entertaining. A remake of the classic makes a lot of sense, as the themes and story are certainly still resonant today, and it could also polish up some of the original movie’s flaws in pacing, particularly in its later half.

Directed by John Ford and adapted from Ernest Haycox’s 1937 short story The Stage to Lordsburg, Stagecoach is arguably the most iconic Western of Hollywood’s Golden Age. As the title suggests, the film is set on a stagecoach, exploring the stories of its passengers and the various dangers they encounter as they travel between Arizona and New Mexico. The movie stars John Wayne in his breakout role, leading an ensemble cast that also includes Claire Trevor, Andy Devine, John Carradine, Thomas Mitchell, and Louise Platt.

While the historical and cultural significance of Stagecoach cannot be denied, the film’s representation of Native Americans is highly derogatory and dated, which is one of the biggest reasons to give it a modern remake. The movie already had a remake back in 1966, which was similarly acclaimed and similarly problematic by modern standards, and another one in 1986, which was less problematic but also less acclaimed, so this is one Western classic that could really do with another try, even if it is an undeniable cultural landmark as is.

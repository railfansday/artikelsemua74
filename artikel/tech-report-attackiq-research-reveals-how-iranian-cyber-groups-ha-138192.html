ANTA CLARA, Calif. — (BUSINESS WIRE) — AttackIQ, the leader in threat-informed Continuous Threat Exposure Management (CTEM), today released upcoming research that connects previously fragmented reporting on individual Iranian cyber campaigns to map the relationships among state-sponsored operators and the institutions supporting them. The findings show Iranian espionage groups turn compromised networks into launchpads for other operators, transforming intelligence-gathering operations into destructive attacks. Threat reporting on Iran typically covers one campaign at a time, and vendors track the same groups under different names. Helix Kitten alone appears under eight other names, including OilRig, APT34 and Scarred Manticore. AttackIQ’s Adversary Research Team consolidated that reporting into Weighing Cyber Power: Evaluating the Strategic and Operational Significance of Iran’s State-Sponsored Cyber Capability, a single map of 20 Iranian adversaries, the government institutions they are linked to and the contractors that supply their personnel, tooling and infrastructure. “The value of threat research is measured by the decisions it informs,” mentioned Paul Reid, VP of Adversary Research at AttackIQ. “Understanding which Iranian groups work together, and how confident we can be in each relationship, gives defenders a clear starting point for emulation and a realistic picture of what could follow an initial compromise. It turns a single detection into a forecast of what comes next.”.

The report traces how a single intrusion can pass between groups. In one documented case, Helix Kitten (Scarred Manticore), conducted prolonged intelligence collection inside a network before handing off the network access to Banished Kitten (Void Manticore), which pursued destructive objectives. In a second case, Static Kitten (Mango Sandstorm) established access and passed it to Storm-1084, a destructive operator. In a separate campaign against the Albanian government, four separately tracked groups contributed to the same operation, which spanned reconnaissance, initial access, data theft and the deployment of destructive tooling.

Recent findings suggest that when the damage arrives, it is frequently disguised. The research also found Iranian groups staging destructive attacks as ransomware or hacktivism, obscuring the intent behind the attacks. Pioneer Kitten provided criminal ransomware affiliates with access to compromised networks.

The organizations hit first may be a route to other targets. Several groups can gain access to more heavily defended organizations, including in the defense industrial base, through their subsidiaries, contractors, and suppliers.

It has been revealed that the analysis classifies 20 Iranian adversaries into three weight classes based on their organizational significance, operational relevance, and demonstrated capabilities. The classifications underscore that even smaller, specialized groups can play critical roles in larger cyber campaigns.

Heavyweight: Established groups with sustained espionage operations, internally developed tooling and a central role in Iran’s cyber strategy.

Lightweight: Groups with more focused missions, including specialized espionage, access facilitation and destructive operations.

Featherweight: Groups with narrower or less consistently documented activity, some relying on support from more established adversaries.

“Iran isn’t as technically sophisticated as China, Russia or North Korea, but what it lacks in sophistication it makes up for in cadence,” mentioned Francis Guibernau, Senior Adversary Research Engineer at AttackIQ. “They target anything and everything, on every operating system and in industrial environments. That volume, combined with how freely these groups share access, is what makes the ecosystem dangerous.”.

In a recent development, the research draws on more than 200 references and consolidates more than 2,600 procedures, 1,300 file indicators, and 2,300 network indicators. The analysis was also validated against threat intelligence and real-world activity observed by several Fortune 150 companies actively targeted by Iranian threat actors. The research team also received input from National Computer Incident Response Teams (CIRTs) and members of the intelligence community.

To help organizations put the research into practice, AttackIQ will be releasing adversary emulations based on the documented tactics, techniques, and procedures of Iranian threat actors over the coming weeks. These emulations will enable security teams to safely test their defenses against relevant adversary behaviors, identify gaps in detection and prevention, and prioritize improvements based on real-world threat intelligence.

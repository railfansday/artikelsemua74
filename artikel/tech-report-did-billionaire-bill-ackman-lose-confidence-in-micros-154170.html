Recent findings suggest that institutional investment managers are required to disclose their holdings quarterly through Form 13F, provided they meet certain criteria, within 45 days of the end of every quarter. Wall Street pays close attention to disclosures from the most famous managers, including Bill Ackman, the founder and CEO of Pershing Square Capital. And during the third quarter, Ackman oversaw what appears, at first glance, to be a significant decrease in Pershing Square Capital's stake in Microsoft (MSFT +0.60%). The fund apparently sold about two million of the tech leader's shares. Is Bill Ackman really bearish on Microsoft's prospects? (Related: Scientists find “field” of washing machines in ocean du...)

Because hedge funds aren't required to disclose all of their financial assets, Form 13Fs do not necessarily capture a fund's full exposure to a particular company. That's what happened here. Ackman himself said in a post on X (formerly Twitter) that although his fund sold roughly two million shares of Microsoft during the third quarter, it also purchased long-term options giving it the right to buy about two million Microsoft shares at approximately $310 apiece -- these aren't required to be disclosed in Form 13F. The billionaire also said that he remains bullish on Microsoft and believes the stock is undervalued. (Related: Samsung Galaxy Watch Ultra 2 vs Watch 9: When is it rig...)

In a recent development, microsoft has underperformed the broader equity market this year, but it has gained significant value since late June. And the company's latest financial enhancement gave the stock a boost. In the first quarter of its fiscal year 2027, which ended on Sept. 30, Microsoft's revenue increased by 19% year over year to $92.3 billion. Its non-GAAP earnings per share jumped 18% year over year to $4.89. Microsoft Cloud revenue was $63.9 billion, up 30% year over year.

In a recent development, and Azure revenue came in at $33.2 billion, up 48% compared to the year-ago period. Microsoft is cashing in on the artificial intelligence (AI) revolution, and it is showing up in cloud revenue. The organization ended the period with a commercial remaining performance obligation of $677 billion, up 73% year over year. The tech giant should experience strong sales growth within its cloud division for the foreseeable future. That's what the strong and growing backlog suggests.

Now, one reason Microsoft has underperformed the market this year is that some investors worry about the future of the AI industry. As hyperscalers like Microsoft spend small fortunes on their AI build-out, if the market runs into trouble and demand for AI services drops, we could see their financial results take a hit. However, Microsoft's business looks strong, and the stock remains a buy even after factoring in that risk. Here are three reasons why.

It has been revealed that first, Microsoft generates meaningful revenue from long-term commitments even outside of its cloud business. The organization's famous operating systems and productivity suite, and deep long-term relationships with millions of enterprises, mean this source of sales is fairly secure. Of course, the cloud and AI businesses are disproportionately contributing to top-line growth. But it's nice to have long-term contracts outside of that to fall back on in case the AI tailwind slows.

Second, even though Microsoft is spending heavily on its AI ambitions, it still has a massive cash pile. The company's trailing 12-month free cash flow is nearly $60 billion, while cash and short-term investments totaled $64.5 billion as of Sept. 30. Microsoft’s solid cash position means it can meet its short-term obligations with ease. The company has an AAA credit rating from S&P Global -- the highest rating available and higher than that of the U.S. government -- for a good reason.

Industry observers note that microsoft's ability to generate significant cash can also allow it to invest in other lucrative opportunities as they appear. Third, Microsoft has a strong dividend program compared to its similarly-sized tech peers. Its forward yield of 0.8% is slightly below the S&P 500's 1.1% average, but Microsoft regularly increases its payouts, and opting to reinvest the dividend could make a real difference in average returns over the long run.

Tech Report: Eventbrite Fees vs Selling Tickets on Your Own WooCommerce Shop

Industry observers note that most comparisons between a hosted ticketing platform and running tickets yourself stop at a percentage. The number that decides it is not only the fee. It is who pays that fee, and what you stop getting when you leave the platform.

I built Tickets & Passes for WooCommerce, so I have an interest here. I will still put the real numbers on the table first, including the parts that do not favour my plugin.

On 27 November 2026, Eventbrite's own pricing page listed a service fee of 3.7% + $1.79 per paid ticket, plus a 2.9% payment processing fee per order. Free events were listed with no platform fee. Those figures are in US dollars on the US site, and vary by country, currency and event type, so read the page for your own market. The same page states the service fee is paid by buyers by default, and that an organiser can choose to absorb it instead. (Related: Mechs vs Kaiju’s Ragdoll codes (November 2026)...)

Industry observers note that A fee you pass on does not disappear. It becomes a higher price at checkout, which you pay for in conversion instead of margin. (Related: GTA 6 made real gyms empty out as players chose in-game...)

The fixed $1.79 is what hurts cheap tickets. A $25 ticket pays a much larger share than a $50 one, which is why small venues notice the fee first.

On your own WooCommerce shop you still pay a payment provider for card processing. I am not quoting a rate, because it depends on your provider, country and volume. The fair reading is that the 2.9% line is roughly what accepting cards costs wherever you sell, and the 3.7% + $1.79 service fee is the part that stops existing when tickets run through your own shop.

That is the whole financial case, and it is enough on its own for a venue selling a few thousand tickets a year.

The plugin adds three product types to WooCommerce: Ticket for plain admission with a validity window and a number of uses, Timeslot Ticket for dated sessions with a capacity per slot, and Pass for season passes and memberships across a period. All three can sit in the same cart and order as your other products.

Recent findings suggest that every purchase complications a QR code. Staff scan it at /check-in/ on your own domain, in a phone browser, with no app to install. Each scan is decided on your server, so a cancelled ticket cannot be waved through by putting the phone in airplane mode. The readme is clear about the trade: the scanner also needs a connection at the door.

According to recent reports, around that comes what a managed platform normally supplies: a Scanner role limited to check-in, a dashboard per product type with search, manual check-in, resend, reset and cancel, and analytics charting check-ins by day and hour with CSV export. Customers find tickets in WooCommerce My Account, with an on-screen QR code, a printable PDF and an add-to-calendar file on timeslot tickets. Codes, PDFs and pass photos are served through a signed link, not a guessable URL.

The WordPress.org readme has the full steps. In outline:.

I would keep the platform in three situations. When discovery through its marketplace is where your audience actually comes from, since a saved service fee is no use against an empty room. When you have no WordPress site and no intention of running one. And for a large multi-day event with a box office team, where a managed invoice and someone else on the phone may be worth the per-ticket cost. That last one is a judgement about operations, not arithmetic.

In a recent development, run a real test purchase before launch: product page, cart, checkout, confirmation email, then a scan at the door. Check your time zone and date format. If a code will not scan, the scanner menu accepts a typed one.

To back out, deactivate the plugin. The readme claims deactivation stops it and clears its scheduled tasks while settings and records stay put, and reactivating picks up where you left off. Deleting it removes settings, scheduled tasks and the Scanner role but keeps tickets and check-in records, so removing it to test a conflict does not wipe a season of history. If you want everything gone, the TPFW_REMOVE_ALL_DATA constant in wp-config.php drops tables, QR codes, PDFs and pass photos. It cannot be undone, so take a backup and leave it out of a trial.

Work out your mix of ticket prices, apply 3.7% + $1.79 to each paid ticket, and see what a year of it totals. If that number is small next to the value of the platform's discovery and support, stay where you are. If it is not, and your venue already runs WooCommerce, this is a free way to test the operational side, with no per-ticket fee to renegotiate later.

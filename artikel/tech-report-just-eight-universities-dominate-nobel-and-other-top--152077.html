Tech Report: Just eight universities dominate Nobel and other top economics prizes

Eight institutions dominate the world's top economics prizes, led Harvard University in Cambridge, Massachusetts.

Sources indicate that this contrasts with prizes awarded in disciplines in the natural sciences, engineering and social sciences outside economics. These have become less concentrated among certain instituions over the past 70 years, according to the working paper, which has not yet been peer reviewed. “The whole world now does science,” says Richard Freeman, an economist at Harvard University and an author on the paper, which is an patch to an article presented at a National Bureau of Economic Research Summer Institute workshop in a 20242. “Our initial thought was that as the science spreads out, so too should all the prizes.” And that was the case — except in economics where “it went the other direction”.

Economist John Hassler, who is based at Stockholm University and chairs the prize-awarding committee for the Economics Prize in Memory of Alfred Nobel, indicates that the overall finding of concentration among a small group of US universities is not a complete surprise.

“The trend is truly eye-opening and adds another dimension to the understanding of inequality within the economics profession,” adds Ashwini Deshpande, an economist at Ashoka University in Sonipat, India. “The low levels of gender, ethnic and geographic diversity within authors of top journal papers and prizes is well known and a matter of concern.”.

Freeman and his colleagues examined 171 different awards and focused their analyses on those given between 1960 and 2022, including Nobel prizes (except for literature and peace), the Fields Medal for mathematics and the Breakthrough science prizes established in 2012 by the founders of technology companies among others.

The authors of the working paper analysed the academic affiliations of the 5,859 prizewinners, working out the number of years they were linked to a specific institution. They call this metric ‘affiliation years’.

According to recent reports, of the 18 scientific fields of the prizewinners, 17 had a greater variety of universities represented among recipients over the time period. Economics was the outlier.

In a recent development, among winners of the economics Nobel prize, seven out of 148 institutions made up 50% of affiliation years. It means that, of all the years that economics Nobel prizewinners dedicated to their discipline in academia, half of them were spent in the seven institutions.

It has been revealed that for chemistry Nobels, 23 institutions out of 216 accounted for half of affiliation years. Twenty-six out of 243 institutions accounted for 50% of affiliation years for the physiology or medicine Nobel, and 17 out of 200 for the physics prize.

According to recent reports, when the authors assessed all the main economics prizes between 1960 and 2022 (not only the Nobel), they found that researchers from eight out of 249 institutions represented 50% of affiliation years. In addition to Harvard, the eight are: the University of Chicago in Illinois; Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) in Cambridge; Stanford University in California; Princeton University in New Jersey; Yale University in New Haven, Connecticut; the University of California, Berkeley; and Columbia University in New York.

It has been revealed that by contrast, for assessed prizes in the natural sciences, 37 institutions out of 1,318 account for 50% of affiliation-years as of 2022; for engineering prizes it is 27 out of 783; and for social sciences prizes (minus economics), it is 23 out of 744. Unlike in economics, the trend for these fields is towards less concentration (see ‘Spot the outlier’).

The authors propose some possible explanations. One of these relates to another finding of the paper: “The institutions producing most of the field’s recognized work also establish the toolkit that the rest of the fields adopts,” the paper’s authors say. “Work using an unfamiliar approach is hard to place or recognize, and findings that disagree with the main tenets of the field are rejected rather than studied as special cases that may yield upcoming knowledge.”.

Another factor, they report is that in economics, 55 per cent of Nobel laureates received the prize for a broad body of work. This contrasts somewhat with medicine and the other sciences. Some 50 per cent of prize winners in chemistry, 59 per cent in physics and 70 per cent in medicine were awarded for testable findings.

Hanzhang Zhou, a co-author on the paper and an economics doctoral candidate at MIT, indicates: “When you’re giving the prize for somebody’s lifetime agenda, the institution that they’re associated with is usually one of the factors that you do take into account,” she explains.

It has been revealed that hassler told Nature that the economics prize is not intended to be a lifetime achievement award and follows the rules governing the other prize categories. He says it is a prize for a well-defined contribution or breakthrough, should also lead to further research in the field and that the finding must also have relevance to society at large.

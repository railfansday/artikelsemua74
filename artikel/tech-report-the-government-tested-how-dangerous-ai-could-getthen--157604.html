The dawn of artificial intelligence brings with it both rewards and risks. With the ability to sift through unfathomably large data sets, AI could help detect cancers in their infancy, unlock some of the most enduring mysteries of mathematics, and even help contain the beating nuclear heart of future fusion reactors.

In a recent development, unfortunately, the risks of this technology are far from minuscule, including its impact on human education, the environment, and potentially the survival of the human race (an all-too-plausible scenario put forward by AI tech CEOs). Even the everyday LLMs that now litter our digital lives—Claude, Gemini, ChatGPT, and Grok—can aid and abet some of the most dangerous actors on the planet.

It has been revealed that at least, that’s the latest conclusion by the Government Accountability Office (GAO), a nonpartisan watchdog housed in the legislative branch of the U.S. government. Typically, GAO turns its investigative gaze on efforts to limit fraud and wasteful spending in the government itself, but in a fresh tactic at the urging of U.S. Senator Maggie Hassan (D-NH), the office instead performed real-world tests on the platform to see if these four popular AI models would aid in planning a terrorist attack. “A terrorist can log onto an AI platform and in less than 12 hours walk away with a concrete playbook for how to carry out a deadly attack,” Hassan, incoming Chair of the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee, stated in a press statement. “Today’s latest report makes it clear that the AI public safety threat is no longer theoretical.”. (Related: F1 star Max Verstappen played 40 hours of GTA 6 before ...)

GAO conducted this test in the spring this year, and earlier this week reported some disturbing results. Two of the platforms, according to an official GAO release, provided guidance on building and detonating an improvised explosion device, or IED, and two other AI models showing differing methods for targeting the electrical grid. Within 12 hours, all four models also gamed through potential locations and methods for carrying out such an attack.

Although these violent lines of questioning triggered each platform’s safeguards, GAO got around them using common safeguard-bypassing techniques. Additionally, they confirmed the validity of the information provided with experts. The results of this investigation were published in a 61-page report that will be central to future senate hearings on AI safety.

“We set out to do two different types of evil,” GAO director Kevin Walsh said in an interview posted on YouTube. “The first type is we wanted to create a bomb. And we didn’t just want to know how to create a bomb, we wanted it to help pick out a target, a location, and a time, and to give us instructions all the way…Evil scenario two, we wanted to do a bunch of economic damage, and in both cases that ended up being targeting the electrical grid.”.

In one case, Walsh reports that one AI model would refuse to answer questions or divert the user to more safe behaviors but noted that “it wasn’t always successful.” Walsh claims that companies theoretically call law enforcement when suspicious activity is detected, but whether that occurs and at what frequency is uncertain.

In a recent development, “As AI capabilities evolve, AI providers will continue to refine their safeguards,” the report concludes. “However, in its current state, this technology can potentially be dangerous and its misuse represents a growing risk to national and economic security.”.

The GAO officially released these terrorist-abetting findings on November 18, 2026, the same day Anthropic, creators of Claude, went public on the stock exchange. Despite the tech giant’s own admission of AI being an existential threat to humanity, its IPO at one point reached a meteoric a $2 trillion valuation before dipping back down to a (measly) $1.6 trillion.

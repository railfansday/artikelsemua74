Tech Report: To Help Children Cope With Loss, Begin With the End in Mind

According to recent reports, there are two wishes most parents have for their child. One is that nothing bad will happen to them. And the second is that if and when something does, they will be OK. Every parent knows that their first wish is impossible, and that their child will be disappointed or frustrated or experience some kind of negative emotion over the course of their life. So one of your tasks as a parent is to help your child develop the knowledge and capacity to emerge from these kinds of challenging moments essentially unharmed. One experience that can be particularly unsettling, especially for younger children, is the sudden departure of a caregiver or teacher. Over the years, many parents have come to me wondering how best to support their child when a nanny or teacher leaves with little or no notice. Two aspects of a caregiver's sudden departure can be particularly distressing. The first is the loss of the connection, support, and caring provided by someone with whom the child has developed a significant relationship. The second may be uncertainty about why the person left. In a recent development, these two aspects of the experience are not mutually exclusive. A child may miss the person who left while also feeling confused or wondering why they left or whether someone else might leave or disappear without warning. Parents may need to address both the emotional loss and the uncertainty, depending on how their child responds.

In a recent development, the first piece of advice I would give is to remember the end goal: for your child to be OK in spite of whatever difficult situation they may be facing. And while you may also be affected by the situation, disappointed by the loss or by what you imagine may come with this change, your child may not experience the situation in the same way. Pay attention to your child's response. Don't assume that your child is as upset as you are or understands the implications in the same way.

Join with your child's emotion before trying to respond or offer an explanation. Acknowledge what your child seems to be feeling. You might say, "I notice that you seem sad." You could also ask, "How do you feel about that?" particularly if your child's emotions are not apparent or obvious. Asking also indicates that you are open to talking about how the event has affected your child. When we don't discuss obvious topics, the elephant in the room, even the youngest children notice and may wonder whether it's a topic that is off limits. That's not the message you want to send your child, even if you don't know how to answer their questions or they ask something you don't want to answer.

Then, if your child has questions, let those questions guide the conversation. You don't need to share everything you know, worry about, or imagine to be true. Answer your child's questions at the level of interest they express, rather than at the level of knowledge you possess as a parent.

According to recent reports, in the absence of information, our default is often to imagine the worst. One caution, then, is to recognize that our own tendency to imagine the worst when we don't have all the information can influence what we communicate to our children. Children learn from what they observe, including how we respond to uncertainty.

If your child is going to the dark side with their assumptions or explanations for what has happened, help your child consider alternative explanations without inventing facts. An absence of information doesn't necessarily mean something terrible has happened. And one goal is for your child to be OK with ambiguity.

In a recent development, for instance, if your child worries that the caregiver left because of something they did, you can help them consider other possibilities without claiming to know the reason for the departure. You may also want to reassure your child that they are not responsible for the caregiver's departure, whether the adult chose to leave or someone else made that decision.

Recent findings suggest that there is also the opportunity to practice tolerating uncertainty. Sometimes, no matter how much we think about what happened, we won't know why someone behaved as they did. There may be several possible explanations, and we may never know which one is correct. Learning to tolerate that uncertainty, rather than continuing to search for an answer that we will never be sure of, is an vital skill.

It has been revealed that you can decide, with each situation that presents itself, how much you want to engage in this kind of exercise and how deeply you want to explore it. Sometimes just acknowledging the uncertainty is enough. At other times, it may be helpful to consider several possible explanations. Not every disappointment can be explained away, and not every question will have an answer.

Industry observers note that even when a child understands what happened, they may continue to miss the person. Parents can offer emotional support while helping their child recognize that unpleasant feelings generally diminish over time. You can acknowledge that your child misses their teacher or nanny, talk about what they liked about that person, or just listen when they want to talk. None of these responses requires you to resolve the situation or make the sadness disappear.

According to recent reports, sometimes there isn't another explanation that makes the loss less painful. Sometimes there isn't a lesson to be learned. Sometimes the child just needs to feel sad and discover that they can tolerate those feelings.

In a recent development, there is also the normal response to disappointment. Do you read a book, talk to someone, reframe the situation, or acknowledge that the situation is painful and that the feeling will pass after a period of time? These are different strategies, and different strategies work for different situations at different times. What we want ideally is a repertoire of strategies that we can pull from. Tolerating an unpleasant feeling is itself an approach we can use to cope with unpleasant situations. Sometimes learning to tolerate an unpleasant feeling is in itself an accomplishment.

While parents may wish to protect their child from disappointment, what's probably more reasonable and helpful to your child in the long run is not to ensure that nothing disappointing ever happens, or even that your child never feels distressed. It is to help your child become increasingly capable of managing those experiences.

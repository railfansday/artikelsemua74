Tech Report: When One Robot Learns, Can a Thousand Others Follow?

  • Industry observers note that the goal is to enable robots to acquire new skills by observing humans and other machines.
  • In a recent development, one of the most interesting developments in this area comes from NVIDIA.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that egocentric videos remain an crucial source of training data for robots. But researchers are looking beyond this approach, exploring how robots can learn from third-person demonstrations and the experiences of other robotic systems.

Industry observers note that the goal is to enable robots to acquire new skills by observing humans and other machines. But how close are we to making this a reality, and what technical challenges still stand in the way? (Related: Industry News: My Journey to a serverless transformers ...)

In a recent development, one of the most interesting developments in this area comes from NVIDIA. The company developed NVIDIA Cosmos, a platform for building Physical AI models, and introduced Cosmos 3 in 2026. The model can interpret what is happening in a video, understand spatial relationships between objects, and predict the outcomes of specific actions. (Related: Building the next generation of multimodal AI experienc...)

According to recent reports, but understanding a video is only part of the challenge. A camera positioned in front of a person does not capture the same view as the person performing the task. Some movements and objects may be hidden from view, so training a robot requires reconstructing the spatial relationships between the person, the tools they use, and the surrounding environment.

In a recent development, this is the challenge NVIDIA is addressing with Grounded-Exo2Ego, a technology introduced in August 2026. It converts third-person footage into videos that show the scene from the perspective of the person performing the task. To do this, the system combines 3D scene reconstruction with semantic understanding of objects, enabling it to reconstruct parts of the scene that were not visible to the original camera. Demonstrations include cooking, performing CPR, and repairing a bicycle.

Industry observers note that for now, Grounded-Exo2Ego does not teach robots to perform the actions they observe. The model generates video rather than a sequence of commands that a robot can execute. It also has to make assumptions about parts of the scene that are hidden from view. Even so, the technology could make third-person footage a more useful source of training data for robotics.

The next step is translating observed actions into movements that robots can actually perform, given that their physical capabilities differ from those of humans.

Industry observers note that in November 2026, NVIDIA introduced GR00T 2, a model designed to predict future states of the environment and a robot’s actions. Its pretraining involved more than 400,000 hours of egocentric video, demonstrations recorded using handheld gripper devices, and footage of teleoperated robots. Unlike Grounded-Exo2Ego, this technology is specifically designed to control robotic systems.

In one experiment, a robot equipped with two five-fingered hands was tasked with placing filter paper into a funnel and wetting it with liquid. The robot had previously seen only human demonstrations of the task, with no examples of it being performed by the robot itself. It achieved a 65% success rate. In another experiment, a robotic arm assembled a toy dump truck using demonstrations recorded with a different gripper type, achieving a 60% success rate.

Recent findings suggest that I have little doubt that robots will eventually be able to learn new tasks by watching humans and other machines. NVIDIA’s research is already showing promising results, although these are still individual experiments rather than technologies deployed at scale. To me, the next major step is enabling robots to share what they have learned with one another. Could robots build on each other’s experience without having to go through the entire training process every time?

Industry observers note that transferring skills between robots introduces new challenges in validating what they have learned. If one robotic arm can learn to grip and move objects successfully, there is no guarantee that another robot will replicate this ability with the same precision. The outcome depends on the gripper design, the characteristics of the objects, the arrangement of the equipment, and the conditions in the working environment.

If that experience is used to enhancement a shared model, validation becomes even more important. We need to assess how reliably a fresh skill performs across different robots, identify the conditions under which errors occur, and determine whether an update can be safely deployed to other machines. And successfully completing a task does not necessarily mean the system has chosen a safe way to achieve the result.

The complexity of validation is already evident in the preparation of training data. For example, in one of the projects of Keymakr involving robots that automate meal assembly, the team had to label transparent containers whose edges were almost indistinguishable from the background. They also had to determine which ingredients a robotic gripper could physically grasp. Some tasks required comparing images taken before and after ingredients were moved, while ambiguous cases underwent additional review. As upcoming scenarios emerged, the annotation guidelines were refined. The project ultimately achieved 98.65% annotation accuracy.

According to recent reports, in this case, the specialists were responsible for validating the quality of the training data. With collective learning, that responsibility would extend to evaluating how robots apply acquired skills in real-world conditions. For instance, we would need to verify whether a robot can handle objects of different shapes or adapt to changes in their positions, without creating safety risks.

I believe human validation will remain essential for at least another 15 years. Even if robots become capable of learning new tasks independently, someone will still need to verify that they are performing those tasks correctly.

It has been revealed that the ability to transfer skills between robots has clear practical implications for manufacturing. If one robot learns a new task and its experience can be validated and used to train others, manufacturers could adapt their production processes much faster, without having to train every machine individually.

It has been revealed that modern factories already demonstrate just how efficient robotic systems can be. In March 2026, for example, Chinese automaker GAC reported that its smart AION factory produces a latest vehicle every 53 seconds. More than 600 industrial robots operate in its welding workshop, while the factory can manufacture up to ten vehicle models with over 100,000 possible configurations.

Recent findings suggest that but a high level of automation does not mean robots can learn new tasks on their own. The next stage of industrial development will depend on how easily manufacturers can adapt their equipment to changing production needs.

Toyota offers an interesting example of this shift. In November 2026, the organization declared looks to establish Toyota Robotics, a dedicated division responsible for developing, deploying, and commercializing robotic systems, starting in January 2027. The scale of its ambitions is significant. Toyota estimates that the Toyota Group and its suppliers may need around 400,000 robots between 2028 and 2040. Eventually, the company also plans to offer its robotic solutions to other industries.

At the same time, Toyota’s leadership recognizes that simply increasing the number of robots is not enough. During a November investor meeting, manufacturer Vice President Yoichi Miyazaki pointed out that to deliver meaningful economic benefits, a robot that presently performs a single task would need to be capable of handling three or four tasks.

This is where collective learning could make a real difference. If robots can learn from one another, manufacturers would no longer have to start the training process from scratch every time production requirements change. Of course, there are still major challenges to overcome, particularly around hardware compatibility and skill validation. But in the long run, a robot’s value may be measured not only by what it can do, but also by how quickly another robot can learn from it.

Industry observers note that the high cost of developing and deploying AI and robotic systems is a subject of considerable debate today. Building hardware, training models, integrating systems into production processes, and maintaining them over time all require substantial investment. But today’s costs tell us little about how much these technologies might cost in a few years.

Historically, many technological breakthroughs have followed a similar path. First comes disruption: a new solution emerges that fundamentally changes how a particular task is approached. At this stage, companies focus their resources primarily on making the technology work. Once its capabilities have been proven in practice, the focus shifts to optimization. Developers start looking for ways to reduce costs, improve performance, and scale the product.

Sources indicate that in my project, Scryon, collecting and processing information about a single professional conference initially cost hundreds of dollars and took about six hours of my time. After we automated and optimised the process, both the cost and the time required fell significantly.

I expect robotics to follow a similar trajectory, although optimization will be more challenging because of the physical hardware involved, safety requirements, and ongoing maintenance. Investors are presently pouring money into Physical AI, and companies are introducing new technologies, but many of these developments are still being tested. It will take several years for many of today’s projects to evolve into products ready for full-scale deployment.

What I find particularly interesting is how the ability to share skills between robots could change the economics of deployment. Today, teaching a robot a new task may require additional demonstrations, configuration, and testing. If one robot masters a task and hundreds of others can benefit from that experience, the cost of training each additional machine could fall significantly. There would still be costs associated with adapting and validating those skills, of course.

Industry observers note that I would measure the success of these technologies using a few practical metrics: how long it takes a robot to learn a new task, how consistently it performs that task, and how much it costs to transfer that skill to other machines. These are the measures that will ultimately tell us whether collective learning can truly transform the economics of robotics.

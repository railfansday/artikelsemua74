Tech Report: Why do some memories stick for years? A teenager’s extraordinary memory offers a clue

A teenager with highly superior autobiographical memory tests like anyone else, but her own past returns uninvited at least once a day.

It has been revealed that give a teenager from the Atlanta area a date from years ago, and she can tell you what day of the week it was, what she wore, or what the weather was like. Yet her scores on standard memory tests were typical.

In a recent development, in a upcoming case report, the teenager noted memories of her past came back on their own at least daily. Adults in an earlier survey reported that happening about once a week on average.

The condition is called highly superior autobiographical memory, or HSAM, and it was first described in 2006.

Psychologist Anne Cleary of Colorado State University and neuroscientist Daniel Drane, then at Emory University, examined the teenager in June 2024.

They report on her as a single case, so the pattern they found still has to hold up in other people. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

“It seems as if people with HSAM are reliving these past experiences more frequently and that strengthens the memories overall, making memories more stable over time for them,” Cleary remarked. (Related: Predicting and detecting failures: How Azure strengthen...)

For years, she added, researchers had speculated that people with HSAM might be better at deliberately retrieving memories, “but our work seems to show the crucial difference lies in how often memories return on their own.”.

Her parents had noticed that her memory for her own life was unusually strong, and a colleague at Johns Hopkins University sent the family to Drane.

She was 16 when Cleary and Drane sat down with her and her mother at Emory. They read out dates, some chosen in advance and some made up on the spot, then holidays, then family trips: 14 prompts in all.

She answered so fast that the researchers paused after the first date to set up a video camera.

“Give me a date and I will tell you everything that happened on that day,” she told them. “What I did, what I was wearing, what happened that day.”.

The first date was November 5, 2021. She said it was a Friday, and that school was out because the Atlanta Braves were holding their World Series parade. It was overcast, she said, and the sunset that evening was unusually pretty.

In a recent development, cleary checked afterward. A local news story confirmed the school closure, a weather archive confirmed the clouds, and a dated photo posted online showed the sunset.

Every one of the 14 prompts produced details that held up the same way, including the day of the week.

Industry observers note that the girl also sat through a standard battery of memory and thinking tests, and her results were unremarkable. That matches what researchers have reported since the first HSAM case 20 years ago.

People with the condition recall their own lives in extraordinary detail, yet they perform about like anyone else when asked to memorize a list of words.

Cleary’s team thinks the reason is what those tests measure.

Sources indicate that most of them ask a person to remember on purpose. Asked whether her own recall took effort, the girl noted she could do it by strategy, “but usually it just sort of happens, just naturally.”.

Industry observers note that so the team gave her a questionnaire built for the other kind of remembering. These are the memories that arrive uninvited while a person is doing something else, which psychologists call involuntary memories.

Industry observers note that earlier descriptions had hinted that people with HSAM get more of them. But Cleary’s team notes no one had directly measured how often they happen in a person with the condition.

The questionnaire has 20 items. Ten ask how often memories of the past pop up on their own, and ten ask the same about thoughts of the future.

Each answer is one of five steps: never, once a month, once a week, once a day, or once an hour or more.

On the past-oriented items, the teenager’s average answer landed between once a day and once an hour. When the same questionnaire was given to 200 adults in a 2015 study, the average answer was about once a week.

Industry observers note that her score was just over two standard deviations above that group’s mean, a gap the researchers call significantly higher than typical. Her future-oriented thoughts were more frequent than average too, though by less.

The team builds its proposal on that gap. Each time a memory comes back, she relives it, and the researchers propose that all those unplanned replays are what makes her memories so durable.

They present it as a hypothesis the case supports, not as proof.

The team looked at one more thing, and it didn’t stand out.

Some researchers argue that déjà vu and involuntary memories come from the same mechanism, with déjà vu as the faint version, a memory stirred but not quite retrieved.

A 2026 study found that people who report more involuntary memories also report more déjà vu.

The researchers asked whether she also experienced déjà vu more often than usual.

She chose “a few times per year,” the most common answer among college students who had taken the same questionnaire.

Sources indicate that her mental imagery didn’t stand out either. On a standard vividness questionnaire she scored 58. People with typical imagery average about 57 on it, and people with unusually vivid mental pictures average about 78.

Whatever sets her apart, then, it isn’t a sharper mind’s eye. On questionnaires about inner speech and the kinds of images she thinks in, she also scored inside the ordinary range.

It has been revealed that one person can’t show how common this pattern is. The questionnaire relies on the very thing it’s trying to measure, a person’s memory of her own experiences.

So the researchers want future studies to catch involuntary memories as they happen, with prompts during the day.

Recent findings suggest that her age limited the testing. The quiz about public events that earlier HSAM studies used was built for adults, and a teenage version didn’t work either, because she doesn’t follow the news.

A June 2026 preprint from another group used both the questionnaire and real-time prompts on a 16-year-old boy with HSAM and found more involuntary memories either way.

Drane, now at The Ohio State University, said the teenager has since completed an MRI of her brain and other experimental tasks “which will be the subject of future work.”.

Recent findings suggest that cleary said the case could help build better memory tests. One use is for epilepsy surgery patients whose families notice something missing even when standard tests come back clean.

Whether those daily returns are what keeps her memories so durable is still untested.

Tracking involuntary memories as they happen in more people with HSAM could show how common the pattern is.

The full study was published in the journal Neurocase.

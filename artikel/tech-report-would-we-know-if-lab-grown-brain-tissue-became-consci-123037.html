Tech Report: Would we know if lab-grown brain tissue became conscious?

Scientists propose testing whether lab-grown brain tissue can learn a pattern and detect when it changes, a possible clue in the search for signs of consciousness.

Scientists can grow small clusters of human brain cells in a dish. One set of joined clusters has reacted to capsaicin, the compound found in chili peppers, but a reaction like that doesn’t show the tissue feels anything.

Industry observers note that two neuroscientists have now proposed a way to check what such tissue, called a brain organoid, can do. Their proposal borrows tests already used on infants, sleeping people, and patients who can’t respond. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

The tests ask whether an organoid can learn a pattern and notice when the pattern is broken. The proposal reports no results, and the authors wrote that it is highly unlikely today’s organoids are conscious.

Lucia Melloni of Ruhr University Bochum in Germany and Stanislas Dehaene of the Collège de France in Paris wrote the proposal. Both belong to a brain and consciousness research program at CIFAR, a Canadian research organization. (Related: Introducing the latest Microsoft IQ: Putting your organ...)

“I don’t believe that brain organoids right now possess consciousness,” Melloni said.

Sources indicate that “But given the pace with which the technology is advancing, they may do so in future. That’s why we need to start thinking about how we can find out.”.

Industry observers note that organoids are grown from human stem cells. The cells organize themselves into tiny tissues that copy some features of the early developing brain.

It has been revealed that researchers can also fuse several organoids into what they call an assembloid. The one that reacted to capsaicin joined four organoids to rebuild a basic pain pathway.

“A reaction, however, is not proof that the tissue feels anything,” Melloni said.

Sources indicate that other teams have wired brain cells into machines, including one hooked to a simplified version of the video game Pong. Melloni and Dehaene wrote that those setups are too complex to show what the living tissue itself worked out.

In a 2024 study they cited, a gel that responds to electricity produced similar results in place of the cells.

The central test comes from research on how people process sequences.

Sources indicate that A person hears ba-ba-ba-ba-bu again and again. The brain reacts to the bu because it differs from the four sounds before it.

Sources indicate that after many repeats, the person learns the whole pattern. If the sequence then comes as ba-ba-ba-ba-ba, the brain reacts again, this time because the expected bu is missing.

The second reaction is the harder one. It depends on a memory of the whole sequence, built up over many repeats.

An organoid has no ears, so researchers would use electrical pulses or light in place of sounds.

They would stimulate two or more separate groups of cells in sequence and record how the tissue’s activity changes.

The question would be whether the same sequence produces a different response depending on what came before it.

Monkeys can learn patterns like this one too. That has let researchers study the brain activity behind the reactions in animals that have no language.

Recent findings suggest that in people, the first kind of reaction survives sleep and anesthesia. The second seems to require a person to be awake and paying attention.

Sources indicate that doctors have also tried the test on patients who can’t communicate. In those patients, 80% of the ones whose brains demonstrated the second reaction later regained consciousness.

In a recent development, the test missed many others. Only 35% of the patients who recovered had shown the reaction.

Recent findings suggest that melloni and Dehaene wrote that no single test would be enough. Their confidence would rise if an organoid passed this test along with several other known signs of consciousness.

In a recent development, the authors wrote that a failed test is perhaps the more likely outcome. A failure would lower confidence that an organoid is conscious.

Industry observers note that dehaene has a financial interest in this kind of testing. He is a co-inventor on a patent for methods to monitor consciousness and an associate at a company that uses those methods in clinics.

Industry observers note that the authors wrote that there is no evidence current organoids hold any rich picture of the outside world or of themselves. The tissues are tiny, and they are cut off from a body and from surroundings.

Organoids can be kept alive for months to years, though, and they keep changing as they age. The authors proposed running the same tests on an organoid again and again as it matures.

Human babies show the second kind of reaction at 3 months of age, and it may be present before birth, after 35 weeks of pregnancy.

Repeated testing would show when each ability appears in an organoid.

The authors also wrote that if several signs ever pointed the same way, the ethical treatment of these tissues should change with the evidence.

Melloni and other researchers have called for an international board to oversee organoid research.

The full study was published in the journal Neuron.

The 10-Step Self-Audit to Run Before You Apply for Permanent Residency in Japan

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Shortly before applying for permanent residency (PR), a man in my research took out a home loan and bought an apartment. He had lived in Japan for more than ten years, and he was confident. The application was refused.

Buying the apartment did not cause the refusal. What it shows is the mistake underneath almost every refusal story I have collected: he trusted his memory of his own record instead of auditing it. Immigration does not review the life you remember. It reviews documents — tax certificates, a pension record read month by month, reports you filed or forgot, days you spent outside the country, and what happens in your life while the review is running.

So before you apply, run the same audit the examiner will. Ten steps, documents in front of you, nothing from memory. If a step produces a "not sure," that step is your application risk. Fix it now, while fixing is cheap.

The standard route is ten continuous years in Japan, including five or more years on a work visa or a residence-based visa. The shorter routes are the points system (70 points kept for three years, or 80 kept for one year) and the spouse route. (Related: Transformer-based Encoder-Decoder Models...)

If you are using points, the question is stricter than "do I have the points today?" Did you have them at the start of the period, and have you kept them the whole time, with documents to prove every item? (Related: Breaking: How to train a new language model from scratc...)

According to recent reports, then check your period of stay. PR requires the longest period of stay for your status. A three-year period of stay has long been treated as the longest in practice, but that treatment ends at the 2027 changeover — March 31, 2027 is the last day to apply under the old treatment. If you hold three years, your dates are part of your eligibility.

Collect your resident tax certificates for the past five years. Then find the dangerous periods: any time in those five years when resident tax was not deducted from your salary — a gap between jobs, self-employment, a period after arriving in Japan. For those periods, immigration wants proof that you paid by each deadline: receipts or bank records showing the dates.

This is the single most common trap in the refusal stories. People paid. They just could not prove when, years later, because the convenience-store slips were thrown away. Also collect your national tax certificates while you are at it.

Open your pension record online through the Japan Pension Service and read it month by month, for at least the past two years and ideally longer. Gaps cluster around job changes. If you ever paid national pension or national health insurance yourself, do you still have the receipts?

And check your employer, not just yourself. One applicant's premiums were deducted from his salary every month while his firm handled the social insurance incorrectly. The record was in his name, so the refusal was too. Your online record highlights you whether your employer did its part.

Sources indicate that immigration examines the household, not the individual. Run Steps 2 and 3 again for your spouse: one applicant with a high income and a clean personal record was refused over his wife's late payments.

In a recent development, work out who counts as your household for the income test, including family members you support outside Japan. Under the revised guidelines, household income is compared with the average for Japanese households of the same size — and it has to stay there, not spike once.

Finally, look forward. The fresh rules add a check on your expected future pension: what will you actually receive in old age, based on your record? If the projection is thin, your savings and investments become part of the answer. People who arrived in Japan later in life, or spent years outside the pension system, need this step most.

List every employer you have had in Japan, with dates. For every job change, resignation and tech giant name change — yes, a name change counts — did you report it to immigration within 14 days? Did you register every change of address with your city or ward office within 14 days?

It has been revealed that if you missed one, file it now, and be ready to explain it honestly in writing. A missed report filed late is a problem you have addressed. A missed report the officer finds first is a different kind of problem.

Recent findings suggest that add up every day you spent outside Japan in the past ten years. Two numbers matter under the revised guidelines: any single trip of six months or more, and a total of two and a half years or more. Either one, without a reasonable reason, can break the continuous-residence requirement.

According to recent reports, for business trips, get proof that your employer sent you. Your employer's records are easiest to obtain now, not after a request from immigration arrives mid-review.

If you are not exempt, is your Japanese at around B1 level — everyday conversation and daily-life reading and writing — and do you know how you will show it? Highly skilled professionals and their families are among the exemptions, which is one more reason the points calculation matters.

Have you read the Guidebook on Living and Working in Japan? And if you have school-age children, are they attending elementary or junior high school? The guidelines treat children's schooling as part of the overall picture of your life in Japan. If they are not attending, take advice on how that will be viewed before you file, not after.

It has been revealed that your review will take a year or more, and it does not freeze your file on the day you apply. Job losses, firm bankruptcies and long trips during the review have all turned good applications into refusals.

It has been revealed that so ask honestly: is anything likely to change — job, income, family, travel? Are you planning a major decision that depends on a yes, like the apartment in the story above? Can it wait? And when does your current period of stay expire — will you need to renew it during the review? Put that date in your calendar now.

According to recent reports, do you have a guarantor — normally a Japanese citizen or permanent resident living in Japan — who has agreed to sign? Line this person up early; it is not a same-week favour to ask.

Then take your document list from the Immigration Services Agency's current official list, not from a friend's experience, an old blog post, or even this article. Lists change, and the 2027 revision changed more than most.

After the audit, you are in one of three positions.

Recent findings suggest that apply now. Your record is clean, your dates work, and nothing massive is about to change.

Wait and repair. You found something. Fix what can be fixed, let time pass where it must — remember the checked windows: five years for resident tax, two years for pension and health insurance — and set a date to run this audit again.

Get help first. Your file includes something complicated: a past violation, a long period abroad, a complex household, the three-year trap. This is when professional advice is easily worth its cost.

Whichever position you are in, you now know more about your own file than most applicants ever do. Under the old rules, learning these things from a refusal cost you a year. Under the newly introduced ones, it costs a year and ¥200,000.

Recent findings suggest that many things can make a book challenging to read. Perhaps it's because their use of language is deliberately labyrinthine or jargon-heavy, because their narrative structure feels almost surreal, or because a lot of background knowledge is required to fully understand the story. In any case, there have been innumerable great-yet-difficult books written over the years, and it just so happens that quite a few of them are among the best books ever written.

From foundational classics like In Search of Lost Time to modern award-winning masterpieces like Angel Down, these are books which are certainly challenging to get through, but are so phenomenal that they are 100% worth the challenge. For the purposes of this list, only books that were specifically designed to be challenging will be considered, rather than great books that are hard to read today mostly because they have dated aspects, which is a whole other topic. The entries are ranked by a mixture of how essential and how challenging each book is.

In a recent development, french novelist, essayist, and literary critic Marvel Proust was one of the most influential authors of the entire 20th century, best-known for his seven-volume magnum opus In Search of Lost Time. Published between 1913 and 1927, the novel is composed of a narrator's recollections of his childhood and journey to adulthood, revisiting his past, romantic obsessions, and relationships with French high society as he tries to find his purpose as a writer.

According to recent reports, the main reason why In Search of Lost Time is such a challenging read goes without saying. Depending on what format one reads it in, the novel is over 4,000 pages and 1.2 million words long. It's a kind of literary Everest that many a bibliophile considers a true goal to climb to the top, and far from being a tedious journey that's mostly worth reading just because it's a classic, it's a genuinely phenomenal work of art of tremendous psychological depth and gorgeous prose. According to recent reports, toni Morrison's Beloved is one of those books that everyone should read before kicking the bucket, one of the most consequential American novels of the 20th century. It tells the story of a formerly enslaved mother in post-Civil War Ohio, as she's haunted by the ghost of the baby she killed to spare her from slavery. The book won the 1988 Pulitzer Prize for Fiction and is still widely remembered as one of the most foundational texts for postcolonial, African American, and feminist literature. Sources indicate that on the one hand, you have the book's brutally harrowing subject matter, which Morrison dives deep into without flinching at any point. On top of that, you get the highly fragmented, non-linear storytelling, as well as Morrison's dense and poetic prose. But though it isn't an easy read, Beloved is absolutely breathtaking, a book whose historical importance is matched only by its artistic quality.

Though the world of Latin American literature hasn't been quite as internationally mainstream throughout the 21st century as it was during the '60s and '70s, it has nonetheless delivered several incredible masterpieces. Case in point: Chilean writer Roberto Bolaño's postmodern meta-novel 2666, a gut-wrenching tale about four European literary critics searching for an elusive German author. On their journey, they encounter a wave of unsolved femicides in a Mexican border town inspired by Ciudad Juárez.

It has been revealed that 2666 is nothing but relentless surrealist cruelty over a whopping 900 pages, easily making it one of the heaviest books of all time. Reading it requires not only deciphering its sprawling, fragmented structure, but also bearing such a devastatingly realistic depiction of violence toward women in Mexico and Latin America that getting to the end of the novel is already an achievement in itself. But far from feeling exploitative, 2666 is propelled by Bolaño's hypnotic prose and unforgettable characters, making all of the bleakness worth every word.

Legendary postmodernist icon Thomas Pynchon was well-known for writing some of the densest, most complex and challenging novels of the entire postmodern movement, such as the satirical historical sci-fi masterpiece Gravity's Rainbow. It's one of the heaviest sci-fi books ever written, about an American soldier fighting in Europe during World War II. There, he discovers that his sexual encounters map onto future German rocket strikes, launching a paranoid quest into a secret conspiracy.

Recent findings suggest that generally considered Pynchon's most deliberately challenging novel, Gravity's Rainbow stands as a total refusal of traditional ideas of narrative. There's no conventional plot, no real character arc, no linear time; instead, the whole book is overloaded with theology, history, economics, pop culture references, and labyrinthine language. Even so, Gravity's Rainbow is a must-read for people already familiar with Pynchon's style, since it very well may be the quintessential countercultural epic.

Plenty of great books have won the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction throughout the 2020s, and Daniel Kraus' Angel Down is one of the best. In it, World War I soldiers venture into No Man's Land to put a wounded comrade out of his misery, only to find a fallen angel whose fate plants greed, paranoia, and moral ruin in their midst. It already seems like a compelling enough premise on paper, but what really makes Angel Down so special—and so challenging—is that the whole thing is a single unbroken sentence.

Recent findings suggest that making a single sentence span a whopping 300 pages was always going to be a gargantuan task to complete successfully, but Angel Down really does live up to the hype. Yes, the complete lack of breaks in the book is mentally draining, but it's worth every bit of effort it demands. Angel Down is a deeply moving and thought-provoking study of the existential horrors of war, blending visceral supernatural horror and war thriller genre elements faultlessly.

One of the most vital and influential American writers of his generation, William Faulkner is still considered one of the spearheads of 20th-century Southern American literature and modernist fiction. In his fourth-ever novel, he delivered his magnum opus. The Sound and the Fury chronicles the collapse of an aristocratic Mississippi family as three brothers' obsessions with their sister unravel across fragmented, shifting perspectives.

Recent findings suggest that the result of such a premise is one of those perfect books that immediately demand a re-read, and that speaks both to this masterpiece's tremendous quality and highly challenging stream-of-consciousness style. The Sound and the Fury feels like one of the most successful experiments that modernist fiction ever gave us, shifting through several different perspectives that make the whole reading experience feel like a psychological maze. Getting through it, however, delivers one of the most foundational masterpieces of early-20th-century American literature.

Industry observers note that "Newly introduced sincerity" was a trend in late-20th-century art that sought to break away from postmodernism's sense of irony and cynicism, in relation to the post-postmodern cultural paradigm of metamodernism. As far as those concepts go, you can't really get many exponents much better than David Foster Wallace's magnum opus, Infinite Jest. Cataloged as an encyclopedic novel, it's set in a near-future North America where Quebec separatists, intelligence agents, tennis prodigies, and recovering addicts collide in a search for a fatally addictive film cartridge created by a late filmmaker.

The result is one of the most ambitious books of all time, a 1,000-page doorstop that serves as a potent statement about addiction, entertainment, isolation, and the endless search for sincere human connection. It's a towering artistic achievement packed with hundreds of endnotes with vital information, a challenging non-linear structure, and hyper-dense prose that makes reading multiple pages at a time all that much harder. However, it's also a brilliantly prophetic satire about entertainment and the media, full of hilarious set pieces and intelligent payoffs.

In a recent development, one of the most influential and groundbreaking writers of the 20th century, James Joyce wrote what many would consider a remarkably strong contender for the elusive title of The Great American Novel: Ulysses, one of the classics that belong on everyone's bucket list. This is a modernist mirror of Homer's Odyssey, following an ordinary Dublin advertiser and a young writer through a single day in their ordinary lives. Who could have possibly predicted that a book that simply narrates a mundane day could be so challenging?

According to recent reports, indeed, Joyce completely reinvents the rules that his book operates under with practically every single chapter, writing in an unfiltered stream of consciousness that never holds the reader's hand. Full of dense allusions to things like Catholic theology, Shakespearean scholarship, and Dublin lore, it's certainly not an easy masterpiece to get through, but it's a must-read nonetheless. But as challenging as it is, Ulysses is also the most admirable and humane portrait of an ordinary person ever written as fiction, an absolute masterwork that belongs on the bookshelf of everyone who loves books.

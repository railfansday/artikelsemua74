On Nov. 23, Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) staff noted US futures exchanges and derivatives clearinghouses may count Bitcoin, Ethereum, and qualifying payment stablecoins toward a rolling year of operating costs after applying valuation discounts.

A hypothetical selloff reveals how a firm can lose part of that buffer while its liquid assets still cover six months of expenses.

According to recent reports, the agency's announcement described the letter as staff guidance. Existing rules remain in force, and the letter offers no assurance that staff would decline to recommend enforcement.

Under the rules for designated contract markets and derivatives clearing organizations, exchanges and clearinghouses need resources to cover at least one year of projected operating costs on a rolling basis.

Within that annual pool, a separate liquidity test calls for enough unencumbered cash or highly liquid securities to cover at least six months of expenses.

The rules allow committed credit lines or similar facilities to make up a liquidity shortfall under certain conditions. The crypto assets in the advisory do not count toward that six-month test.

For the annual calculation, staff says they would raise no objection to the approach in the advisory, which includes a minimum valuation discount of 20% for Bitcoin and Ethereum and a 2% discount for qualifying payment stablecoins.

These discounts, known as haircuts, apply to current market value, so the amount a firm can count moves with the price.

Take a hypothetical exchange with $12 million in annual costs spread evenly across the year, $6 million in unencumbered cash or other eligible liquid assets, and a Bitcoin position worth $7.5 million.

Industry observers note that at a fixed 20% haircut, the Bitcoin counts for $6 million. Together, the liquid assets and the discounted Bitcoin position count for $12 million. At the stated benchmarks, $1 million worth of Bitcoin or Ethereum counts as $800,000, while $1 million in qualifying payment stablecoins counts as $980,000. The smaller discount for payment stablecoins comes with conditions on which tokens qualify.

Before the GENIUS Act takes effect, staff limit the stablecoin treatment to dollar-denominated tokens from a state-regulated money transmitter or trust organization, or a national trust bank.

The issuer must back the tokens with reserves limited to cash, US Treasuries, or overnight Treasury repurchase agreements, held directly or indirectly.

Monthly attestations must cover what the reserves hold and whether their fair value matches the amount in circulation.

Once the law takes effect, the advisory applies its definition and requires a permitted issuer or a foreign issuer that meets the law's applicable conditions.

Recent findings suggest that customer margin is posted against trading positions, and clearinghouses separately set aside resources to cover clearing-member defaults. The clearinghouse rule bars them from counting the same resources toward both default coverage and operating costs.

Sources indicate that the commission registered Backpack Clearing, formerly TLD Clearing Inc., on Nov. 13. Its registration order permits it to clear margined futures as well as fully collateralized futures, options on futures, and swaps.

Those records do not disclose what Backpack Clearing holds for operating costs or whether it uses the staff guidance.

The S&P 500 index (^GSPC -3.70%) includes the 500 largest publicly traded companies in America. Its name is often interchangeable with "the stock market" because the 500 large-cap stocks in this index make up about 80% of the total U.S. stock market capitalization.

So, when "the stock market" (as in the S&P 500) loses more than 10% of its value in a month, people get nervous. As of Nov. 20, since reaching its record high on Oct. 6, the S&P 500 is down about 11.6%. (Related: Man sees his cat on people’s faces everywhere after bra...)

The biggest driver of this recent stock market swoon is the same reason stocks have been delivering such strong results since 2022: the artificial intelligence (AI) industry. Big-spending AI hyperscalers and innovative AI frontier labs have been buoyed by investor enthusiasm for futuristic technology that promises to boost productivity and economic growth. (Related: Industry News: Buttigieg supporters launch group to sup...)

But to get to that bright future, these AI companies have been spending (and borrowing) hundreds of billions of dollars. Investors are starting to balk at the high price of AI. As a result of these AI fears, the tech-heavy Nasdaq-100 index has fallen even further than the S&P 500. The tech sector benchmark is down about 20% since Oct. 6 -- heading into a bear market.

Industry observers note that it's understandable to feel nervous when the stock market is falling like this, but investors need to take heart, stay calm, and have confidence in the future. If you've been investing as long as I have (or longer), you've lived through worse than this.

Here's one move I'm making with my investments right now and one I'm avoiding.

It's easy to say and harder to do, but it's true: When stock prices are plummeting, that's when I try to just keep buying more stocks. A 10% stock market correction or a 20% (or worse) bear market is ironically the best time to buy stocks. It means stocks are "on sale.".

It has been revealed that so that's what I'm doing. I'm going to keep buying stocks in a diversified portfolio of low-cost index funds. My favorite U.S. index fund is the Vanguard Morningstar Total Stock Market ETF (VTI -3.52%) because it lets me own 3,511 U.S. stocks of all sizes and industries, not just the tech sector.

Additionally, times when the U.S. stock market is struggling can be good occasions to diversify with international stocks. I'm going to keep buying the Vanguard Total International Stock ETF (VXUS -3.27%) and its remarkably diversified portfolio of 8,874 stocks from dozens of other countries beyond the U.S. market.

Eviedence indicates that corrections and bear markets are temporary. If you look at the long run of stock market history, the S&P 500 has always bounced back from even the biggest stock market crashes and economic crises.

Because I'm diversified with a portfolio of thousands of stocks from the U.S. and around the world, I believe that there's never a "bad time" to buy stocks. The stock market might keep falling for a while, even after I buy my next shares -- that's OK. I don't know which individual companies or industries will be the winners or losers -- that's fine, too. By owning these index funds, I'm betting on the stock market itself to sort out the winners for me.

Buying "all the stocks" this way lets me own a piece of the global economy’s long-term upside.

It's usually a bad idea for investors to sell stocks during a bear market. It's a little like selling a car or a house that's underwater on the loan -- it puts you in a disadvantaged position and locks in your losses.

(The only exception is if you have individual stock positions that you no longer believe in and want to sell at a loss for tax-loss harvesting purposes. That's a longer conversation to be had with a professional tax advisor.).

With a diversified portfolio of index funds that I'm holding for the long term, I'm not looking to sell anything right now. That's because I don't need the money out of my investment portfolio right now; I need it 20 or 30 years in the future. I don't need to liquidate my investment portfolio for cash. I don't need my stocks to go up today or tomorrow or next year. I need them to deliver long-term returns for the next 20 years or more.

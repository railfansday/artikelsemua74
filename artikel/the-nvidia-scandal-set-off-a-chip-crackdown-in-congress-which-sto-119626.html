According to recent reports, nvidia (NVDA -3.91%) has spent November at the center of a political storm, with Jensen Huang reinstated as CEO amid allegations that its chips were smuggled into China. The stock fell another 4% on Wednesday. But the firm most at risk from Washington's response may be one most investors don't connect to the scandal at all.

In a note published Wednesday, research firm TS Lombard said the Nvidia fallout has galvanized lawmakers, putting Congress on track to pass three to four latest chip export-control measures, most likely as part of the fiscal 2027 National Defense Authorization Act. Grace Fan, the firm's managing director of global policy and disruptive themes research, said passage is a question of "when" and not "if," though a government shutdown fight around the Dec. 11 funding deadline could push it into early 2027. In a recent development, the bills would close several loopholes at once. One would give Congress a say over exports of the most advanced AI chips. Another would require location tracking on exported AI chips to stop smuggling. A third would target Chinese companies renting U.S. chips through overseas cloud services. The centerpiece, the MATCH Act, goes after the machines that make chips rather than the chips themselves. (Related: Industry News: My Journey to a serverless transformers ...)

In a recent development, the MATCH Act would pressure U.S. allies, mainly the Netherlands and Japan, to match U.S. restrictions on the sale of chipmaking equipment to China. If they don't, TS Lombard said, the Senate version would direct the Commerce Department to impose the controls itself after about 150 days. The Senate bill also names every deep ultraviolet (DUV) lithography machine among the tools that couldn't go to China at all.

According to recent reports, that's why TS Lombard says the companies most at risk aren't U.S. equipment makers such as Applied Materials (AMAT -3.89%), Lam Research (LRCX -5.68%), and KLA (KLAC +0.41%), which already face U.S. rules. Instead, it points to ASML (ASML -2.47%), the Dutch lithography leader, and Japan's Tokyo Electron, along with several other Japanese suppliers. The Dutch government has said it opposes the bill.

Wednesday's trading didn't reflect that ranking. Lam fell 6% and Applied Materials 4%, while ASML dropped 2.5% to about $1,303, roughly 35% below its 52-week high.

In a recent development, there are two reasons ASML's exposure is shrinking. TS Lombard estimates that China will account for about 20% of ASML's sales this year, down from roughly 30% in fiscal 2025. Chinese chipmakers also front-loaded their purchases, building what the firm estimates is about three years' worth of DUV machines.

Industry observers note that that stockpile cuts both ways. It means fewer upcoming China orders were coming anyway, but it shifts the fight to servicing and spare parts, which the Senate version would also restrict. TS Lombard expects China to retaliate in measured ways, possibly by squeezing Dutch and Japanese companies, and sees a likely middle path in which some servicing licenses are traded for access to critical minerals.

According to recent reports, for long-term investors, yes, if you buy gradually. ASML is the only firm that makes the extreme ultraviolet (EUV) machines needed for the most advanced chips, and that position doesn't depend on China. A smaller China business is a hit to near-term sales, not to its long-term moat.

Sources indicate that the near-term risk is real, though. Headlines on the defense bill, the Dec. 11 funding deadline, and the Trump-Xi meeting at the G20 summit in Miami on Dec. 14-15 could all move the shares. Investors who want exposure to semiconductor stocks can use that volatility to build a position in stages rather than all at once, and keep an eye on Tokyo Electron and other tech stocks with heavy China exposure as the bill takes shape.

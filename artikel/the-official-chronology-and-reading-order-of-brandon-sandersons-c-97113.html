Recent findings suggest that brandon Sanderson's Cosmere is a multi-genre shared literary universe that has been going on since 2005. The premise is that thousands of years ago, in a far-away galaxy, a god known as Adonalsium shattered into 16 pieces called Shards. These Shards found their way to different planets in the galaxy, imbuing them with unique magic systems. Each stand-alone story or series is set on its own planet, with each world varying greatly in terms of culture, ecology, and geography. Yet, they do all connect in some way or another.

The Cosmere is planned to be a multi-decade event, and is not expected to conclude anytime soon. Right now, it consists of 33 entries, including short stories, novellas, novels, graphic novels, and even children's picture books. 33 stories to read is quite overwhelming at first glance, so if you've been looking for a place to start, look no further, because this is the definitive chronology and reading order of the Cosmere. The great thing about the Cosmere is that it's engineered to feature multiple entry points — you can generally pick it up from any series or stand-alone story you want and still understand things. Sanderson's debut novel, and the first Cosmere novel to be released, was Elantris in 2005, which many fans use as an entry point to the universe. However, seasoned fans do not recommend going this route, as Sanderson's writing is less refined. Instead, many fans suggest picking up the first era of Mistborn as an entry point, as it is more accessible, enticing, easier to read, and generally more exciting.

Sources indicate that however, another viable entry point is the White Sand graphic novel. Originally, White Sand was released as a trilogy, but the canon version is the omnibus version, which includes the original three plus an additional prologue sequence. While a lot of fans don't like White Sand as a story, it is a perfectly good graphic novel and highly crucial to the overall Cosmere. As it right now stands, White Sand is also the first story in the chronology, occurring before Elantris.

Industry observers note that if you really wanted to, though, you could start with Warbreaker, The Stormlight Archive, or any other stand-alone novel, but beginning with White Sand, Elantris, or Mistborn definitely grants a more streamlined approach and makes it easier to hop from one story to the next without needing to jump back and forth through the chronology. On his official website, Sanderson actually recommends reading the books in release order if in doubt, which is a crystal-clear recommendation that doesn't convolute things more than necessary. The "main" saga of the Cosmere begins with era one of Mistborn. This is followed by the stand-alone novel Warbreaker, which serves as a necessary prequel to The Stormlight Archive. Incidentally, this is what comes next, as the first arc of Stormlight occurs between the two eras of Mistborn. In fact, the final novel in Stormlight's first arc provides a smooth liaison to the second era of Mistborn.

Industry observers note that both of these series consist of multiple novels, as well as various short stories and novellas, which typically serve to fill the gaps between the main novels. Some recommend only reading the short stories or novels after you've finished the main novels in the series, but this isn't strictly necessary: they can be read in the order they chronologically occur, as well.

Four worlds and four completely different ideas of what a good life looks like. This is where you belong, what it would ask of you, and what you would get back in return.

According to recent reports, you belong somewhere old enough that the mountains have names and somebody can still tell you who gave them. Middle-earth is for people who think the right thing is worth doing even when it will not be finished in their lifetime.

You want the sea, a crew, and absolutely nobody telling you where you are allowed to go. The Grand Line is the world for people whose main ambition in life is finding out what is over there.

You would rather be feared and useful than liked and useless. The Continent is a world where the monsters are the straightforward part of the job and the people are where it all gets complicated.

You want the myths to be real and loose in the actual world, and you want to find out at about thirteen that you were never anywhere near as ordinary as everybody kept telling you.

According to recent reports, "Hoid's Travails" is the informal name given to a trilogy of "Secret Projects" that Sanderson wrote while in quarantine. These three novels are Tress of the Emerald Sea, Yumi and the Nightmare Painter, and The Fires of December, which just came out for backers and is expected to arrive in bookstores soon. This series is more a loose collection of stand-alone novels, each taking place on a different, minor world, with Hoid narrating the story. Other secret projects include The Sunlit Man and Isles of the Emberdark, though these are distinct entities from Hoid's Travails.

According to recent reports, the Sunlit Man absolutely has to be read after Stormlight, or readers will find themselves completely and utterly devoid of context. Also note that when it comes to Isles of the Emberdark, a revised version of the novella Sixth of the Dusk is fully integrated with the novel, so, assuming you read Isles of the Emberdark, Sixth of the Dusk can safely be skipped outright. There are also four picture books, dubbed "Hoid's Storybook Collection," that have just come out, best read alongside The Stormlight Archive because Hoid tells three of the stories to characters from that series, and the picture books are just illustrated versions of his fables. The fourth picture book, The ChasmFriends Get a Pet!, is non-canon and can safely be ignored, although it is a fun read.

Other than that, all that's left is the novellas The Emperor's Soul and Shadows for Silence: In the Forests of Hell. Truth be told, you can read these stand-alone tales pretty much whenever you want. They do have a place in the chronology, but you ultimately won't miss out on anything if you read them first or last. Note that most novellas and short stories can be found contained in a single volume, titled Arcanum Unbounded: The Cosmere Collection.

Industry observers note that this chronology is based on a combination of fan speculation and official word from Sanderson himself, though it is by no means the final timeline. As the Cosmere evolves, this timeline is subject to change. Buckle up, because it's been estimated that Sanderson is only about halfway done with the saga, if not a little bit less than that. It is known that a forthcoming series titled Dragonsteel is set to be the first chronological entry, even before White Sand, while the forthcoming final era of Mistborn is set to be the conclusion to the universe itself. These releases are pretty far away, however, so there's no need to worry about those just yet.

All of this can seem overwhelming, but if it's any reassurance, there really is no "right" way to read the books. Obviously, make sure you read each individual series in order, but other than that, you basically have free rein when it comes to entry and exit points.

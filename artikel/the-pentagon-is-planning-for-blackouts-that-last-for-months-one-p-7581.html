The Pentagon Is Planning for Blackouts That Last for Months. One Problem Could Doom the Strategy.

🌐 Source: Popularmechanics • 📅 17 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 6 min read (1161 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Picture a military base on day thirty of a regional blackout. Its generators are still going strong, but the local utility has stopped pumping water to the base. Keeping the lights on won’t do much good if there’s no water to cool computers or fight a fire.

The Pentagon wants bases to keep working through outages like this. In September, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth revealed FORTRESS America, a blueprint to help military bases withstand attacks and disruptions to civilian services. The goal was to keep essential operations going for at least three months without outside help. Pentagon installations official Dale Marks shared more details in October, explaining that power targets would range from 30 to 90 days, depending on the base, and critical supplies would need to be stocked for at least three months. Paul Stockton, a former assistant secretary of defense for homeland defense and Americas’ security affairs, tells Popular Mechanics that many defense installations “depend on civilian-operated water utilities that lie outside installation fencelines.” Suppose the base keeps its power on for three months. Can it still do its job if the services outside the gate stop working?

The first thing to fail may be the water.

Recent findings suggest that stockton puts water at the top of his concerns. Beyond drinking supplies, bases need it for fire suppression and computer cooling, uses he calls “absolutely vital for mission execution at most bases.” Sanitation depends on water, too. “Losing that electricity-dependent water utility support would have devastating effects in an extended conflict,” he notes. Even if the base’s generators are running perfectly, that won’t matter if there’s a fire and there’s no water running through the hydrants. Some missions need very little from the outside world. Stockton notes bases that launch nuclear strikes with bombers or other nuclear forces depend relatively little on civilian services. Bases that send troops overseas are different. For them, keeping the base running is only the first step.

A unit heading to a conflict in the Taiwan Strait could start its trip at a base hundreds of miles from the coast. Getting troops and equipment to a ship would mean relying on civilian transportation and a working seaport, Stockton claims. Keeping a base running through a blackout would be an achievement. But if its troops and equipment can’t get to the fight, the military still has a problem.

A reactor could help with the fuel trucks.

Keeping generators supplied creates another outside dependency. Stockton says bases commonly run on natural gas or diesel, and an extended outage could disrupt deliveries. Bases may have room to store substantial diesel reserves, but replenishing them through a region with disabled infrastructure would be difficult.

Small nuclear reactors could help. A microreactor could power a base for a long time without regular fuel deliveries. In August, the Army said it would spend up to $2.2 billion on five companies to build reactors at five bases. It hopes to have the first one running by September 2028. Reactors won’t be the only answer, though. Marks has said bases will also use other energy sources, and the mix will vary by location.

In a recent development, “You park the reactor, turn it on, and it just sits there being hot,” says Brendan Kochunas, an associate professor of nuclear engineering and radiological sciences at the University of Michigan. Once engineers can get it running reliably, a base could go a long time without a fresh delivery of reactor fuel. Diesel generators, by contrast, would keep burning through their fuel supply.

Industry observers note that the military microreactors are “very much still in development,” Kochunas states. Antares Nuclear, the company the Army chose to build a reactor at Fort Bragg, demonstrated a self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction in its Mark-0 reactor in June. Its follow-on Mark-1 is scheduled to demonstrate electricity production in 2027, according to the company’s congressional testimony.

Recent findings suggest that that leaves a significant question: can a reactor keep going on its own? Kochunas indicates the real test comes when a reactor runs at full power for a long stretch. That’s when engineers find out what needs fixing, “once you see everything heat up.” A single broken pump or sensor could leave a base waiting for outside help. That’s the same kind of help a blackout could cut off. For Kochunas, a reactor is only ready when it has shown it can run “without interaction, only observation, for the duration of the anticipated mission outage.”.

The exercise has to follow the mission.

Recent findings suggest that stockton claims the first step in knowing whether a base could really survive a blackout is a surprise test called a blackstart exercise. Without warning, the base cuts itself off from the grid and starts its own generators. Then it has to get power to the equipment its mission depends on. That’s harder than it sounds. “Such blackstart operations are challenging,” Stockton says. But a successful start only answers the first question—the next is how long the base can continue operating if fuel deliveries become unreliable or the water utility fails. An exercise that assumes those services will keep working could give commanders too much confidence in what the generators have accomplished.

Sources indicate that david Alexander, an emeritus professor of emergency planning and management at University College London, sees a similar problem in hospitals. They “function as part of the medical system. Hence, they do not work independently,” he says. Backup power keeps the building running, but it can’t replace everything else a hospital depends on to do its job.

The strongest fortress may extend beyond the fence.

Industry observers note that so where should the money go? Stockton notes one of the best investments might not be on the base at all. The pumps that send water to the base sit at the local water utility. So if a blackout shuts them down, the base’s taps and hydrants will go dry. Helping the utility keep its own power on could do more for the mission than spending on the base itself. Stockton also wants planners to think bigger than one base and its town. In a 2026 report for the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory, he proposed choosing large sections of the grid, some covering several states, that could cut themselves off when trouble started spreading elsewhere. He calls them “microgrids on steroids.”.

Sources indicate that first, utilities would need a initiative to keep the power on inside each section. If that works, a region’s seaports could keep running along with its bases, and troops and equipment would have a real chance of reaching their ships. Hegseth has promised that if an adversary takes down the grid, “our bases stay lit.” That’s an key goal, but just keeping the lights on isn’t enough. The real goal is getting troops to where the fight is—and a base that can’t do that is just a well-lit place to wait.

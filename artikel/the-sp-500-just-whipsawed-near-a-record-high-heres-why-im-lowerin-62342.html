It has been revealed that volatility in the S&P 500 (^GSPC -3.70%) has picked up since the midterm elections. That week was the index's worst since April 2025, and it now sits roughly 10% below its all-time high.

According to recent reports, that kind of volatility doesn't usually happen when the S&P 500 is near its all-time highs. It's often considered a sign that the conditions that lifted stock prices to records may no longer be in place. And investors are dealing with some real concerns about where the market heads from here.

While bounces like the one that took place the week ending Nov. 13 might feel like relief, history reveals that might not be the case. (Related: AI breakthrough cuts energy use by 100x while boosting ...)

I went back and looked at S&P 500 history since 1970. Specifically, I looked for periods of market swings that look just like the one we experienced. Over history, there were 19 instances in which the index fell by at least 2.5% one week and rebounded by more than 2.5% the next week, while still within reach of an all-time high.

The good news is that recent history has shown solid gains for the S&P 500 following these periods. Since 2018, all six occurrences produced positive returns six months later, with a median return of more than 11%.

Industry observers note that the bad news is that prior to that, the results are incredibly mixed. There are more instances of negative forward returns, and the gains six months later were often minimal. Plus, some of these examples came during some of history's worst bear markets.

Industry observers note that for instance, “as women navigate through the wonderful world of the menopause transition, there are changes, health-wise, that happen,” says Leslie Bonci, RD, a sports dietitian based in Pittsburgh. As your hormone levels start to decrease, you may be more likely to lose bone density and muscle mass. When these changes happen, “there are certain nutrients that are going to be needed more than others,” she adds. That’s when it can be helpful to turn to a supplement. It has been revealed that knowing which supplements you should take in each stage of life may be complicated, but we found the right experts to simplify everything. Consider this your supplement roadmap.

Recent findings suggest that first, have a conversation with your primary care physician—they should know about all of the medications and supplements you’re at the moment taking or are considering. Your doc will help you ensure supplementation is the best course of action after considering your holistic health care proposal. Then, they can rule out any potential medication interactions and decide which dosage is best, states Navya Mysore, MD, a primary care physician, women's health expert, and WH advisory board member based in New York City.

It has been revealed that you also might want to get blood work done before starting a supplement, especially if you think you may have a deficiency. “Checking things like iron stores (ferritin), vitamin D, or B12 can tell you if you actually need a supplement and how much,” indicates Dr. Mysore. However, you don’t need blood work if you’re just taking a basic prenatal or multivitamin.

Sources indicate that just remember: “Supplements are a complement to—not a replacement for—eating,” Bonci indicates. They’re just one piece of the puzzle; in order to live your healthiest, longest, best life, you’ll want to make sure you’re eating nutritiously and exercising consistently.

In a recent development, sorry to our gummy lovers out there: “Capsules usually win—they tend to absorb better and have fewer fillers,” states Dr. Mysore. “Tablets are fine but a bit more variable.” Unfortunately, gummies are often underdosed and may not include key nutrients like iron, she adds. So, your safest bet is a capsule.

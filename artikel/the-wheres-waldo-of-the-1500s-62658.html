Sources indicate that this summer I did a two-week trip to Italy with my girlfriend starting in Rome, spending a night in Montepulciano, and finishing up in Florence. In addition to eating copious gelato and pasta and blasting AC in the Airbnbs to cool off after 100°F days, we visited a lot of museums. One of these was Le Gallerie degli Uffizi in Florence, which is known for its collection of Botticellis, Da Vincis, and Caravaggios. 1 Medusa is Caravaggio's better-known piece there, but I much preferred Bacchus—placid where he's usually revelrous, dirty pillows concealed beneath the opulence, leaves beginning to brown and fruit beginning to rot, a delicate Venetian tazza held in dirty fingers—a god subtly on the turn.

And yeah, The Birth of Venus is good. Really good. 2 Though in my opinion Primavera gets slept on. Venus demonstrates one nipple and everyone loses their mind! But the famous pieces weren't what I kept thinking about after we left.

Instead, three pieces stuck with me. The first was van Honthorst's Supper with a Lute Player, which certainly relies on Caravaggio's popularization of chiaroscuro, but in a way that feels more inviting than stark, van Honthorst's light drawing me in where Caravaggio's shadows push me out. The second piece was Andrea Commodi's The Fall of the Rebel Angels. My photo of this is likewise quite bad, but it gives a certain cinéma vérité, so I'll force you to click in again to actually make out the piece. This is a preparatory painting for a fresco that never materialized, but for me that only adds to it, the incomplete nature of the piece a parallel to the angels' fall. It feels like an Escher piece out of time, fractal bodies paying the singular price of a plural sin, unified by the lack of Satan: for they are angels, all. Here's the placard that was next to the piece (emphasis is mine).

Herri de Bles detto MET DE BLES o il CIVETTA.

The ore being mined in the picture is iron, by the indirect method which became widespread in the 16th century. The action takes place in the foreground, peopled with tiny figures, against a backdrop of woods and tall mountains. Civetta was nicknamed "the owl" for his habit of signing some of his works—including this one—with a picture of the bird. His paintings were much appreciated for their originality and naturalistic detail. This Landscape may have entered the Medici collections precisely because the subject of mines tied in with Francesco I's interest in raw materials and mining techniques.

Why have I never heard of this guy before?

The opening sentence of his Wikipedia page immediately sets the stage: "Herri met de Bles, also known as Henri Bles, Herri de Dinant, Herry de Patinir, (c. 1490 – after 1566)". There's nothing quite like being immediately hit by three versions of someone's first name and a lower bound on death date to realize that we just don't know that much. In fact, much as your high school English teachers cautioned, 10 I'm not sure if they're still worried about this. Maybe the latest evil is LLM citations, not Wikipedia. Looking at the Rijksmuseum biography for his piece Paradise 12 There's no known owl in it, though there are a lot of animals. immediately counters the 1490 birthdate:.

In a recent development, "Today it is generally accepted that Herri met de Bles 13 "met de Bles" is not really a last name, it's an epithet meaning "with the blaze" in Dutch. This ostensibly refers to a white streak in his hair, which is referenced in van Mander's 1604 Het Schilder-Boeck (though unfortunately not capturable by Jan Wierix's 1572 monochromatic engraving). is probably the same person as the ‘Herry de Patenir’ who was registered as a free master in the ledgers of the Antwerp painters’ guild in 1535. His date of birth is accordingly placed around 1510; in the past it was situated between 1490 and 1500.".

All of this is vague for one reason, which also explains why there's no definitive naming for his work.

"No information about the painter Herri met de Bles written during his lifetime has been found, and none of his works are signed or dated.".

Sources indicate that this likely also explains why 'il Civetta' took off as a nickname/calling card: there's no other way to unify his works. The biography also explains where the estimate of his death comes from.

It has been revealed that "Bles’s date of death is also unknown, but according to a handwritten note in an early edition of Lampsonius he was still alive in 1550. This agrees with Van Mander’s statement that Bles was the teacher of Frans Mostaert (c. 1534-60), who was about 16 years old in 1550. Van Mander probably got that information from Bartholomeus Spranger, who was himself a pupil of Mostaert’s. Guicciardini lists Bles [Hendrick van Dinant] in his Descrittione di tutti i paesi bassi of 1567 among other artists who were deceased by then. Thus the publishing date of 1567 functions as a terminus ante quem 14 Terminus ante quem or TAQ for 'limit before which', or the latest it could happen. This is in contrast to Terminus post quem, or TPQ for 'limit after which', or the earliest something could happen. Thus any event is bounded by [TPQ..TAQ], or in the case of his death, [1550..1567]. for Bles’s year of death.".

This also makes Wikipedia's "after 1566" likely outright wrong, as I couldn't find any source for it. 15 Wikipedia references "recently discovered contemporary documents" but an edit conjured that terminology out of nowhere, and likely just stems from a misreading of "before 1567" (I emailed RKD to find out). Regardless, it's hard to know who he was, or when and where he lived and died. The lack of signatures also makes it hard to conclusively delineate originals from copies, either by his contemporaries or by those in his Antwerp workshop or even by Herri himself. 16 Louisa Wood Ruby has a really well-written and interesting piece about how copies are handled in historical art scholarship that I enjoyed reading and recommend.

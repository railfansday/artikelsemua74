When business leaders hit a difficult patch, they have historically relied on family, business partners, mentors, and peers for emotional support and guidance. Many respond by working harder or taking practical steps, such as changing business practices; more than a few turn to prayer.

But in an era of AI and rising business pressures, even these traditional sources of support are being tested. A survey of 1,250 American business owners and senior leaders found that 60% felt more stressed than the previous year. Meanwhile, another recent poll found that 38% of executives and aspiring C-suite leaders have considered leaving their role over the last 12 months. Of course, it’s not just the C-suite feeling the strain. Workers report having to weather stress, uncertainty, and AI. Already eroded by email and WFH, the line between home and office has become increasingly permeable. To some extent, psychological well-being is now a workplace concern in ways it simply wasn’t a generation ago. (Related: Latest Update: AI lets chemists design molecules by sim...)

Sources indicate that CEOs are experiencing the same blurring of boundaries. And some are increasingly asking, if they already need therapy in their off-hours, why not draw on its techniques while they are on-call too, as a way to sustain their performance under pressure?

But isn’t that what executive coaching is for? A 2025 survey of 90 current and former CEOs by Stanford Graduate School of Business, the Hoover Institution and The Miles Group found a majority (58%) do use a professional coach, up from 34% a decade earlier. Overall, 95% say they use either professional coaches or informal advisers to help them think through important organizational complications.

In a recent development, but that’s about organizational issues. What’s interesting is that senior leadership is increasingly seeking mental health support. In 2024, 85% of executive coaches surveyed by the International Coaching Federation noted clients were asking for help with mental well-being. This is the fresh world of therapy-informed executive coaching.

Understanding real motivations and blind spots.

According to recent reports, despite the growing openness about mental health, not everyone is keen to admit to seeing a therapist. But helping leaders understand how their personal histories, emotional patterns and relationships shape the way they lead seems a logical way to unlock deeper change.

I can confirm this is happening, as some of these executives are in my office or on Zoom with me every week. I’m not here to critique mainstream executive training or leadership development, although I do wonder whether generic leadership skills training can really prepare someone for the realities of being a CEO today. What my clients tell me they want from the approaches I’m developing with them is help navigating the pressures, relationships and personal patterns that shape how they lead.

They can access help on practical management techniques and frameworks. What’s often missing from conventional executive training is the deeper work to understand the underlying emotional and relational patterns influencing decisions and behavior.

According to recent reports, whether it’s a VC, a founder, or a tech leader, the aim is the same: to help them achieve “radiant change”, lasting change that comes from within, rather than applying a veneer to outward behavior. This involves a focus on the past, the present, and the future. The first step is exploring how their upbringing, family dynamics, and cultural background have shaped how they respond to conflict, relationships and pressure.

Industry observers note that understanding where those patterns come from helps leaders decide which serve them well and which constrain them, opening the way to a more authentic and nuanced approach to leadership. Here we begin looking at current relationships from a three-dimensional perspective, exploring what was remarked and felt. Understanding these patterns in slow-motion replay allows upcoming relational skills to be built.

By looking beyond the business itself to understand themselves better, leaders can develop the self-awareness, empathy and emotional intelligence they need to lead teams, navigate conflict and build stronger stakeholder relationships.

A new face for the talking cure?

It has been revealed that isn’t this simply conventional therapy? I would argue not. While the approach draws on psychotherapeutic techniques, this coaching is guided by the aim of helping the client become a more effective leader.

According to recent reports, finally, there are cultural aspects to this trend that make me wonder if this isn’t a specifically American phenomenon. While there is interest in leaders exhibiting empathy, self-awareness, and relational intelligence, Silicon Valley’s legacy of experimentation and self-actualization may also be driving this shift.

According to recent reports, in the late 2020s, founders are pushing beyond conventional coaching and support models of their predecessors. Many of my clients tell me they are working punishing hours to get their AI ideas to market. They recognize that their personal lives, emotional well-being and professional effectiveness are interconnected, and that addressing these connections can help them navigate the extraordinary demands they face today.

Franchise owners already have a business model to follow, but they still have to build a strong operation in each market. Here are five signs I look for in deciding whether to expand, plus one that overrides them all.

In a recent development, start with an honest look at your financials. Strong sales are encouraging, but steady profitability tells you more about whether your business is ready to expand.

Sources indicate that look beyond a great month or a busy season. Your first location should generate enough to cover operating expenses, royalties, financing obligations, and a reasonable salary for you, with money left over. Review both profitability and cash flow, since they don’t always move together.

Recent findings suggest that also, consider how the business performs during slower periods. If it needs constant cash infusions to get through normal seasonal fluctuations, strengthening that operation should come first.

Next, separate the cost of opening from the cost of getting established. You’ll need money for both.

A new location may take time to attract customers and cover its expenses. Therefore, your budget should account for startup costs, ongoing operating needs, and a cushion for delays or slower sales. Build that plan around realistic assumptions about your brand and market.

According to recent reports, before adding another unit, ask yourself what happens if it takes longer than expected to gain traction. You should be able to support the launch while protecting the original business’s working capital. Otherwise, one difficult opening could put both operations under pressure.

Sources indicate that can you leave for a week without the operation falling apart? That’s a useful test because a second business will (or should) demand a great deal of your attention.

Some of our franchise owners have told me, “Man, I left for a week, and we had our best week ever.” Once you get over the discovery that you’re not irreplaceable, take that as an encouraging sign.

Sources indicate that however, you need more than one dependable person. Look for employees who can step into leadership roles, supported by clear expectations, training, and regular reporting.

Our manufacturer has a policy of promoting leaders from within. Existing team members already know your standards and your customers. They’ll still need management training, but you’re building on an established relationship. If you hire externally, give yourself enough time to bring that person into the business before opening day.

Pay attention to where your customers live and work. A steady stream from a nearby area may suggest an opportunity to serve them closer to home.

It doesn’t prove there’s no competition, though. Nor does it guarantee enough demand to support another unit. Instead, treat those customers as a starting point for research.

Study the area’s demographics, competitors, site access and territory availability with your franchisor. Then consider whether the latest business will attract additional customers or mainly shift sales away from your first location.

You want evidence that the opportunity extends beyond a handful of enthusiastic requests to open closer to someone’s neighborhood.

Financial readiness matters, but so do your expectations. You need the patience to let a latest operation establish itself.

Recent findings suggest that when I started Signarama 40 years ago, people compared it with Minuteman Press, which my dad had founded. Minuteman had been operating for 20 years. Yet people still pointed to its higher earnings as evidence that it was the better business.

That wasn’t a useful comparison. One business had a long history; the other was just getting started.

According to recent reports, similarly, when you open a second location, measure it against realistic launch milestones. Track its progress in sales, customer retention, and operating performance. Use what you learned in the original business, while recognizing that the latest team needs time to build relationships and momentum.

Finally, you have to care enough to show up and do the work.

It has been revealed that I recently spoke with a franchise owner who had opened another location in a different state. He hadn’t visited in 90 days. He stated things were going fine, but I told him he needed to be there regularly, especially during the startup period.

It has been revealed that if you want to grow, you have to go. That’s it. You don’t have a choice. You have to be there on a consistent basis. I go to Australia every year to visit with our franchisees there, and I was in India recently for grand openings of two fresh locations for one of our brands.

All the signs about financing and staffing and market penetration mean nothing if you don’t care about doing everything you can to make your latest location a success. Are you committed to the expansion?

If you’re not committed to it, fully committed to it, don’t do it.

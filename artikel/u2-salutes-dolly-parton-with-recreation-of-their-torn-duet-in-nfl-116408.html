Recent findings suggest that U2 paid tribute to Dolly Parton as part of its halftime performance Wednesday night at the NFL‘s first-ever Thanksgiving Eve game. The “duet” between a live Bono and a recording of Parton from last summer was the centerpiece of a highly promoted 10-minute set between halves of the contest between the Los Angeles Rams and Green Bay Packers, streamed exclusively on Netflix. (Related: Summer at Hugging Face...)

It has been revealed that U2 did not attempt to cram as many songs into their brief performance as other superstars have during halftime performances, getting in four. The band opened with an abridged “Sunday Bloody Sunday” and ended with “”Pride (in the Name of Love)” and Where the Streets Have No Name” — only squeezing in one song from their latest album, the Parton duet, “Torn,” with images of Dolly projected onto three huge curtains that dropped around their stage at the 50-yard line. In a recent development, about a hundred extras took part in the performances — forming not a marching band, but a marching choir. U2 was also joined by the Womack Sisters, a vocal trio that has just been nominated for best new artist at the Grammys.

Sources indicate that the dancers first appeared as part of “Sunday Bloody Sunday,” which included an interpolation of the Supremes’ “Stop in the Name of Love,” rendered as something more somber, as performed by the Womack Sisters, to better fit in with U2’s theme of civil war. Bono repeated the “torn” part of that early ’80s anthem as a segue into the song “Torn,” as he and the spectral Parton harmonized on the recent song about a family at war. After one last look at Dolly, the curtains dropped to the field and the dancers formed three concentric circles around the band’s platform, repeating the familiar strains of of “Where the Streets Have No Name” and then “Pride (in the Name of Love)” as the Womacks joined U2 on stage.

Sources indicate that technically, five U2 songs made their way into the set, as eagle-eyed or -eared U2 fans were able to catch a bit of “American Obituary” during “Sunday Bloody Sunday.” The song, which appeared on a recent EP from the band, references the killing by ICE of Renee Good. The four key songs were just condensed enough that U2’s members were actually able to use part of their allotted 10 minutes walking to the stage at the beginning and walking back out through the assembled marchers at the end, in casual yet dramatic fashion.

Some political subtext relevant to the times was seen by at least a small subset of fans in that assemblage of material, although it hardly would have been evident to less hardcore fans or strictly NFL-oriented viewers. That was evident in connecting “Sunday Bloody Sunday,” about the Irish conflicts, with the “American Obituary” snippet. Then, bringing in the Parton song might have been a no-brainer anyway, as her part on the duet was said to be the last studio recording ever made by the superstar before she died in August. But beyond that, the theme of the tune has been described as reflecting the kinds of squabbles that occur among families when relatives get together for Thanksgiving and air their political differences around the dinner table, with Parton playing the role of the mother who wants everyone to mend what drives them apart.

Although this was the NFL’s first such Thanksgiving Eve halftime show, it is in a very recent tradition of superstars doing holiday halftimes; the previous ones have just been for Christmas. On Dec. 24, 2024, a Baltimore/Houston game featured a performance by the latter city’s hometown favorite, in the “Beyoncé Bowl.” For Christmas Day 2025, the NFL enlisted Snoop Dogg for a “Holiday Halftime Party.” No intends have been revealed as of yet for a Christmas halftime extravaganza from the NFL.

U2’s camp reportedly approached the NFL themselves with the idea of doing a Thanksgiving halftime show — obviously timed to capitalize not just on families gathered around the TV before a holiday, but, from U2’s perspective, following closely on the heels of the Nov. 13 release of “Carnaval de Luz,” the band’s first album of new material in nine years. The album debuted at No. 4 on the latest Billboard 200 chart.

It was U2’s third tango with the NFL, albeit its first in two decades. The Irish band famously did the Super Bowl halftime show in 2002, with an emphasis on honoring the victims of 9/11. In 2006, the group performed in celebration of the opening of the Louisiana Superdome after Hurricane Katrina.

The group has kept up a heavy cycle of broadcast and streaming promotions before and since the new album release, including a surprise opening to the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame ceremony at L.A.’s Peacock Theatre on Nov. 14, which is being edited into a special to be broadcast Dec. 8 on ABC.

Sources indicate that on Monday, U2 announced the itinerary for a 15-date tour of North American stadiums in 2027, on top of already announced shows in Central America and Europe. The tour has them returning to the site of their NFL performance, SoFi Stadium in Inglewood, Calif., presumably for much longer performances, on oct. 1-2, 2027.

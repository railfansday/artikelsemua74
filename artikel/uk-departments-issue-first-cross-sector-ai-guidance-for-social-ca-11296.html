Recent findings suggest that the Department of Health and Social Care and Department for Education announced joint national guidance on November 20, 2026, covering artificial intelligence (AI) use in children’s and adult social care. They described it as the first cross-sector guidance of its kind and cited a local pilot reporting a 63% reduction in administrative time.

The guidance gives leaders, practitioners and sector organisations a common national framework, including local authorities and providers making adoption decisions. Alongside practical advice, it addresses governance, accountability, data protection and professional oversight, with the aim of making adoption more consistent and increasing confidence across the sector. Potential uses include transcribing and summarising case notes, translating information, searching complex case histories and identifying gaps in support. The objective is to reduce administrative work so staff can spend more time supporting people and families, while making services easier to understand and access. It has been revealed that the pilot cited in the announcement took place within Swindon Borough Council’s adult social care work and used an AI tool to support social workers. The government reports that a typical Care Act assessment conversation shortened from 1.5 hours to 30 minutes, while assessment write-ups fell from four hours to 1.5 hours. Staff with visual impairments and those for whom English is not their first language saw particular benefits from the tool.

It has been revealed that at Peterborough council, staff built a bespoke assistant called Hey Geraldine using the knowledge of an experienced occupational therapist, giving colleagues immediate access to guidance. Its reported totals were more than 1,200 questions answered and around 15 minutes saved per interaction.

Children and Families Minister Josh MacAlister said technology cannot substitute for a social worker’s knowledge and skill, presenting administrative relief as a way to preserve time for listening to children, working with families and securing the support and protection they need.

Isabelle Trowler, Chief Social Worker at the Department for Education, remarked the guidance recognises AI’s potential while keeping practitioners and their professional judgement central. “Organisations must take responsibility for governing, implementing and supporting its use,” she said.

The government reports that four in five care providers now use digital social care records, helping almost 90% of people who draw on care. The digitisation effort is intended to cut paper records, repeated conversations, errors and inaccuracies.

The wider aim is for all care providers to be fully digitised by the end of the current Parliament. For local authorities and providers, the practical task is to turn the national principles into informed adoption decisions: the local projects offer measures of administrative benefit, while organisational accountability and practitioners’ professional judgement remain central to responsible use.

Women in Cambridgeshire are the first to join a UK trial testing whether AI can take on the second check of a screening mammogram.

The study will compare two AI approaches with the usual two-reader process, and researchers will measure how many cancers each approach detects and how often women are called back for further tests.

NIHR Cambridge reported the first enrollments on Nov. 23. The study, known as EDITH, aims to enroll 660,000 women across about 30 UK breast screening centers, according to the ISRCTN trial registry.

Eligible women receive information about the trial and how to opt out with their screening invitation. The appointment itself proceeds as usual, and every mammogram in the trial still gets at least one human read. (Related: Introducing the latest Microsoft IQ: Putting your organ...)

EDITH randomly assigns clinic days to one of three approaches, and each participating center uses all three. Under standard screening, two NHS experts read the images. In one trial arm, an expert reads the mammogram first, then AI checks for signs of cancer the expert may have missed. (Related: Latest Update: Hugging Face Reads, Feb. 2021 - Long-ran...)

In the other AI arm, the software assesses the images first, and one NHS expert reads mammograms the tool rates as low likelihood for cancer, and two read those it rates as high likelihood.

The registered study design treats at least one additional cancer detected per 1,000 women screened as a meaningful gain. For recalls, it sets a comparison margin of up to two extra callbacks per 1,000 women screened relative to standard double reading.

Both rates are calculated across all women screened.

Recent findings suggest that the two rates capture different steps in screening, and staff counts a callback when they ask a woman to return for more tests. The cancer detection rate counts cancers found during the screening episode.

Sources indicate that researchers will count how many readings staff carry out, how many cases need an extra expert review, and how many women are called back for assessment. The study will also track cancers found between screening rounds.

NIHR Cambridge says the NHS would change routine screening only if the evidence demonstrates the latest approach is as safe and effective as the care women receive today.

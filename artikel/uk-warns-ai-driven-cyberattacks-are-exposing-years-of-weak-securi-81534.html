Recent findings suggest that the U.K. government’s cybersecurity unit claims it has seen a “marked rise” in AI-driven cyberattacks in recent months, as hackers increasingly rely on autonomous hacking tools to identify and exploit simple weaknesses to break into systems and networks.

It has been revealed that the U.K. National Cybersecurity Center (NCSC) remarked it has seen several cyber incidents since September involving autonomous hacking tools, such as penetration testing tools used by defenders to stress-test their cybersecurity defenses. The NCSC claims that while none of the vulnerabilities being exploited are new, the speed at which these AI tools are able to carry out simple hacks has changed. (Related: AI breakthrough cuts energy use by 100x while boosting ...)

The rise in AI-related cyberattacks coincides with the broader release earlier this year of open source autonomous penetration testing tools like Artex and Cairn, among others, which anyone can download and use at no cost. The U.S. Federal Bureau of Investigation recently warned that malicious hackers are using AI penetration testing tools to carry out cyberattacks at unprecedented speeds.

While cyberattacks and intrusions are on the rise, the message from London is that many of these attacks are preventable with basic cybersecurity defenses and best practices.

“Notably, the challenges being exploited presently are not novel memory corruption vulnerabilities (or even known but unpatched vulnerabilities), but unmitigated weaknesses that are often the result of years of technical debt that organisations have accumulated,” the NCSC wrote.

The unit remarked that agentic hacking tools often look for exposed or internet-facing systems, then rapidly run through lists of weak or easy-to-guess passwords or abuse API endpoints often used by developers to access data.

The NCSC remarked that AI “does not make established cyber security controls obsolete,” but rather that “building strong foundations has never mattered more.”.

The unit added that AI has made it easier to find vulnerabilities and faster to exploit them, warning that weaknesses that have gone unnoticed for years can now be found in minutes.

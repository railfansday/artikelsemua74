US Senate approves bill to speed up permitting reviews for energy projects and datacenters

Bipartisan legislation now goes to the House amid concerns by some Democrats and environmental groups.

The Senate approved bipartisan legislation on Thursday aimed at speeding up permitting reviews to build power plants, transmission lines and other energy infrastructure as lawmakers seek to increase the nation’s energy supplies to meet surging demand and lower electricity prices. In a recent development, the legislation’s sweeping changes would shorten the timeline to start massive energy projects and make sure data centers pay for the energy they use. Anger over datacenters and rising electricity prices helped usher in a wave of Democrats pledging to clamp down on the energy-hungry behemoths. Recent findings suggest that the 62-33 vote is the first major action by Congress since midterm elections that soundly repudiated Donald Trump and congressional Republicans. The bill now goes to the House, which approved a more limited, industry-friendly version last year.

The Senate bill would overhaul several environmental laws to make it easier to build energy infrastructure, drawing criticism from Democrats and environmental groups about limits on judicial review and changes to bedrock laws such as the National Environmental Policy Act, Clean Water Act and Endangered Species Act.

It has been revealed that trump urged approval of the bill, calling it a “MAJOR WIN for US ENERGY INDEPENDENCE and DOMINANCE” in a recent social media post. The bill, formally known as the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026, will ensure the US can build pipelines, mines and power plants more swiftly, “lowering Energy Costs for American Families”, Trump wrote.

According to recent reports, fifteen Democrats, including Senate minority leader Chuck Schumer, voted for the bill. Two Republicans, Josh Hawley of Missouri and Mike Rounds of South Dakota, voted no.

Major industry groups representing developers in clean energy and the oil and gas sector support the bill as a way to deliver more electricity to the grid and avoid delays for infrastructure projects that can stretch a decade or more.

However, many environmental organizations have denounced the measure, saying it would lock in fossil fuel development, weaken environmental safeguards and make it harder for communities to challenge energy project approvals.

“Make no mistake: This backroom deal will pave the way for even more fossil fuel projects like gas power plants for data centers, oil and gas pipelines and (liquefied natural gas) terminals while sacrificing fundamental and common-sense public health and environmental protections for communities across the US,” said Jen Duggan, executive director of the Environmental Integrity Project.

Benefits for renewable energy are outweighed by likely increases in greenhouse gas emissions and air and water pollution from the fossil fuel industry, Duggan and other critics mentioned. She called the bill “a gift to some of the biggest polluters in America” and complained that it was being rushed through a lame-duck Congress without public hearings or sufficient debate.

A final vote on the measure was held up this week as Oregon’s two Democratic senators pushed to add amendments, including one to ban datacenters on public lands.

Senators Jeff Merkley and Ron Wyden are among several Democrats in both chambers who say the party should use its stronger position in the next Congress to pass legislation that boosts clean energy, such as solar and wind power, while limiting expansion of oil, natural gas and other fossil fuels.

In a recent development, “Getting more renewable electrons where they are needed doesn’t tackle climate change if you simultaneously promote the burning of more fossil fuels. Unfortunately, this bill does exactly that,” Merkley said.

Majority Republicans stymied Merkley’s effort to ensure votes on a host of amendments, including one that would block the Trump administration from continuing to cancel clean energy grants and other Democratic priorities for “purely political purposes”.

The bill’s sponsors reneged on promises of “a robust amendment process”, he noted. In the end, the measure was approved without amendments offered by rank-and-file senators.

“I do think that this bill is very consequential to the future of this planet,” Merkley said.

Major environmental groups, including the Sierra Club and National Wildlife Federation, oppose the bill, citing concerns about limits on judicial review and changes to the National Environmental Policy Act and other laws. The environmental policy law, known as Nepa, requires agencies to consider a project’s possible impacts and allows the public to be heard before a project is approved. The current review process often takes five years or more, and shortening that time frame is a key goal of the bill.

According to recent reports, “In America today, it takes too long and costs too much to build,” stated Wyoming senator John Barrasso, the No 2 Republican in the Senate. “Energy projects that would help lower prices remain frozen for years” as they await approval by the federal government.

“Construction cannot start without first spending years in the regulatory penalty box,” driving up costs and creating uncertainty, Barrasso stated.

The bill’s four main champions – two Democrats and two Republicans – have released a detailed set of amendments designed to bolster support for the legislation.

The four senators, who lead the chamber’s energy and environment panels, negotiated the deal a few weeks before the election. Republican senators Shelley Moore Capito of West Virginia and Mike Lee of Utah and Democratic senators Martin Heinrich of Fresh Mexico and Sheldon Whitehouse of Rhode Island say Americans are tired of paying more in utility bills while projects that could lower costs sit stuck in the planning phase for years.

“We are going to make it easier to build in America again, and make life more affordable for American families,” Lee said.

Whitehouse, a longtime climate hawk who has been criticized by some allies for supporting the permitting bill, stated the measure is “the floor, not the ceiling, of our climate and environmental aspirations”, adding that he hopes “to accomplish major things with our big Democratic majorities” over the next two years.

But Democratic senator Maria Cantwell of Washington state remarked the bill has been rushed and its impacts are not fully considered.

Cantwell, who met Tuesday with tribal, outdoor recreation and conservation leaders, said the bill would undermine tribal nations and weaken protections for the nation’s bodies of water, land and wildlife.

Recent findings suggest that “Now is the time to slow this process down, to actually have a formal process” to debate and amend the bill, she stated. “Don’t sacrifice the Clean Water Act or hunting or fishing grounds, historic or sacred sites, or protections of tribal sovereignty. We all need new sources of energy and power. But this bill, as it’s currently drafted, is not the answer.”.

It Looks Exactly Like Steak Vibe coding works like this. You describe what you want. The model hands you something. It runs. The UI is clean. The buttons have rounded corners. There is a dark mode you didn't ask for. Someone on your team says "wow.".

This is the marbled filet in the freezer case.

Industry observers note that the demo is the one moment in a program's life where nothing is asked of it. No real consumers. No bad input. No 3 a.m. traffic spike. No auditor. It just has to sit there under good lighting and look delicious. Generated code is extremely good at sitting under good lighting.

That is not a criticism of the model. That is a description of what it was asked to do. You asked for something that looks like steak. You got it.

The Red Is Beets A food printer is a shape machine. It is superb at the outside: the grain, the marbling, the exact silhouette of a filet. What's inside is a different question. Reported ingredients for the printed line are soy, wheat and rice proteins, oils, starches, flavorings, and beetroot powder for color.

That is not poison. It's just not what the picture promised.

Vibe-coded apps have beetroot powder too. The outside is perfect. Then you cut in and find: • Error handling that catches everything and logs nothing • A retry loop with no backoff, aimed directly at a paid API • Secrets in a config file, helpfully committed • A database query inside a loop inside another loop • Tests that assert the function returns something • A dependency nobody has heard of, last updated during a previous administration.

None of this highlights up in the screenshot. All of it shows up in production. Usually on a Friday.

It has been revealed that you're Supposed to Eat This One The old plastic burger in a restaurant window was honest. Nobody bit it. Everyone understood the deal: a picture of food, made of not-food.

Printed steak is different. It's meant to be eaten. It sits next to real food, priced like premium food, and the label lists what's in it without mentioning how it was made.

Recent findings suggest that that is the vibe coding problem exactly. Generated code doesn't stay in the window as a demo. It goes into the kitchen. It gets plated. It gets served to customers. And nothing on the package notes "a model wrote this and nobody read it.".

Recent findings suggest that I've been writing software for 45 years. I started in assembly and C, where the machine told you immediately and personally when you were wrong.

According to recent reports, modern tools are far more polite. They'll let you ship the printed filet with a smile. The feedback loop that used to take one segfault now takes one incident review.

In Fairness to the Printer I'm not anti-printed-food. Food scientists point to real upsides: precise control over texture and nutrition, and help for people with specific dietary needs. Some people will genuinely like it. Taste is personal.

Recent findings suggest that the printer isn't the villain. The villain is skipping the taste test because it looks right.

Industry observers note that same with AI-generated code. It shines for prototypes, spikes, internal tools with one user who is also the author, throwaway scripts, and clickable demos that help a stakeholder say "no, not that" in week one instead of month six. With real engineering behind it, it can absolutely become dinner.

Industry observers note that the mistake isn't printing the steak. The mistake is serving it without tasting it.

The Taste Test Before you serve anything that came out of a prompt, take a bite.

If it fails, also congratulations. You found out in the kitchen instead of the dining room.

Recent findings suggest that takeaway AI coding tools are incredible at producing things that look done. Looking done and being done have always been different. We used to have a lot of friction that forced us to notice the gap. Now the gap arrives vacuum-sealed, with dark mode.

So go ahead. Buy the printed steak. Try it. Maybe you'll love it. Just taste it before you serve it to guests.

Industry observers note that the ruling reverses the Virginia Court of Appeals’ judgment, which held that the circuit court properly sentenced Ginevan, albeit for different reasons. The circuit court determined that felons are not “the people” referenced in the Second Amendment. Justice Wesley Russell Jr. said the determination was error.

Sources indicate that “The fact that the phrase ‘right of the people’ means the same thing in the First, Fourth and Second Amendments is, by our reckoning, sufficient to resolve whether a felony conviction, without more, removes a person from the political community,” Russell said. “Because a felony conviction does not remove a person from ’the people’ referenced in the First and Fourth Amendments, we conclude that, as a matter of language and logic, it does not remove a person from ’the people’ referenced in the Second Amendment.".

It has been revealed that the Court of Appeals assumed Ginevan was included as part of “the people” but ruled Ginevan’s 2018 conviction for possessing ammunition is a violent felony under state law. The Court of Appeals then examined the nation’s historic traditions and observed that the United States has long barred those judged to pose a danger to the public from possessing firearms. The Supreme Court of Virginia, however, ruled no founding-era law permanently disarmed nonincarcerated people based on a felony conviction.

Industry observers note that “Statutes banning convicted felons from possessing firearms as a result of their prior felony convictions are a creature of 20th-century legislatures,” Russell noted. “The inability of the commonwealth to offer even one example from the relevant timeframe of a statute barring all felons or even violent felons from possessing even a class of firearms, let alone all firearms and ammunition, is a significant strike against the constitutionality of Code Â§ 18.2-308.2.”.

Shawn Weneta, director of government affairs at Cavalier Consulting, who himself regained his Second Amendment right in January, said the laws banning possession come from the Jim Crow era.

“Once we started saying it’s okay to strip people of their rights because of a criminal conviction, and then started, you know, criminalizing Black culture, we became very much desensitized,” Weneta stated in an interview.

Executive Director of Justice Forward Virginia Brad Haywood mentioned the felon-in-possession law disproportionately impacts Black people.

Recent findings suggest that “If you’re Black in America, you’re much more likely to have a felony conviction,” Haywood said during an interview. “So I think this has also just a disproportionate positive impact on them.”.

In affirming Ginevan’s conviction, the Court of Appeals observed English laws denying firearms to those who declined to join the Church of England or colonial laws disarming Native Americans. Russell ruled the laws aren’t analogous because they disarmed those viewed as disloyal to the state rather than punishing crimes.

Haywood said he doesn’t expect the ruling to impact the restoration of civil rights, like the right to hold public office or serve on a jury. Weneta said he’s interested to see how Virginia’s Democratic-led General Assembly approaches the ruling during the 2027 session. Weneta noted the ruling intersects the interests of criminal justice reform and Second Amendment advocates.

“I think there are quite a few very interesting questions now in front of advocates, you know, Second Amendment advocates, gun control advocates, very interesting questions in front of legislators, and of course a very interesting question in front of the attorney general,” Weneta mentioned.

Industry observers note that the Office of Attorney General Jay Jones did not respond to a request for comment. Attorney Elliot Harding stated he expects a flood of habeas corpus petitions from people presently serving time for felon-in-possession offenses, but he doubts the ruling will apply retroactively. The ruling is narrow because it applies only to Ginevan, but Harding said he would be interested in bringing a habeas petition for a similar client under the right circumstances.

“This isn’t a broad, sweeping decision that undermines felon-in-possession laws, but it does invite future defendants, particularly those with histories lacking a crime of violence, to raise Second Amendment arguments,” Harding remarked in an interview.

Recent findings suggest that the ruling doesn’t change the fact federal law prohibits felons from purchasing firearms. Attorneys representing Ginevan did not respond to a request for comment.

While history has repeatedly shown that the Dow, S&P 500, and Nasdaq Composite rally over multiple decades, it also tells us that bull markets don't last forever. Eventually, something is going to knock Wall Street's AI-driven bull market off its pedestal -- and we may be watching that bubble burst before our very eyes right now.

While everyone's focused on the potential formation and subsequent bursting of an AI bubble, there's a $1.5 trillion bubble that looks to be bursting in real time.

Even though past events can't concretely predict the future, history has a way of rhyming on Wall Street. In other words, historical precedent often sets the tone.

Over the last three decades, few, if any, past events, metrics, or indicators have accurately forecast the future quite like outstanding margin debt.

Margin represents the money an investor borrows, with interest, from their broker to short-sell (wager against) or purchase securities, such as stocks or exchange-traded funds (ETFs). When used to buy stocks or ETFs, margin acts as leverage -- and leverage can be risky.

Industry observers note that on the one hand, if a stock or ETF moves in the desired direction, margin can help an investor amplify their gains. But if the security in question moves in the opposite direction, it can quickly magnify losses, on top of owing the broker interest on what's borrowed. This dynamic is what makes outstanding margin debt a crude but incredibly useful risk-measuring metric.

Over multiple decades, we'd expect outstanding margin debt to rise in lockstep with the cumulative value of the U.S. stock market. But when margin debt rises in parabolic fashion over a short period, things typically break.

While the AI revolution is fueling risk-taking, we're witnessing possible evidence of a top. Over the previous four months, outstanding margin debt has fallen from $1.502 trillion to $1.448 trillion, as of October.

When investors lighten up their leverage following a parabolic increase, it's historically been the ultimate red flag for Wall Street and investors that a bubble is about to burst.

Though history doesn't always offer the rosiest of short-term outlooks, historical precedent can change drastically depending on investors' perspectives.

In one respect, corrections and bear markets are par for the course when putting your money to work in the stock market. Given that emotions often drive downturns, there isn't much that can be done from a monetary or fiscal policy perspective to stop these double-digit declines from cropping up.

But these downturns share a theme: they're historically short-lived.

Sources indicate that six months ago, the analysts at wealth management firm Bespoke Investment Group calculated the calendar-day length of every S&P 500 bull and bear market since the start of the Great Depression in September 1929. They uncovered a night-and-day difference between optimism and pessimism on Wall Street.

The current bull market that began on 10/12/22 is now the 9th longest in S&P 500 history, surpassing the 1,324-day bull that ended on 2/9/1966: https://t.co/4mGsS2t2ft.

Whereas the typical S&P 500 bear market has lasted just 286 calendar days (about 9.5 months) over the last 97 years, the average bull market has persisted for approximately 3.6 times as long (1,023 calendar days).

Furthermore, no bear market has taken longer than 630 calendar days to find its trough, according to Bespoke. Meanwhile, just over half (14 of 27) of the S&P 500 bull markets since September 1929 have lasted longer than 630 calendar days.

Sources indicate that there isn't a single predictive tool that can tell investors precisely when a stock market downturn will begin, how long it'll last, or how steep the decline will be. But history does conclusively show that these downturns have always represented buying opportunities for optimistic, long-term-minded investors. If the margin debt bubble does burst, opportunity will await.

What Happened to “Cuffing Season?”

In the early 2010s, the holiday season and “cuffing season” were basically synonymous. The dropping temperatures paired with the pressures of having to endure Thanksgiving dinner without a date seemed to usher in an urgency around dating. Cuffing season was the perfect time to find someone—even if it was just until the season was over.

Recent findings suggest that but something has shifted. Cuffing season is nowhere near as prevalent as it used to be. Has it become yet another trend that’s just run its course—or is the decreased popularity a sign of something much larger happening in dating culture? In a recent development, relationship therapist Dr. Natalie Harvey explains that cuffing season hasn’t necessarily disappeared, but it has become fragmented. Dating apps and dating fatigue have changed the landscape of dating—including when people do it. (Related: Breaking: Simple considerations for simple people build...)

Recent findings suggest that if you look at the numbers, people are still eager to couple up in the fall and winter. According to a survey by the Hily dating app, 79 percent of Gen Z and 65 percent of Millennial Americans want to find a partner to share the season with. But this doesn’t mean attitudes toward cuffing season are still positive. In the same survey, more than half of respondents said they believe people rush into relationships during cuffing season for the sake of comfort.

Recent findings suggest that “There is less urgency, because the scarcity is gone,” Dr. Harvey notes. “When you can meet someone in any month, winter loses its deadline power.”.

She explains that there are other things at play as well. Dating is expensive, requires a lot of energy, and—most importantly—women’s attitudes toward relationships have shifted tenfold.

It has been revealed that if we’re being totally transparent, cuffing season was essentially born out of a little bit of panic. People were so afraid to face fall and winter without a cuddle buddy that they decided to deem the season one of relationships. But views around dating have changed—especially with women in the dating pool.

Industry observers note that “A lot of women have full, established lives that a seasonal relationship would disrupt rather than improve,” Dr. Harvey claims. “The cold still makes companionship appealing—it just competes with more self knowledge than it used to.”.

Sources indicate that vogue declared that having a boyfriend is embarrassing and we’ve been talking about the death of dating apps for years now. There are plenty of factors that feed into this (a failing economy, the rise of social media, the birth of red pill), but women play a pretty huge role in this shift.

“They have done the math on settling, and it never adds up,” Dr Harvey claims.

It has been revealed that she explains that women have their own incomes, homes, friendships, and entire lives they’ve built from the ground up. Having a man isn’t as “appealing” as it used to be, mostly because there’s no longer the sense of panic that cuffing season was based on.

“For a growing number of women, a partner has to add to their life, not just fill a seat at the holidays.”.

Sure, cuffing season may not be as prevalent as it was before, but that doesn’t mean it has lost its charm entirely. Dr. Harvey explains that while the desire for partnership still exists, there are ways to use the season in a way that fueled by curiosity and reflection rather than hastiness.

“[Cuffing season] is still useful, but as a mirror, not a deadline,” she notes. “Cuffing season is a good checkpoint to ask yourself what you actually want this winter.”.

In a recent development, there’s nothing wrong with wanting to share the holidays with someone. Going to a pumpkin patch with a S.O., cuddling up with some hot chocolate and Christmas movies, having someone to kiss at midnight on New Year’s Eve—these are all fun and romantic things that are super fun with a date. But cuffing season should be about figuring out what you’re looking for in a partner—not just trying to find any “rando” to fill the void.

Sources indicate that “If you do want to find a partner, decide your standards now—while you are clear—and do not renegotiate them in December,” she claims. Don’t let cuffing season accelerate your search for a partner, either. There’s no point in partnering up for the holidays if you don’t really like who you’re dating.

Recent findings suggest that plus, dating has become so horrendous that partnering up just because there was a change in the season really feels like doing more harm than good. While the desire to partner up in the winter clearly still exists, there’s a general attitude that feels like people aren’t as pressed to follow through.

Recent findings suggest that cuffing season will always exist because people will always collectively want partnership. We’re just human. But the circumstances have changed significantly—and our requirements for who we date should adjust as well.

“Wanting love is healthy,” Dr. Harvey says. “Settling is just loneliness with a plus one.”.

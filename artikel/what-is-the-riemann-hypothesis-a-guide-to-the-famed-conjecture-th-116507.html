According to recent reports, mathematicians want to know how predictable the prime numbers are. The German scientist Carl Friedrich Gauss (1777–1855), who many consider to be the greatest mathematician in history, had noticed in the late 1700s (when he was a teenager) that if you pick any whole number, the odds that it’s a prime depend on the number’s size. Primes slowly become sparser as numbers become bigger. Gauss observed that their frequency seems to depend on (or, is inversely proportional to) the number of digits. Roughly speaking, among numbers with k digits, one in every k of them is a prime. (Related: Introducing the latest Microsoft IQ: Putting your organ...)

In Riemann’s time, a half-century after Gauss’s guess, there was still no rigorous proof of this. In a landmark paper in 1859, Riemann stated his belief that the late Gauss, who had been one of his mentors at the University of Göttingen, had been right.

But Riemann — who was a “daring” thinker, says maths historian Rossana Tazzioli at the University of Lille in France — went much further. He proposed that Gauss’s “one in every k” guess was a good statistical prediction, meaning that the frequency of the primes should not fluctuate away from it too much. In other words, Riemann made a guess about the margin of error of Gauss’s prediction.

According to recent reports, if true, this ‘Riemann hypothesis’ would make primes’ behaviour similar to that of a random process, such as a series of coin tosses. More precisely, he suggested that when you count the primes among the first n whole numbers, fluctuations would take a form that many scientists are familiar with: “The idea of the Riemann hypothesis is that the error term looks a lot like √n, which is what any random model will give you,” says Andrew Granville, a number theorist at the University of Montreal in Canada.

Industry observers note that in his paper, Riemann did much more than guess. He introduced tools that would constitute the basis for much of the work on number theory — the branch of mathematics that concerns integers, primes and divisibility — in the following centuries.

First, by developing new techniques in analysis — another word for advanced calculus — as he went along, Riemann constructed the ‘Riemann ζ function’ (ζ is the Greek letter ‘zeta’).

Riemann then showed that the function embodied an enormous amount of information about prime numbers. First, he proved that the solutions to the equation ζ (z) = 0 — where z is a point on a plane — encode the statistical distribution of prime numbers along the number line. To do so, Riemann employed a type of Fourier analysis, the type of maths that nowadays enables the compression of music into compact mp3 files by breaking sound waves down into sinusoidal components.

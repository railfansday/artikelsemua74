Recent findings suggest that the Trade Desk (TTD -8.10%) used to be one of the market's favorite advertising technology companies. It offered investors an attractive way to benefit from the shift toward digital advertising, particularly as more people watched television through streaming services.

It has been revealed that but the sentiment has changed. The stock has fallen sharply as investors question whether the manufacturer can maintain its growth, compete with technology giants, and protect its profits. (Related: Jensen Huang question gets reporter ejected from Trump-...)

The problem is not that digital advertising is going away. It is that The Trade Desk is struggling to turn the industry's growth into growth for its own business. (Related: “Giant superatoms” could finally solve quantum computin...)

I see three main reasons behind the market's pessimism.

The biggest concern is the company's slowing revenue. In the third quarter of 2026, The Trade Desk generated $736 million in revenue, down 0.5% from a year earlier. That is a sharp reversal from the 18% growth it recorded in the same quarter last year.

According to recent reports, the slowdown is not limited to one quarter. Revenue increased just 4% during the first nine months of 2026 compared with 20% growth over the same period in 2025. For a tech giant that investors once expected to grow rapidly, these numbers are difficult to ignore.

Industry observers note that to understand why this matters, consider what The Trade Desk actually does. It provides software that helps advertisers buy digital advertising across websites, apps, and streaming services. Instead of negotiating separately with hundreds of publishers, advertisers can use its platform to plan and manage campaigns in one place.

In a recent development, the opportunity should be substantial. More advertising dollars are moving online, and streaming services are creating new places for advertisers to reach consumers. Yet The Trade Desk is not capturing that opportunity as promptly as investors expected.

Industry observers note that management is investing in artificial intelligence (AI) and improving its advertising platform to help customers run more effective campaigns. Its expanded relationship with Netflix is another positive development.

But latest products and partnerships are only valuable if they increase advertising spending. Investors want to see customers putting more money through the platform, not simply that the organization is launching new technology or entering into partnerships.

The second concern is competition, particularly from Amazon, Google, and Meta.

The Trade Desk built its business around an independent platform that helps advertisers buy advertising across the open internet. Its value proposition is that advertisers can access different publishers and advertising inventory through one platform, rather than relying entirely on the advertising ecosystems of individual technology companies.

In a recent development, that independence remains valuable. But the competitive environment is changing.

Sources indicate that amazon has a particularly strong advantage. Its advertising business combines inventory with valuable shopping and purchase-intent data. Advertisers can reach consumers and measure whether campaigns lead to sales. Google and Meta also have extensive first-party data, large advertising ecosystems, and sophisticated targeting capabilities. These platforms offer something The Trade Desk cannot fully replicate: direct access to their own owners, data, and inventory.

There is also a customer relationship problem. The Trade Desk has faced tensions with major advertising agencies, including the European giant Publicis, after disagreements over its platform and commercial practices. The dispute has largely been resolved. Still, it demonstrates the bargaining power that large customers have over the organization.

It has been revealed that the risk is not necessarily that advertisers will abandon The Trade Desk overnight. Its independent position and broad relationships with publishers still serve an vital purpose. The bigger concern is that it may become harder to win incremental advertising budgets when competing platforms offer simpler, more integrated solutions.

It has been revealed that slower growth would be easier to tolerate if profits remained resilient. Unfortunately, that has not been the case.

According to recent reports, in the third quarter, adjusted earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization (EBITDA) fell 16% year over year to $266 million. Its adjusted EBITDA margin contracted from 43% to 36%. Operating expenses increased to $643 million, compared with $578 million a year earlier, despite lower revenue.

Sources indicate that this is a difficult combination for investors. The Trade Desk historically benefited from operating leverage: as revenue grew, the company could increase profits without raising costs at the same rate. That advantage is now under pressure.

In a recent development, management has responded by restructuring, reducing its workforce by approximately 15%. That may help restore margins. But cutting costs is not a substitute for revenue growth. If the firm has to shrink its workforce simply to protect profitability, investors may question whether its previous growth model remains intact.

According to recent reports, the Trade Desk is not necessarily a broken business. It still has a substantial cash position, strong customer retention, and exposure to the long-term growth of programmatic advertising.

But the challenges are real and substantial, and investors want to see proof that the company can turn around its operations.

Sources indicate that the next few quarters will be important. Revenue growth must recover, margins must stabilize, and existing advertisers must demonstrate willingness to spend more through the platform.

Until then, The Trade Desk likely remains a problem child for investors.

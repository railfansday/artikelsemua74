Why hiring neurodivergent talent isn’t enough, and what leaders should do instead

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Companies are increasingly recognizing the value of neurodivergent talent, but hiring is only part of the picture. Many neuroinclusion strategies still rely on employees to disclose their diagnoses and request workplace adjustments themselves, an approach that assumes they feel safe doing so. According to a 2026 survey by the nonprofit Understood.org, about 70% of neurodivergent employees in the U.S. worry that disclosing their neurodivergence could negatively affect them at work. When employees stay silent, employers may never learn which barriers need addressing.

Jonathan Mok, a Hong Kong-based neurodiversity advocate, speaker, and leadership trainer, has seen that gap from both sides. He was diagnosed with ADHD and autism at 37, after years of working in education in Hong Kong. Today, he trains corporate teams across Asia on neuroinclusion and coaches executives who have received late diagnoses. According to recent reports, after his diagnosis, a workplace medical committee recommended that Mok should not feel obliged to attend department social gatherings. When his boss invited the team to a New Year lunch and he raised his hand to join, colleagues kept telling him nobody was forcing him to go. They meant well, but a measure intended to protect him left him feeling excluded. “I wanted to go. My boss was paying for lunch,” Mok recalls. “But the message I kept hearing was that I didn’t really belong there.”. (Related: Japanese VTuber actually escapes GTA 6’s “impossible” 6...)

Despite receiving formal accommodations, Mok continued to face challenges at work, and he resigned in 2020. His experience reflects a wider pattern. In a 2025 report by Boston Consulting Group, supported by SG Enable, 62% of neurodivergent employees surveyed in Singapore said their neurodivergence had been misunderstood as a poor attitude. While 99% believed reasonable accommodations would help, only 33% said these were available at their workplace, and just 24% considered them adequate.

In a recent development, the findings point to a disconnect between recognizing neurodivergent talent and creating the conditions for it to thrive. Gartner predicted in 2023 that by 2027, a quarter of Fortune 500 companies would actively recruit neurodivergent talent to improve business performance. Recruitment, however, is only the first step. Inclusion also depends on whether employees feel they belong, can speak up without fear of judgment, and receive support that works in practice.

Recent findings suggest that mok believes that an emphasis on meritocracy can lead some employers to assume that anyone capable enough to be hired does not need to disclose anything. Employees, in turn, try to fit in until they no longer can. He adds that organizations often focus on deficits and rely heavily on medical guidance rather than on what individuals actually want or need. In his view, support should not depend on employees asking for it; it should be built into how the organization operates.

He also challenges the idea that organizations must be ready to hire neurodivergent employees before taking any steps toward inclusion. One cosmetics retailer found that quiet shopping hours did little to ease the sensory demands of a busy store. After consulting Mok, a colleague, and store staff, it introduced ribbon-marked baskets that let customers signal a preference for minimal interaction without explaining their needs. Because retail turnover is high, the accompanying staff training was kept simple enough to pass on to newly introduced employees.

Mok points to a neurodivergent internship program at the Hong Kong office of an international insurer as a more comprehensive example. Rather than treating recruitment as the endpoint, the company paired the program with manager training and structured onboarding, helping teams build the skills to support neurodivergent colleagues. It also works with a local nonprofit organization to source talent and provide training.

To help organizations turn good intentions into action, Mok uses a 4P framework covering People, Place, Policy, and Program. It is designed as an ongoing approach rather than a one-time training session. Under People, he encourages organizations to recognize inclusive managers, create safe channels for raising concerns, and highlight neurodivergent role models. Under Place, he recommends sensory-friendly workspaces and normalizing tools that allow people to work in ways that suit them.

Sources indicate that policy involves clear procedures around disclosure, workplace adjustments, and job expectations, so that employees and managers know what support is available. Many managers, Mok notes, only look for guidance after a problem arises and then have to dig through documents that can run 50 or 60 pages. The program focuses on long-term planning, consultation, and partnerships with community organizations, including neurodivergent individuals and their families, so that initiatives address real needs rather than assumptions.

For companies operating across Asia, Mok stresses the importance of understanding how culture, law, and local knowledge shape neurodiversity in each market. Even within the region, conditions such as autism and ADHD are recognized differently, as the contrast between Hong Kong and mainland China shows. Such differences can complicate standardized inclusion policies. He also believes that meaningful progress is possible for organizations of every size, not only multinational corporations.

Industry observers note that for Mok, the next stage of neuroinclusion means looking past whether organizations are willing to hire neurodivergent people and asking whether their cultures, policies, and leadership allow those employees to stay and succeed. “The next diversity metric should not be hires,” he claims. “It should be stays.” A seat at the Fresh Year lunch, offered without hesitation, may say more about an organization’s inclusion than any recruitment target.

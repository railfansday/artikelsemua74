Love them or hate them, low-cost airlines dominate the skies in some parts of the world. Nowhere is that more true than in Europe, home to Ryanair, the largest low-cost airline in the world (per 2025 passenger numbers).

Back in my late teens and early 20s, I was all for booking the cheapest ticket to a place I’d never heard of, just because I could.

According to recent reports, that remarked, I still do still love to find a bargain, and can spend hours researching the best value option even when booking the simplest of itineraries. The buck stops, however, at Ryanair. My last flight was in 2023, and despite nothing glaringly awful happening, I vowed it would be the last.

Industry observers note that part of the low-cost airline business model is choosing airports far from the cities they serve, since landing fees are often cheaper. One of the best examples of this is Frankfurt Hahn (HHN), located about 80 miles from Frankfurt am Main — the city the airport’s named after. In a recent development, after a quick check on the Uber app, a one-way Uber ride would likely cost around $180. A more wallet-friendly alternative would be this €24 bus. The journey time would likely be longer than the flight itself…. (Related: My Journey to a serverless transformers pipeline on Goo...)

Even Ryanair’s largest base at London Stansted (STN) is around 40 miles from London, and requires a relatively expensive train or lengthy bus journey to the city.

Recent findings suggest that this is the part I hate the most about Ryanair. They are so stingy that they built their planes with built-in steps at the front boarding door so they don’t have to pay for air bridges. Whether it’s a sizzling 110+ degrees in the middle of the day in Seville, or deep in the minuses at Rovaniemi in Finland (the gateway to Lapland and Ryanair’s northernmost airport), you’ll have to brace the elements while boarding and deplaning.

Industry observers note that getting a deal on a flight departing at daft o’clock in the morning isn’t limited to just Ryanair or low-cost airlines. Who would ever want to set an alarm before 6 a.m.? However, Ryanair can charge such a premium to fly at a respectable time of day, that its fares end up almost the same as flag carriers.

Industry observers note that the screenshot below is a great example. British Airways’ one-way fares of 88 pounds from Heathrow (LHR) are over twice as much as two of Ryanair’s options from Stansted to Berlin.

However, Ryanair charges £74 for its midday departure (without a cabin bag). Compare that to BA’s 1:20 p.m. departure for £88 including a cabin bag; it’s a wonder why anyone would even consider Ryanair’s option if they needed to fly in the middle of the day.

Industry observers note that ultimately, airline preference is very subjective, and I know there are some die-hard Ryanair fans who won’t hear a bad word said about the airline. In its defense, if you’re heading somewhere off the beaten track, you can usually rely on Ryanair to get you as close as possible.

Personally, I’d rather enjoy status benefits (and earn miles and status-earning points) by flying a legacy carrier like Air France-KLM, British Airways, or Lufthansa to the closest airport, then figure out ground transportation the rest of the way.

Why more Americans are choosing to pay over time

🌐 Source: Fortune • 📅 23 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 4 min read (811 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

  • The common thread behind both habits is timing.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Paying with a credit card for lunch is routine today. It wasn’t always so. In a 1993 television report covering Burger King’s trial acceptance of credit cards, one customer objected, saying, “I think it’s pretty bad if you have to use a credit card when you go to a fast food restaurant. For something as little as $3.10.” Her objection assumed using credit for a small purchase meant you could not afford it. Newer payment tools face that same assumption today, even though the ways people manage everyday spending have changed.

Today we see a major shift in how people across income levels access wages they have already earned and use deferred-payment options instead of traditional financing like credit cards. Block recently surveyed deferred-payment people, finding that 49% used cash advances or early wage access in the past year, alongside growing adoption of buy now, pay later. Survey findings also show about two in five Afterpay Pay in 4 and Pay Monthly users reported household incomes of $100,000 or more, underscoring that these tools reach across income brackets rather than serving only people without other options. The common thread behind both habits is timing. People often have money on the way that has not hit their account yet, and they use these tools to line up an expense with an incoming paycheck. By our estimate, U.S. workers at any given time have earned about $260 billion in wages they have not yet been paid. Among surveyed deferred-payment owners, 65% financed an expense at least once in the past year because money was coming from a paycheck, refund, or another source, but had not arrived yet. That timing helps explain why many consumers are moving away from revolving card balances. Federal Reserve survey data shows the same share of credit cardholders who carried a balance at some point in the year fell from 57% to 45% over the past decade. Over a shorter span, adult use of buy now, pay later rose from 10% in 2021 to 16% in 2025, according to the Federal Reserve. When carrying a card balance is costly, a proposal with a defined cost and payoff date can become more attractive.

When we ask customers what they want from a payment initiative, predictability wins. In our study, 57% preferred a fixed payment schedule with the same amount, the same date and a clear endpoint over payment flexibility. One customer in our research put it plainly, saying, “We could probably buy this upfront, but then we’d be talking about a cushion of 200, 300 dollars at the end of October, which is probably cutting it too close.” Spreading payments lets households protect a cash buffer instead of draining their account at once.

According to recent reports, in many situations, these products provide consumers a lifeline, but at other times, they simply provide choice. In the same study, 53% of respondents’ most recent financed transactions were necessity-driven, where available funds could not cover the expense when needed, while 47% were ones the customer could have paid upfront. Those numbers describe different situations, not different types of people. The same customer can use a payment blueprint out of necessity one month and to preserve savings the next.

Importantly, these consumers are building a habit of repayment, but traditional credit files miss so much of this picture. Legacy scoring models look backward at reported debt, treating a thin borrowing history as a lack of creditworthiness even when someone earns steady income and pays bills on time. Our underwriting models consider current cash flow, historical cash flow trends, and a customer’s debt history. By weighing the frequency and stability of incoming deposits against recurring commitments, we can spot repayment capacity a static credit report overlooks. That ongoing view also lets us reduce a limit when incoming cash slows, keeping commitments aligned with what a customer can handle.

Sources indicate that for credit to fit how money actually moves, regulation has to keep pace with how these products work. Policymakers should support the responsible use of cash-flow data in underwriting so capable borrowers are not shut out for lacking a traditional credit file. Regulators should also judge financial products by what they actually do, including loan size, duration, cost and repayment structure, rather than forcing them into roles built for older categories. Clear disclosures should state the total dollar cost, payment due dates, when repayment ends and what happens if a payment is missed so every customer knows the full commitment before they agree.

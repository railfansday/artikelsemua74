The Amazon rainforest is a cornerstone of regional and global carbon cycling1 and is widely regarded and modelled as a perennially evergreen ecosystem2,3. This prevailing view, however, masks substantial fine-scale variation in leaf phenology, limiting our capacity to predict ecosystem responses to climate change. Here by combining 10-m Sentinel-2 observations with machine learning, we uncover pervasive, spatially structured seasonal deciduousness across the basin. In 70.0% of the Amazon, leaf shedding precedes leaf flushing by at least 3 months, creating pronounced asynchrony between deciduousness and greenness seasonality, whereas the remainder highlights near-synchronous phenology. Hydroclimate emerges as the dominant factor associated with this asynchrony across 51.6% of the area, followed by soil properties (33.2%) and light availability (15.2%). Motivated by this widespread decoupling between deciduousness and canopy greenness, we incorporated deciduousness-constrained leaf turnover and the resulting leaf-age demography into ecophysiological models, yielding spatially widespread improvements in the seasonal agreement between simulated gross primary productivity and satellite-derived photosynthetic seasonality across 79.9% of the basin. These findings challenge the conventional view of Amazonian phenology, demonstrating that previously overlooked deciduousness influences Amazonian carbon dynamics. Incorporating these insights into process-based models is essential for refining projections of climate–forest feedbacks in the Amazon and for understanding its ecological functions under accelerating global change.

The Amazon rainforest accounts for about 16% of global terrestrial carbon fixation through photosynthesis1, hosts nearly a third of known terrestrial biodiversity4 and is a critical yet vulnerable component of the climate system of Earth5,6. Its role as a carbon sink, driven by ecophysiological processes such as photosynthesis, is increasingly threatened by climate change7,8, yet the impacts of climate on these processes remain uncertain, particularly related to tropical leaf phenology9. Leaf phenology, the rhythm of leaf shedding and flushing, substantially influences carbon cycling and other ecological processes10,11. Because photosynthetic capacity develops after leaf expansion, typically peaks in mature leaves and declines with leaf ageing12,13, shifts in phenological synchrony between leaf shedding and flushing could substantially change leaf-age composition, influencing the carbon balance of the Amazon11,14. According to recent reports, the warm, humid climate and extraordinary tree diversity in the Amazon support diverse phenological strategies across species and landscapes15,16,17 (Fig. 1). Field and satellite studies show that these strategies generate widespread and regionally variable seasonality in canopy greenness18, leaf demography11,19 and photosynthetic activity20,21. Yet, variation in these strategies remains poorly resolved at the basin scale because commonly used satellite products, such as those derived from the Moderate-Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS; greater than or equal to 500 m), are vulnerable to subpixel cloud contamination18 and average over crowns with different phenological states, blurring fine-scale phenological dynamics22. Field measurements11,16, proximate remote sensing15,17,23 and high-resolution satellite imagery24 instead show local mosaics of deciduous and evergreen phenological strategies (Fig. 1a,b). Deciduous species seasonally shed and regrow leaves16,25, producing marked fluctuations in leafless-canopy fraction (deciduousness), whereas evergreen species show smaller changes in foliage cover but can exhibit seasonal variation in photosynthetic activity through near-synchronous leaf turnover11,26. This functional diversity creates spatiotemporal heterogeneity in canopy status, as species follow distinct phenological trajectories. In particular, temporal lags between leaf shedding and flushing (increasing greenness) cause asynchrony, further increasing heterogeneity in canopy status over time (Fig. 1b). This phenological asynchrony shapes ecosystem carbon, water and nutrient cycles in the Amazon19,27, yet its basin-wide distribution remains poorly understood. In a recent development, the drivers of Amazonian phenological asynchrony are also poorly understood (Fig. 1c), with seasonal differences in leaf shedding and flushing traditionally attributed to distinct climatic niches. Leaf shedding peaks under seasonal water limitation25, whereas flushing peaks with dry-season solar radiation in less water-restricted regions29,30. However, across much of the Amazon, high solar radiation and low rainfall are seasonally coupled31, complicating efforts to disentangle the roles of light and water in phenology. Soil texture32, nutrient availability33 and herbivory pressure34 may also modulate these dynamics by shaping leaf traits and leafing strategies (for example, deciduous versus evergreen). For instance, sandy soils may amplify water stress, favouring deciduousness, whereas nutrient-poor soils favour longer leaf lifespans35. The incomplete mechanistic understanding of phenological asynchrony hinders predictions of how climate change will alter phenological patterns and their cascading impacts on carbon cycling.

To bridge these gaps, we use 10-m Sentinel-2 observations (2019–2021) to map monthly deciduousness across the Amazon. We validate these maps against independent ground and high-resolution satellite observations (Extended Data Figs. 1–3) and quantify asynchrony between deciduousness and canopy greenness represented by the MODIS enhanced vegetation index (EVI)36. We then assess the environmental and biotic factors associated with this asynchrony. Finally, we incorporate deciduousness-constrained leaf turnover and the resulting leaf-age demography into photosynthetic models and evaluate the consequences for gross primary productivity (GPP) seasonality using independent solar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF) and eddy-covariance (EC) observations (Methods).

Our Sentinel-2 mapping uncovers strong spatial heterogeneity in Amazon forest canopy phenology (Fig. 2a). Using a 10% amplitude threshold in leafless-canopy fraction to indicate moderate seasonality, we estimate that 75.4% of the basin exhibits seasonal deciduousness (Fig. 2a), whereas 32.8% exceeds a stricter 20% threshold (sensitivity to alternative thresholds is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1). When we repeated the same analysis after aggregating Sentinel-2 reflectance data to 500 m, these fractions declined to 54.5% and 24.5%, respectively, corresponding to relative reductions of about 28% and about 25% (Supplementary Fig. 2). This scale dependence indicates that fine spatial resolution is critical for detecting canopy deciduousness, because crown-scale differences in leaf state and phenological timing are progressively averaged in coarser pixels. The largest amplitudes occur in the Southern Amazon (particularly the southwest and central-south) and in the Guiana Shield (northeastern Amazonia), contrasting with little-to-modest variation in the Everwet Amazon (including the wetter western and northwestern basin, where monthly precipitation remains greater than or equal to 100 mm year-round37) (Fig. 2a). These spatial patterns differ markedly from existing satellite-based representations of Amazon canopy structure and seasonality, including MODIS evergreen forest cover38, MODIS vegetation continuous fields tree cover39, MODIS EVI greenness amplitude and microwave vegetation optical depth (VOD) amplitude from the Vegetation Optical Depth Climate Archive (VODCA)40 (Methods; Extended Data Fig. 4). Although deciduousness amplitude is negatively correlated with MODIS evergreen forest cover (r = −0.38) and tree cover (r = −0.27), substantial amplitudes still occur in areas classified as evergreen and in forests with more than 80% tree cover (Extended Data Fig. 4a–d), indicating that structurally closed-canopy forests can nonetheless exhibit moderate-to-strong seasonal deciduousness. Deciduousness amplitude is positively correlated with EVI amplitude (r = 0.40; Extended Data Fig. 4e,f) and VOD amplitude (r = 0.37; Extended Data Fig. 4g,h), but these products capture different aspects of canopy dynamics. EVI integrates variation in leaf area, leaf age, canopy structure and optical properties15,26, whereas VOD is sensitive to vegetation water content and biomass40,41. Consequently, neither metric uniquely indicates seasonal changes in leafless-canopy fraction, because EVI or VOD may vary as a result of leaf ageing or canopy water dynamics even without substantial leaf shedding. Their divergence from our product therefore reflects the fact that conventional optical- and microwave-based indices capture composite canopy signals. By contrast, our Sentinel-2 product provides a more direct proxy of seasonal deciduousness across Amazon forests.

To examine the temporal relationship between seasonal deciduousness and canopy greenness, we quantified their asynchrony as the time lag (Δt) between the two metrics. Although deciduousness amplitude and Δt were strongly related at the bin-mean level (r2 = 0.96; Supplementary Fig. 3), they were not redundant. Substantial within-bin variation in Δt indicates that similar deciduousness amplitudes can occur under different degrees of temporal coupling between canopy leaf loss and greening. We therefore use Δt as a timing-based metric that complements deciduousness amplitude, which quantifies the magnitude of seasonal change in leafless-canopy fraction. Across the basin, Δt ranges from 0 months to 6 months (Fig. 2b), showing substantial spatial variation in the degree of coupling between leaf shedding and canopy greening. In 70.0% of the Amazon, Δt ≥ 3 months, indicating that peak deciduousness precedes peak greenness by at least 3 months and reflecting pronounced phenological asynchrony between deciduousness and greenness. The remaining area exhibits near-synchronous phenology, with lags of 0–2 months (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 4a). Spatially, strong asynchrony (Δt ≥ 3 months) is most prevalent in the Southern Amazon (78.9% of the area), followed by the Guiana Shield (63.5%) and the Everwet Amazon (54.0%) (Supplementary Fig. 4b–d). By contrast, low asynchrony is concentrated mainly in the everwet, weakly seasonal parts of the basin, where both deciduousness and greenness show muted seasonality. Site-level observations further illustrate this diversity. At the Amazon Tall Tower Observatory (ATTO) in central Amazonia (mean annual precipitation (MAP) = 2,628 mm yr−1), increased deciduousness coincides with enhanced greenness (Fig. 2c), indicating rapid leaf turnover with minimal delay. Conversely, at Reserva Jaru (RJA) in southern Amazonia (MAP = 1,990 mm yr−1), pronounced dry-season leaf shedding is followed by delayed canopy greening later in the season (Fig. 2d), indicating a more asynchronous phenological regime. At the basin scale, these spatial patterns were robust to the choice of greenness metric and lag definition, with similar results obtained using the kernel normalized difference vegetation index (kNDVI)42 and an alternative peak-to-peak lag defined as the interval between the months of maximum deciduousness and maximum greenness (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6).

According to recent reports, these patterns show a complex mosaic of varied phenological strategies across the Amazon, characterized by the coexistence of near-synchronous and asynchronous regimes. Such diversity highlights the limitations of relying on a single indicator (for example, either deciduousness or greenness) for phenological monitoring in the Amazon and calls for a multimetric framework to capture the full range of phenological behaviours across tropical rainforest landscapes15,16,17. Our findings also align with emerging LiDAR evidence of divergent leaf shedding strategies across the landscape2, underscoring the need for high-resolution, basin-scale characterization to refine global vegetation models.

The striking variability in the degree of asynchrony between deciduousness and greenness across the Amazon (Fig. 2b) prompts us to investigate its underlying mechanisms. We tested four hypotheses (Fig. 1c) focused on: (H1) hydroclimatic constraints (maximum cumulative water deficit (MCWD), MAP and soil moisture)43; (H2) soil property constraints (soil fertility, clay and sand)35; (H3) light optimization (surface downwelling shortwave radiation flux (SR))11,29; and (H4) herbivory pressure34,44. Using an XGBoost model45 with Shapley additive explanations (SHAP)-based interpretation46 (Methods), we find these factors collectively yield a squared correlation coefficient (r²) of 0.67 between predicted and observed asynchrony (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 7). On the basis of normalized SHAP importance, hydroclimatic predictors contribute most strongly (48.4%), followed by soil properties (30.0%) and light (14.4%), whereas herbivory has the least influence (7.2%). SHAP dependence plots show how each predictor contributes to the modelled asynchrony (Extended Data Fig. 5). As an extra sensitivity test, including MODIS-derived land-surface temperature (LST) left the r² unchanged at 0.67 and largely preserved the ranking of the leading predictors, with LST ranking below MCWD, soil fertility and solar radiation (Supplementary Fig. 8). The unchanged predictive performance probably reflects overlap between LST and the hydroclimatic and radiation conditions already represented in the model, supporting the robustness of the broader environmental attribution. Herbivory has long been hypothesized to be a principal driver of tropical phenology34 and its limited contribution here probably reflects the coarse spatial resolution of available proxy data47. Incorporating finer-scale, field-based herbivory observations would help quantify its role across both spatial and temporal scales. Moreover, the hypothesis that herbivory pressure is reduced during the dry season, when light availability and cumulative water deficit are elevated, may still hold, but our spatial analysis may not fully capture this effect. Given the limited contribution of the herbivory proxy, we therefore focus below on hypotheses H1–H3 (Fig. 3a).

It has been revealed that focusing on the top three predictors (MCWD, soil fertility and SR), we further identify complex controls on phenological asynchrony across the Amazon (Fig. 3b–d): H1, H2 and H3. For hydroclimatic constraints (H1), severe water stress promotes high asynchrony, with Δt increasing strongly and linearly with reduced MCWD (Fig. 3b). This aligns with H1 predictions43,48 indicating that prolonged seasonal water deficits promote drought-avoidance through leaf shedding while delaying canopy flushing until water conditions improve. For soil property constraints (H2), below approximately 0.32 cmol(+) kg−1 (corresponding to 10−0.5 on the log-scale axis in Fig. 3c), soil fertility showed limited influence on phenological asynchrony, with SHAP values remaining close to zero. At higher fertility levels, increasing SHAP values indicate a positive association between soil fertility and asynchrony. This pattern is consistent with nutrient-poor soils supporting more evergreen species with longer leaf lifespans, probably because leaf construction costs are high under nutrient limitation33,43. By contrast, fertile soils can support species with shorter leaf lifespans49, allowing leaf shedding during unfavourable environmental conditions and subsequent canopy flushing when conditions improve, thereby contributing to greater temporal separation between the two. This pattern is particularly pronounced in the fertile southwestern Amazon, supporting H2 (Fig. 3e). For light optimization (H3), under low light conditions, SR strongly suppresses asynchrony (reflected by strongly negative SHAP values), but this suppression diminishes linearly with increasing SR and eventually plateaus at high SR (Fig. 3d). In aseasonal regions such as the Everwet Amazon, persistently low and weakly seasonal light availability may favour tightly coupled leaf loss and replacement, as a transient reduction in leaf area would further constrain canopy carbon gain under limited irradiance16,50. By contrast, higher SR may relax this light constraint, allowing leaf replacement to become more temporally separated from leaf loss and thereby contributing to greater deciduousness–greenness asynchrony.

Our subsequent modelling of how hydroclimate, soil properties and light availability interact to shape phenological asynchrony across the Amazon shows that hydroclimate is the dominant driver, influencing 51.6% of the basin area, followed by soil properties (33.2%) and light availability (15.2%) (Fig. 3e). The relative importance of these factors varies regionally: hydroclimate drives phenological asynchrony in 52.8% of the Southern Amazon, 48.6% of the Guiana Shield and 51.1% of the Everwet Amazon, with soil properties acting as the secondary driver in each region (34.1%, 32.2% and 31.9%, respectively) (Extended Data Fig. 6). These Δt-based driver rankings are consistent across low-, intermediate- and high-deciduousness-amplitude classes, with MCWD remaining the top predictor in all classes (Supplementary Fig. 9a–d). A complementary analysis using deciduousness amplitude as the response variable identified MCWD, MAP and soil fertility as the main predictors of seasonal leafless-canopy magnitude (Supplementary Fig. 9e–h), supporting the interpretation that deciduousness amplitude and Δt capture connected but non-redundant dimensions of Amazon canopy phenology. Notably, light ranks third in spatial dominance, contrasting with earlier studies that emphasized light as the primary control of Amazonian (leaf flushing) phenology27,29,31. We propose that light mainly optimizes leaf-age turnover to enhance photosynthetic capacity, whereas water availability governs the timing of leaf shedding, together shaping phenological asynchrony11,51. This reframes the prevailing paradigm of Amazonian phenology from a traditionally light-driven system29,30 to a water-constrained framework, with nutrient and light optimization modulating responses.

In a recent development, to test whether a more realistic representation of canopy phenology improves simulations of Amazon forest photosynthesis, we incorporated satellite-derived seasonal deciduousness and its constraints on canopy leaf-age composition into three established light-use efficiency (LUE) models (Methods). This framework allows us to separate the effects of canopy structure from those of leaf-age regulation by comparing three configurations driven by identical climate forcing: (1) a conventional baseline based on greenness or leaf area index (LAI), (2) a structurally harmonized baseline using deciduousness-constrained canopy input but seasonally fixed photosynthetic capacity and (3) a full leaf-demography-driven framework (LD-LUE), in which canopy photosynthetic capacity varies seasonally with leaf-age composition following the established leaf-age regulation scheme11. Comparisons between LD-LUE and the conventional baseline quantify the combined effect of representing seasonal deciduousness and leaf demography, whereas comparisons between LD-LUE and the structurally harmonized baseline isolate the physiological contribution of leaf-age regulation beyond canopy structure alone. Model performance was evaluated against EC GPP at four contrasting Amazon forest sites and against basin-wide SIF, represented here by the mean of two satellite-derived products (GOSIF52 and CSIF53) as an independent benchmark of seasonal photosynthetic dynamics20.

Explicitly representing seasonal deciduousness and leaf demography substantially improves model performance. Across the basin, LD-LUE improves the seasonal correspondence between modelled GPP and SIF in 79.9% of Amazon forests relative to conventional LUE models (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 11). The magnitude of improvement varied spatially. The basin-wide distribution of Δr, defined as the difference in correlation with SIF between the LD-LUE ensemble and the conventional LUE ensemble, had a median of 0.16 (interquartile range, 0.031–0.37), with the largest gains concentrated in specific regions (Fig. 4a). The spatial pattern is consistent across the two SIF products (GOSIF and CSIF; Supplementary Fig. 10) and across all three LUE formulations (Extended Data Fig. 7), indicating that the improvement is robust to both the choice of SIF benchmark and model structure. Site-level EC validation further supports these results (Fig. 4b–g). EC observations and model simulations are compared in absolute GPP units (gC m−2 d−1), allowing both seasonal phase and amplitude to be evaluated. Because EC records span earlier multiyear tower observation periods and do not fully overlap with the 2019–2021 Sentinel-2 mapping window (Supplementary Table 1), we evaluated the climatological mean seasonal cycle rather than year-specific interannual variability. In humid central and eastern forests, LD-LUE more accurately captures both the phase and magnitude of seasonal GPP variation than do the conventional greenness- or LAI-based formulations (Fig. 4b–d and Extended Data Fig. 8), consistent with the idea that seasonal productivity in these forests is governed less by simple changes in canopy quantity than by coordinated leaf flushing, leaf replacement and age-dependent shifts in photosynthetic capacity11,54. By contrast, improvements relative to the conventional baseline are smaller at the drier southern site in RJA (Fig. 4e), where traditional greenness or LAI metrics already reproduce much of the observed GPP seasonality, in agreement with previous findings9.

In a recent development, the structurally harmonized comparison further shows that the performance improvements do not arise from canopy structure alone. When both simulations are forced with the same deciduousness-constrained canopy input, the LD-LUE framework still improves the seasonal correspondence between modelled GPP and SIF relative to the fixed-capacity structural baseline across all three LUE formulations (Extended Data Fig. 9). Because canopy structure is identical in this comparison, the remaining improvement can be attributed to seasonal variation in canopy photosynthetic capacity associated with leaf-age composition. Relative to the broader gains obtained against conventional LUE models (Fig. 4), this physiological contribution is more spatially heterogeneous. The magnitude and spatial prevalence of improvement indicated distinct regional patterns. Across much of the central and western Amazon, improvements become weaker, whereas the largest gains in Δr persist in the northeastern Amazon, suggesting a stronger added role for leaf-age-dependent photosynthetic regulation there. By contrast, positive gains are most spatially extensive in the more seasonal Southern Amazon, where the fraction of pixels with improved seasonal agreement (mean Δr > 0 across three formulations) reached 79.6%, but was much lower in the northwestern Everwet Amazon (35.7%; Extended Data Fig. 9e). The weaker improvement in the northwest probably reflects low phenological seasonality16,55, which may increase uncertainty in detecting deciduousness-constrained leaf demography. At the site level, LD-LUE also better reproduces the seasonal amplitude of GPP than the structural baseline, particularly at three humid forest sites (Extended Data Fig. 10), indicating that leaf demography improves simulation of the magnitude of photosynthetic variation, not only its timing11.

Recent findings suggest that these differences are also consequential for regional carbon accounting. Relative to conventional LUE baselines, LD-LUE changes basin-integrated annual GPP by about 1.42 PgC yr−1 (Supplementary Fig. 11), equivalent to a 9.4% difference from conventional estimates and approximately 10–20% of current global annual anthropogenic CO2 emissions when converted to carbon units56. These results show that incorporating satellite-constrained deciduousness and leaf demography systematically shifts not only the seasonal distribution but also the magnitude of simulated photosynthesis. Together, these findings highlight the importance of explicitly representing canopy phenology and leaf turnover in tropical carbon-cycle models to improve projections of climate–carbon feedbacks.

In a recent development, our study reshapes the understanding of Amazon rainforest dynamics by showing that seasonal deciduousness is both widespread and spatially structured across the basin. We demonstrate that deciduousness and greenness cycles are often asynchronous, and that this asynchrony, shaped by hydroclimate, light and soil nutrient availability, substantially modulates Amazon carbon-cycle dynamics by changing both the seasonal distribution and magnitude of simulated photosynthesis. Incorporating seasonal deciduousness and associated leaf demography into LUE modelling improves the representation of tropical leaf phenology and seasonal GPP estimates, highlighting the importance of explicitly incorporating canopy phenology into process-based models used to project Amazon responses to climate change.

In a recent development, these findings carry three implications for tropical ecology and global change research. First, traditional vegetation monitoring overlooks key phenological variation in tropical forests, highlighting the need to evaluate similar dynamics in other forests presumed to be predominantly evergreen. Emerging monitoring frameworks should integrate multidimensional, high-resolution datasets to capture the full complexity of tropical forest phenology. Species ecophysiological responses manifested in these multidimensional phenological dynamics10,11 can provide early warning signs of ecosystem transition, such as shifts from evergreen- to deciduous-dominated ecosystems. Second, our mechanistic understanding of basin-wide phenological asynchrony provides a basis for predicting carbon–climate feedbacks. As climate change intensifies dry seasons7,8, elevated hydroclimatic stress may expand deciduous-dominated regimes, potentially reducing the carbon sink capacity of the Amazon and altering species composition. Third, current process-based models (including mainstream terrestrial biosphere models9) misrepresent Amazon rainforest phenology, leading to systematic biases in carbon flux simulations3,9. Because phenology underpins many important ecophysiological processes, our results highlight the need to explicitly incorporate deciduousness and its constraint on leaf demography into terrestrial biosphere models. Doing so will improve predictions of: (1) carbon sequestration under present and future climate conditions9,11, (2) evapotranspiration dynamics, which affect regional rainfall patterns10,57 and (3) ecosystem–microclimate interactions that are tightly linked to seasonal canopy changes2. Integrating these advances into next-generation models will refine projections of Amazon rainforest responses to climate change and strengthen our broader understanding of vegetation–climate feedbacks at the global scale.

To elucidate seasonal patterns of greenness and deciduousness in the Amazon rainforest, we integrated multisensor satellite observations. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. Experimental randomization and blinding were not applicable to this observational study based on existing datasets.

We assessed canopy greenness dynamics using the EVI derived from daily MODIS data. EVI was selected, as it reduces saturation effects in high-biomass forests compared with the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI)36, while demonstrating accuracy in tracking leaf flushing and canopy structural changes in the rainforest36,58,59. Previous studies have also demonstrated that EVI can capture dry-season canopy greening associated with upcoming leaf flushing in the Amazon15,23,26. We accessed daily MCD43A1 bidirectional reflectance distribution function (BRDF) model parameters at a 500-m resolution by means of Google Earth Engine (GEE). To minimize cloud and shadow contamination, we conducted stringent cloud filtering following the method described in ref. 24. We then used these BRDF parameters with the RossThick-LiSparse kernel model to reconstruct reflectance at a nadir view and a 45° solar zenith angle, thereby reducing view- and illumination-angle effects. This approach has been extensively validated for tropical forests58,60. Subsequently, BRDF-adjusted, quality-assured daily EVI values from 2019 to 2021 (matching the temporal range of Sentinel-2 data, detailed later) were aggregated into monthly composites. Remaining missing data were gap-filled following ref. 61 to reconstruct complete monthly EVI trajectories for subsequent seasonal analyses24,26. To evaluate the robustness of our results, we repeated the primary greenness-based analyses using kNDVI (Supplementary Fig. 5) and an EVI dataset derived from the MODIS Multi-Angle Implementation of Atmospheric Correction (MAIAC) MCD19A3CMG V061 product62 (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). Both produced closely comparable seasonal patterns.

This period was selected because systematic global production of Sentinel-2 Level-2A surface-reflectance data became available only in late 2018 (ref. 63) and because it avoids the potentially confounding effects of the severe 2023–2024 El Niño-related Amazon drought64. We used the 10-m visible and near-infrared bands, including blue (B2), green (B3), red (B4) and near-infrared (B8), because these bands have demonstrated effectiveness in differentiating leafless-canopy status from other phenophases26 and the 10-m spatial resolution also provides an appropriate scale for both fine- and ecosystem-scale analyses22,28. Cloud and shadow contamination was minimized using a two-step quality-control procedure. First, pixels with cloud probability values greater than 50% were masked using the Sentinel-2 Cloud Probability product generated by the s2cloudless algorithm65. To improve spectral comparability among images acquired under different viewing and illumination geometries, we applied image-specific BRDF correction using coefficients derived from the MCD43A1 product, following ref. 67.

The model was implemented using TensorFlow v.2.20.0 in Python v.3.13.5. IG-ECAE is an automated, biophysically grounded spectral-unmixing framework developed for scalable mapping of tropical forest deciduousness22,28. Recent findings suggest that we compiled phenological observations from five tower-mounted phenocams across tropical American rainforests (Extended Data Fig. 1). At the basin scale, we used satellite SIF as an independent benchmark for the seasonal phase of photosynthetic activity. Two global 0.05° SIF products (GOSIF52 and CSIF53) were used to characterize basin-wide seasonal dynamics. Because SIF is not expressed in GPP units, we used SIF to evaluate seasonal phase rather than absolute carbon uptake. Before comparison with EC-derived GPP at the site level, GOSIF and CSIF were matched to the same year–month observations available in the corresponding EC record at each site (Supplementary Fig. 24). Because both SIF products performed well, we used their ensemble mean in the basin-scale analysis to reduce product-specific bias. For the basin-wide evaluation, pixel-level simulated GPP, driven by ERA5-Land meteorological forcing and satellite-derived canopy inputs, was compared with the corresponding SIF time series across Amazon forest pixels. Seasonal agreement was quantified as the temporal correlation between monthly modelled GPP and SIF at each pixel. We also calculated Δr, defined as the difference in the SIF correlation between model configurations, to identify where deciduousness-constrained canopy structure and leaf demographic dynamics improved or degraded seasonal agreement.

Recent findings suggest that comparisons were made at two levels. First, each LD-LUE configuration was compared with its conventional baseline, quantifying the combined effect of improved canopy structural forcing and explicit leaf demography. To ensure our findings were robust to the choice of observational benchmark, this primary comparison was evaluated separately against GOSIF and CSIF (Supplementary Fig. 10), as well as against their ensemble mean (Fig. 4 and Extended Data Figs. 7 and 8). Second, each LD-LUE configuration was compared with a harmonized-structure baseline, in which both simulations used the same deciduousness-constrained LAI forcing but differed in whether seasonal leaf-age dynamics were represented (Extended Data Figs. 9 and 10). This comparison isolated the incremental contribution of leaf demography. All comparisons were performed for each LUE formulation and for the multimodel ensemble mean (Extended Data Figs. 7–10).

To quantify the changes in basin-integrated GPP in absolute physical units, we compared annual GPP simulated by the LD-LUE framework ensemble with that simulated by the conventional LUE ensemble. For each pixel, we calculated the difference in annual GPP between the two modelling approaches while retaining the direction of change and then spatially integrated these differences across the Amazon basin and main subregions to estimate the net annual GPP change (ΔGPP; Supplementary Fig. 11). Pixel-level differences in GPP, originally expressed in physical units per unit area, were converted to area-integrated carbon fluxes using pixel area before spatial aggregation. Regional improvements in seasonal phase were summarized as the percentage of forested area exhibiting a positive performance gain (Δr > 0). To test robustness to observational uncertainty, we propagated deciduousness uncertainty through the GPP simulations using LD-EC-LUE as a representative case. The model was rerun at the four flux-tower sites with deciduousness inputs perturbed within the ±1 s.d. uncertainty bounds derived from the Monte Carlo analysis (section ‘Uncertainty quantification for deciduousness estimates’). The resulting spread in simulated GPP indicated that the inferred improvements were overall robust to uncertainty in the Sentinel-2-derived deciduousness input (Supplementary Fig. 25).

We further assessed uncertainty associated with the empirical conversion from leafy canopy fraction to LAI (equation (9)). Using the original 12 monthly paired observations from K67, we performed 10,000 residual-bootstrap realizations by resampling the fitted residuals with replacement and refitting the slope and intercept jointly in each realization. The resulting bootstrap ensemble was propagated through the four flux-tower simulations. For the basin-scale analysis, we selected ten representative paired slope–intercept combinations spanning the empirical joint 95% parameter space and repeated the LD-LUE simulations for each combination. We then quantified the resulting variability in annual GPP, basin-mean seasonal GPP and the spatial extent of positive seasonal-performance gains (Supplementary Figs. 26–28).

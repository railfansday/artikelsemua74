Is Oobah Butler a journalist or a satirist? The latest voice to step up in HBO’s many forays into news-like programming doesn’t seem to know himself. Perhaps he’s both: the 34-year-old host of the new five-part series Oobah: Scams for a Better Tomorrow has carved out his own niche in the loose structure that cable TV news has taken on of late. The bleach-blond Brit finds a space between black-pill cynicism, cutting satire and actual public service as he holds a mirror up to several industries and cultures, often by mimicking their own languages and modes to show, in the simplest ways, how warped, corrupted or morally bankrupt they have become — or always were. Industry observers note that “I think the problem is, whenever you try and pin yourself down as one, the other one feels intriguing,” Butler told The Hollywood Reporter in an interview this week. “I don’t know. I think at the heart of all of it is some sort of truth-seeking. And I’m trying to learn something through using satire, which I think there’s a massive tradition of in both of our countries.”. According to recent reports, satire may be too light a term for the lengths he goes to. In the series premiere, a deadpan, dead-serious Butler bamboozles the wellness industry, getting entirely too far into the fray as he pitches, focus-groups and activates a skin serum sold as the product of children’s tears. In a later episode, he explores the fine line between scamming and marketing by pitching an eager tech industry on a dating app where singles open up to purported AI agents who turn out to be the very owners they’ve matched with. And after that, he handily infiltrates Amazon’s rankings and charts with a terrible pop song to expose how hackable the attention economy has become in the TikTok/nepo baby era of late-stage capitalism. It has been revealed that with the series, which premiered on HBO Max on Sunday, Butler is being introduced to his widest American audience yet. While his aim and agenda are global in scope, they are very much rooted in America, his adopted home for over two years, and its many cultures. Still, it’s no stateside debut: As he notes, his résumé includes years writing for Vice Media, the youth media brand whose DIY, parachute style of journalism raised eyebrows in the same ways Oobah is about to. Butler’s work during the now once-again-fledgling brand‘s globally focused era no doubt earned him some American fans — his articles include “I Became a Fidget Spinner to Try to Understand Fidget Spinning” and “I Turned Bottles of Amazon Drivers’ Pee Into a #1 Bestseller” — and the show’s first-person style has the feel of an offshoot of the Emmy-winning Vice on HBO.

According to recent reports, in 2013, that show took Dennis Rodman to North Korea to meet Kim Jong Un for some basketball diplomacy. While Butler, more than a decade later, doesn’t quite top that bizarre feat in his initial run, he does carry the torch, using his charm, wit and an alarmingly natural fearlessness to finagle his way into the top echelons of U.S. industries and get his satirical products in front of some notable gatekeepers, including the New York Times wine critic and Theranos’ former chief creative officer. It’s a ruse, but it takes the kind of steely nerve that makes great journalism.

Recent findings suggest that in a sense, Butler has been riding the twists and turns of a vastly shifting industry into what’s become the perfect career for a modern journalist. He started at 15, carving out a freelance career writing for magazines instead of enrolling at one of the U.K.’s many fine universities. The gonzo style and large-scale efforts to disrupt systems he sees as rigged began early, with a prank website for his fake restaurant, The Shed at Dulwich (shaving cream and dishwasher fluid were pictured as menu items), which he used to game TripAdvisor until it became the No. 1 restaurant in London on the popular travel site. That surprising result rolled out a career defined by massive, flashy, newsmaking satire.

“All of a sudden, I had a kind of, like, encouragement to go and make my work about inauthenticity and manipulation and social engineering,” he told THR. “Well, the stuff that I’d always been interested in. And you have a success, and it gives you permission to be like, ‘OK, now here’s a version of my work that I can get really excited by.'”.

Recent findings suggest that A short film, How I Faked My Way to the Top of Paris Fashion Week, followed as Butler went along with Vice and the rest of the startup media world’s ill-fated pivot to video. But his schemes, style and sensibility broke through, and soon he leveled up to the major leagues: He was given the hosting job at MTV’s Catfish UK and in 2023 he produced a documentary for Channel 4; it was here, he says, that he cut his teeth, learning to produce documentaries under specific standards and practices.

“I’m a disruptor. I like to come up with stuff which is going to make news, make people talk,” Butler claims in voiceover in the series’ first episode. (Spoilers ahead!) Part of this is following his narratives down rabbit holes that lead to the truly unexpected. Cannibalism, for example, is the conclusion of that first episode, and it may leave viewers taken aback, to say the least. (“I didn’t realize I’d be a vegetarian and a cannibal, which is fun,” he gleefully told THR.) It all gets stranger by the time the major finale episode arrives — a lengthier effort that spanned the time the first four episodes took to produce — as Butler marries into royalty, nearly causes an international scandal (and even gets some new laws on the books) as he takes on what he believes is at the heart of British and American identity.

Recent findings suggest that with such heights of disruptive newsmaking reached in his first season, what can we possibly expect if the series is picked up for a second run? Like most journalists, Butler doesn’t really know what his next story will be. But he is certainly grounded in why he’ll be chasing it.

“I’m trying to entertain and to provide people with a language to make themselves feel less fucking insane,” he says. “I think we know the world feels rigged in a certain way, and I think it’s good to lay that out on the table. I’m constantly learning that you can’t trust the systems to protect you, which is a little, little sad. At the very base level, [I want to] give people a language for their frustrations.”.

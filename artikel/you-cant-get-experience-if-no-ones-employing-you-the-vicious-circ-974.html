‘You can’t get experience if no one’s employing you’: the vicious circle facing Blackpool’s Neets

The English seaside town tops the list for 16- to 24-year-olds out of work and education, but not for lack of young jobseekers’ efforts.

Speaking random words like “cat” and “box” into her phone while filming herself in her pyjamas, Amy Butterworth’s relentless search for a job had reached a latest level of absurdity. (Related: Hyperparameter Search with Transformers and Ray Tune...)

The 19-year-old from Blackpool was applying for a role at Marks & Spencer, but first had to prove she was not a robot.

After downloading an app, filming her face from different angles, speaking particular words, inputting numbers and sharing a picture of her passport, she eventually received an email offering interview slots. (Related: Breaking: How to generate text: using different decodin...)

Recent findings suggest that yet within two minutes of the message arriving, all the slots were gone. In the small print, the retailer admitted it could not promise an interview to everybody offered one. “I just felt kind of stupid,” Butterworth noted.

This is the demoralising reality for many young people in Blackpool, where more than one in five 16- to 24-year-olds are out of work and education, the highest proportion in England.

Alan Milburn, the former Labour minister, this week urged “full throttle” overhaul of policy across healthcare, education, benefits and employment regulation as Britain reckons with a growing crisis of nearly 1 million young people not in education, employment or training (Neet), a far higher rate than many other western European nations.

Recent findings suggest that eight of the 10 English local authorities with the highest Neet concentrations are in the north or the Midlands. Top of the list is Blackpool, where the town’s reputation as a pleasure resort sits uncomfortably with its deep-rooted challenges of poor health, housing and addiction.

Sources indicate that on Saturday, dozens of seasiders will put on their glad rags hoping for a ticket to the BBC’s Strictly Come Dancing, which is filmed in the Blackpool Tower Ballroom for one week each year. Yet the queues are unlikely to match the town’s annual jobs fair earlier this year, when more than 4,000 jobseekers lined the promenade.

One local official described the enormous turnout in February as a “sad indictment” of the British economy. Of the 2,000 jobs on offer, 1,200 were given out on the spot.

The huge demand for work makes life more difficult for teenagers such as Butterworth, who say they are constantly told by employers they need more experience but find few willing to take a chance on them.

Describing this vicious circle, she mentioned: “You need to get a job, but to have a job you need some experience. And you can’t get experience if no one’s employing you.”.

The lucky ones find seasonal work cleaning hotels or serving up chips to day-trippers. But the lack of year-round jobs means competition is extremely high, with some employers receiving 200 applications for every vacancy.

Companies have started using AI to sift through CVs, putting applicants at the mercy of algorithmic bias. Less scrupulous firms use “postcode screening”, effectively blocking applicants from some parts of Blackpool, according to a report by the Social Mobility Commission in September – a practice Chris Webb, the Labour MP for Blackpool South, described as “abhorrent”.

In a recent development, since finishing her art diploma at Blackpool and the Fylde College, Butterworth has lost count of the amount of job applications she has submitted. Despite volunteering at a local primary school, she has had no end of knockbacks.

“It really knocked my confidence a lot and I felt really disadvantaged, because nearly everyone I knew had got their first job because they knew someone,” she stated.

Butterworth is bright, articulate, hard-working and full of character, with a cuttingly dry sense of humour. But in school she struggled with concentration. Her autism was only diagnosed in February after a years-long wait.

The Covid pandemic, which struck when Butterworth was in her second year of secondary school, left her marginalised. Her mother had just finished cancer treatment and was classed as highly vulnerable, so teachers put Butterworth “behind a wall on a desk on my own”.

Sources indicate that “It was very isolating,” she remarked. “It made me very socially anxious, which definitely didn’t help when I was originally trying to find a job.”.

Industry observers note that blackpool has one of England’s highest school exclusion and absence rates, and one of the lowest levels of attainment for GCSEs. Experts say both are a result of the challenging backgrounds of many of the town’s children, as well as mental or neurological conditions that often go unsupported.

In a recent development, last November, headteachers started adopting less punitive measures to try to keep more children in class. The number of pupils excluded or suspended has since fallen by 33% and 29% respectively, according to Blackpool council.

Local leaders are working to reverse the town’s position as England’s Neet capital. A brightly coloured youth skills hub called the Platform gives 16- to 24-year-olds personalised support with writing CVs, training, doing interviews and securing work or volunteering.

In a recent development, kath Benson, a Labour councillor who is the council executive’s member for young people, said: “There’s a massive perception about young people that they just want to stay in bed and play on their phones. I know that’s not the case. In Blackpool, they’re queueing up for jobs.”.

It has been revealed that the Platform was rolled out in 2022 with £5m of funding from the UK’s shared prosperity fund, which replaced EU money previously given to disadvantaged areas. Since then, it has supported more than 2,350 young people into learning, training or work, according to the council.

Industry observers note that butterworth was one of those it helped, finding her training to work behind a bar while she tries to establish a career as a tattooist. She pulled her first pint last month at Abingdon Street Market, a trendy food-and-booze hall, and is hoping to start an apprenticeship at a tattoo parlour where she showed off her artwork.

Things are now starting to look up, she said: “I just feel very grateful that I was in a situation for once where I actually know someone that can give me an ‘in’ somewhere.”.

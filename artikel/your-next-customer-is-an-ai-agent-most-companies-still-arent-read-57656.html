Your Next Customer Is an AI Agent. Most Companies Still Aren't Ready to Be Evaluated by One.

Your next customer may be an AI agent, one that evaluates 40 vendors overnight and books a human conversation only with the manufacturer that can prove its claims in a form a machine can verify. (Related: The Age of Machine Learning As Code Has Arrived...)

Most commercial organizations were never designed for that kind of buyer.

In a recent development, I've been thinking a lot about how AI is changing the way companies go to market. Most of the conversations I come across still circle familiar themes: AI-written sales emails, automated prospecting, smarter CRM systems, better seller productivity. All of that is useful. But I think it describes the small version of what is happening.

According to recent reports, the bigger shift begins when buyers start using AI agents not just to research products, but to shortlist vendors, compare solutions, test integrations and, eventually, negotiate within limits their company has approved. We have spent decades building sales and marketing organizations to influence human buying decisions. Very few of us have asked what changes when part of that decision is delegated to software. (Related: How to train a new language model from scratch using Tr...)

That question is why GTM engineering has become so interesting to me. It is no longer only about automating how we sell. It is about how an enterprise makes itself discoverable, understandable, trustworthy and easy to buy from, for people and for the intelligent systems acting on their behalf. And that, in my view, is as much a technology architecture challenge as a sales challenge.

In a recent development, let me make this concrete. This is a hypothetical scenario, not a case study.

A manufacturer's procurement agent detects a latest requirement. Overnight, it screens 40 potential vendors on specifications, security evidence, integration fit and price, and narrows the list to three. It then contacts each vendor's own systems.

Two of them return well-designed but generic capability pages, the kind of content we have all been producing for years. The third returns a signed capability manifest, verified certifications, a sandbox already configured with the buyer's anonymized data, and documented outcomes from similar deployments.

The agent books a 20-minute call with a human, and only with the third vendor. That vendor's account executive joins the call already knowing the three open technical questions and exactly which evidence the agent relied on.

Figure 1: Who won the deal? A procurement agent screens 40 vendors and books a human call only with the vendor whose claims it can verify.

Notice what did not happen. There was no cold call, no lead moving through a funnel, and no salesperson controlling the flow of information. The firm that won was not the one with the largest sales team or the most polished marketing. It was the one whose products, knowledge and interfaces were ready to be evaluated by another intelligent system.

Sources indicate that I'm not predicting that enterprise procurement will be fully autonomous by 2028. Human buying committees will still make the significant decisions. But I believe this is the direction of travel, and it exposes a blind spot in how most of us have built our commercial organizations.

It helps to look at how we got here. Each generation of go-to-market solved the previous generation's bottleneck, and in doing so created a new one.

In the first generation, one account manager did nearly everything: found prospects, ran discovery, demonstrated the product, priced it, closed it and looked after the customer afterwards. Growth meant hiring more sellers. It worked until it didn't, because no single person can be excellent at a dozen different jobs.

The second generation broke that work into specialized roles (SDRs, account executives, customer success) connected by handoffs. Growth meant hiring more SDRs. That model is under strain today. Buyers do much more of their research on their own, they actively avoid vendors who send irrelevant outreach, and every handoff loses some context about the customer.

In a recent development, the third generation, which I think is just beginning, combines people and AI agents in a deliberately engineered system. Agents take on the repetitive, always-on work. People concentrate on strategy, trust and judgment. And a fresh discipline, GTM engineering, designs and continuously improves the system that connects them.

Figure 2: Three generations of go-to-market: the full-stack seller, the assembly line, and humans plus AI agents.

In a recent development, if prospecting, qualification and first-round discovery increasingly become software capabilities, it is reasonable to ask what the long-term economic case is for the traditional SDR-heavy model. I don't think the answer is "no humans." I think the answer is different humans doing different, more valuable work.

Sources indicate that the term "GTM engineer" has spread quickly, and I understand the skepticism. Plenty of people hear it and think of someone wiring enrichment tools to an email sequencer.

That is part of the job, but it misses the point. Traditional revenue operations focused on processes, systems, reporting and coordination. GTM engineering adds something new: the ability to build and continuously improve the technical systems through which a company finds, serves and grows its customers.

In practice, a capable GTM engineering function:.

Industry observers note that the strongest GTM engineers rarely start as pure software engineers. They tend to come from commercial roles: a seller or marketing-ops person who couldn't stop optimizing their own workflow. You cannot automate well what you have never done yourself.

According to recent reports, here is a simple example of the mindset. Imagine a customer who bought a product three months ago but has never completed the integration needed to get value from it. In a traditional model, someone in customer success might notice at the next quarterly review, if at all. A GTM engineering approach treats that as a signal worth designing for. The system detects the incomplete setup from product data, checks that it has the right customer and that nobody is already helping them, pulls the approved implementation guidance, and proposes help, not an upsell, to the account owner. Then it measures whether customers who received that help actually activated faster than similar customers who didn't.

Sources indicate that none of those steps is exotic on its own. What is fresh is treating the whole path, from signal to action to measured outcome, as a product that gets engineered, tested and improved over time.

This is the distinction I find most useful when talking with other executives.

AI-assisted GTM makes existing work faster. AI drafts outreach, summarizes meetings, enriches leads and recommends next steps. It is valuable, it is available today, and most organizations should be doing it.

AI-native GTM redesigns how the work happens. Intelligent systems coordinate discovery, evaluation, evidence, workflows and decisions, with people providing oversight where judgment and accountability matter.

The difference is not the technology. It is the ambition. AI-assisted GTM improves the efficiency of the existing commercial model. AI-native GTM questions whether that model is still the right one.

The evidence supports taking this seriously. Field studies of generative AI show strong gains on well-defined tasks and weaker (sometimes worse) judgment on tasks just beyond what the models handle reliably. And while executive surveys consistently find that most people feel personally more productive with AI, only a small share of companies report meaningful impact on profit. The ones that do tend to have one thing in common: they redesigned the workflow end to end rather than adding AI to the old one.

Renewals illustrate the difference well. An AI-assisted renewal process gives the account manager a better summary before the call: usage trends, open tickets, a suggested talking point. Useful, but the process is unchanged. An AI-native renewal process starts months earlier. It continuously watches whether the customer is realizing the value they bought, intervenes when adoption stalls, assembles evidence of outcomes as they happen, and brings a human in at the moments where a relationship conversation genuinely matters. By the time renewal arrives, the case has largely been made, by the customer's own results.

When I map out how commercial interactions are changing, I see three modes emerging side by side.

Figure 3: Commerce is splitting into three modes: human to human, human to agent, and agent to agent.

Recent findings suggest that the first is the one we know well: human to human, where trust, judgment and negotiation carry the day. The second is human to agent, where a buyer or seller uses AI to research, evaluate and act faster; clear, well-grounded answers win here. The third is agent to agent, where software on both sides handles discovery, evaluation and quoting. In that mode, verifiable evidence and trustworthy interfaces matter more than persuasion.

The third mode is where it gets genuinely upcoming, because the two agents represent competing interests. A buyer's agent is trying to optimize price, fit and risk. A seller's agent is trying to protect margin and make sure the customer is a good fit. That raises questions most GTM leaders have never had to answer. How do two agents that have never interacted establish trust? How does a seller verify what a buyer's agent is actually authorized to commit to? What prevents manipulation, whether through prompt injection or fabricated evidence? And at what point must the agents hand the conversation back to people?

As buyer-side agents mature, I expect them to negotiate standard terms within mandates their organizations have approved, escalating anything outside those limits to humans. That makes the traditional website only one of several front doors. Machine-readable product information, trusted APIs, verifiable evidence and agent-facing interfaces could become just as vital.

Picture what that front door looks like when the visitor is software. It is less a web page and more a trust gateway: it checks who the agent is and what it is allowed to commit to, serves only signed and current facts, and knows exactly when to hand the conversation to a person.

Figure 4: A trust gateway between a buyer's procurement agent and the seller's capability manifest, compliance proofs, pricing API, proof-of-value sandbox and seller agent.

In autonomous commerce, authorization is not just a security checkbox. It becomes part of your brand.

In a recent development, one point for the technologists: protocols such as MCP are excellent for connecting AI to tools and data, but on their own they don't solve identity, authorization or negotiation between companies. That gap is where an important new layer of commercial infrastructure will be built.

According to recent reports, sophisticated AI models are becoming widely accessible. Every competitor can buy roughly the same ones. That raises the question I find most important: if intelligence is available to everyone, where does sustainable differentiation actually come from? I see three answers.

It has been revealed that context. I've become convinced that generating more content isn't the answer. Buyers are getting better at recognizing generic AI writing, and when you sound like everyone else, they assume you are like everyone else. What matters is whether your systems understand the customer's situation well enough to offer something genuinely useful, and that depends on proprietary context: your experts' perspective, your outcome data, your knowledge of why deals are won and lost.

This matters most at the top of the tech giant. In many growth-stage businesses, the founder's or CEO's point of view is the differentiation, and buyers are judging the leader as much as the product. The temptation is to let AI produce that voice at scale. I think that's backwards. The better pattern is to treat an executive's voice as infrastructure: capture their best emails, call recordings, talks and strong convictions, encode that into the context every outbound agent works from, and let AI scale and personalize it, not invent it. A weekly recorded conversation about customers, product and competitors can feed a month of content without the executive writing a word. Then, for the moments that really matter, a human adds back some "friction": a strong opinion, a specific story, an imperfect but genuine phrase. Perfectly polished prose slides past people. Texture gets read.

According to recent reports, done well, this is a loop rather than a campaign. The voice is captured and encoded once, agents scale it, people roughen it where it counts, and what lands feeds back into the next round.

Figure 5: The executive voice loop: capture, encode, generate, roughen, distribute and measure.

Evidence. A compelling story will still matter. But as AI participates in purchasing decisions, I expect verifiable evidence to carry more weight: specifications that can be checked, certifications that can be authenticated, pricing that is structured, benchmarks that can be reproduced. Brand credibility may come to depend as much on this evidence infrastructure as on messaging.

This is especially true when the product being sold is itself AI. Buyers of agentic products have good reasons to be cautious (accuracy, security, privacy, hallucinations) and a slide can't settle those concerns. They want to see the system working in their own context, over time. That shifts the emphasis from proof of concept ("does it work?") to proof of value ("does it create value here?"). I'd expect more vendors to reserve proofs of value for well-qualified buyers, charge for them, and treat them as the first phase of implementation rather than a throwaway demo, while making lighter self-service experiences, such as interactive product tours and sandboxes, available much earlier in the journey.

Industry observers note that learning. What interests me more than how quickly a company deploys agents is how well it learns from the outcomes. The question I keep coming back to is not "how many agents can we deploy?" but "how fast is our commercial system learning what actually creates customer value?".

I've started to think of this as a commercial learning rate: the time it takes to go from hypothesis to validated result, the share of experiments that actually change a decision, and how quickly you retire what isn't working. It is a metric I now care about more than most activity metrics. Models are rented; learning rate is owned.

One design choice makes the difference between compounding and chaos: what the system learns should reach production through an evaluated, authorized release, not by quietly drifting on its own.

Figure 6: Governed learning loop: act, observe, evaluate, learn and pass a release gate.

To make that concrete: suppose two competitors both launch AI-driven outreach to mid-market manufacturers. The first measures replies and meetings, sees the numbers rise, and scales. The second holds back a comparison group, tracks which meetings turn into qualified opportunities and healthy customers, and discovers within a few weeks that one segment converts well while another produces meetings that go nowhere. It shuts down the weak motion, doubles down on the strong one, and feeds what it learned into its targeting and messaging. Six months later, both companies are "using AI." Only one of them has become meaningfully smarter about its market.

In a recent development, that is why I'd rather see a team retire three experiments that didn't work than launch ten latest agents. The retirements are evidence that learning is happening.

Recent findings suggest that consider what happens when a customer's agent can reach your product specifications, technical documentation, service history and pricing directly. The CRM will still matter as the system of record. But the more strategic asset becomes the intelligence layer that decides what evidence supports a conclusion, what is likely to happen next, and which actions are permitted. For any CTO or CAIO, the question becomes: who owns that intelligence layer, your enterprise or your vendors?

These are the three principles I would put on the wall for any team building this:.

In a recent development, underneath, I find it useful to think in three planes. The execution plane is where AI interprets signals and acts for a specific customer, right now. The control plane is where software, not the model, decides what it may do: identity, permissions, approvals, budgets and a kill switch. The learning plane asks whether the action was right and worth it, and proposes improvements only through reviewed releases. AI interprets; software governs.

Figure 7: Execution, control and learning planes for AI-native GTM: AI interprets, software governs.

Two architectural choices deserve particular care.

The first is build versus buy. I'd buy the commodity pieces (enrichment, email delivery, standard connectors, workflow tooling) and keep control of what differentiates you: your customer definitions, your signal-to-action logic, your approved claims, your evaluation sets and your experiment history. If all of that lives inside a vendor's platform, your commercial learning walks out the door the day you switch.

In a recent development, the second is ownership across the leadership team. In my view this only works as a shared charter. The commercial leader owns customer outcomes and adoption. The CTO owns architecture, reliability and security. The CAIO owns the intelligence layer: which decisions AI should make, which models and tools are used, and the evaluation standard every system must meet. Without that clarity, GTM AI tends to become either a collection of disconnected sales tools or a slow central IT project. Neither works.

There's an aspect of this transformation that I don't think gets enough attention: what happens to the people who built their careers around these processes?

Consider a young sales development representative joining an enterprise technology tech giant. Traditionally, that person would spend months researching accounts, preparing for calls, hearing objections and slowly learning how buying decisions really get made. That apprenticeship is how many of today's best sales leaders, solution architects and consultants learned their craft.

Much of that work can now be assisted, or done outright, by AI. From a productivity standpoint, that is exciting. But it creates a harder question: if AI handles the research, qualification and preparation, where does the next generation develop its commercial instincts? Early data already suggests that hiring of young workers into AI-exposed roles is slowing.

In a recent development, I think we have to be careful not to confuse removing repetitive work with removing the experiences through which people develop judgment. And in commercial roles the stakes are higher than in many technical ones. When a commercial system makes a silent error, it doesn't stay inside the company. It lands on your customer. We need people who can tell when the system has misread a situation.

That means being much more intentional about how we develop talent: simulated customer conversations before real ones, rotations that put junior staff on the cases the AI got wrong or wasn't sure about, supervised time with real customers, and recognition for people who catch the system's mistakes rather than those who simply approve its output fastest.

The role of managers changes too. A sales manager who spent much of the week inspecting activity reports and pipeline hygiene will increasingly be coaching people on judgment: when to trust the system's recommendation, when to override it, and how to handle the customer situations it can't. That is a more demanding job, and most organizations haven't yet thought about how to prepare their managers for it.

And for individuals, the most durable career move I can see is to combine deep commercial understanding with the ability to shape, question and improve these systems. People who can do both will be the ones designing how their organizations grow.

Recent findings suggest that I also expect the shape of teams to change. The middle layer of routine coordination will likely thin out. Value will concentrate in two places: people who can design and govern these systems, and people who earn trust in complex, high-stakes relationships. The future of GTM isn't about fewer humans. It's about redefining where human expertise creates the most value, and continuing to develop it.

It has been revealed that the most practical way I've found to think about this is by jobs to be done rather than job titles. Agents are well suited to work that benefits from being available around the clock, multilingual, fast and consistent: administrative tasks, scheduling, routing, monitoring, and coordinating with other agents. People are better placed for strategy, customer meetings, negotiation, building alignment inside a buying group, and overseeing the agents themselves. The organizations that redesign roles around that split first, instead of simply bolting agents onto existing job descriptions, will set the cost and quality curve everyone else has to match.

Disruption is only half the story. Some of the biggest opportunities may come from infrastructure that enterprises don't yet realize they need. These are possibilities rather than proven categories, but they are the ones I'm watching:.

For professionals, the opportunity is just as real. Anyone who combines commercial experience with the ability to build, govern and evaluate these systems is going to be in demand for years.

According to recent reports, it's worth separating what is possible today from what is still emerging. Today, AI can reliably assist with research, drafting, summarization, enrichment and routing. Agents can take on well-bounded tasks inside clear rules. What is not yet reliable is long, multi-step commercial work without supervision: public benchmarks of agents on realistic CRM tasks show they still struggle as tasks get longer and more ambiguous. The gap is less about the models themselves and more about tool design, data, identity and evaluation.

Sources indicate that before choosing tools, I'd also choose where to aim. A common mistake is to start from the technology and look for somewhere to apply it, or to automate a process that was broken in the first place, paving the cowpath. A better starting point is the activity itself: how painful it is today, and how much a different outcome would be worth. I find three levels of ambition useful. You can optimize an activity, doing the same work faster or more cheaply. You can amplify it, using AI to produce outcomes that were previously uneconomic, like continuously refreshed competitive intelligence or a value model for every account. Or you can reinvent it, changing both how the work is done and what it delivers, as with an agent-run evaluation or a pipeline engine with no manual prospecting layer. Experiment in the first category to build skill; place your massive bets where pain and potential value are both highest. And give every cross-functional workflow a named owner, because the biggest gains usually sit between departments, not inside them.

I would also be cautious about giving commercial agents broad autonomy before we have confidence in their reliability, their decision boundaries and how they handle exceptions. Autonomy should be earned in stages:.

And measure what matters: incremental profit, how promptly customers get useful answers, the accuracy of what the system claims, and your commercial learning rate, not the number of agents deployed or emails sent.

Two risks are specific to putting an optimizing system in front of a market, and I think they deserve more attention than they get. The first is drift toward the wrong goal: a system rewarded for booked meetings or short-term conversion can quietly attract poorer-fit customers and hurt economics downstream. The second is a narrowing view of the market: a system that only acts on what has worked before stops exploring, and slowly loses sight of segments it never tried. Both are avoidable, by measuring lifecycle outcomes rather than activity, and by deliberately reserving some effort for exploration, but only if leaders know to look for them.

Sources indicate that predictions age badly, so treat these as hypotheses to test rather than forecasts. They're the ones I keep returning to.

Sources indicate that if even two of these come true, the companies best placed won't be the ones with the most AI tools. They'll be the ones that made themselves easy for machines to understand and safe for machines to trust.

Recent findings suggest that when I think about the future of GTM engineering, I don't see it as just another function inside sales or revenue operations. I see it becoming part of the enterprise's intelligence architecture: the way customer understanding, product capability, commercial decisions and AI-driven execution are connected into something that actually creates value.

According to recent reports, I don't believe anyone has all the answers yet. There are real open questions about trust, accountability, customer relationships and how much decision-making we should delegate to machines. But one thing is becoming clearer to me: deploying AI into existing commercial processes is only the beginning. The bigger opportunity is to rethink how those processes should work in the first place.

The next competitive advantage won't be the AI model. It will be the enterprise built around it.

So here is the question I'd put to every leadership team:.

Is your organization ready to be evaluated by a buyer's AI agent, or are you still optimizing only for human readers?

If you're working through this inside your own organization, I'd genuinely like to hear what you've found hardest.

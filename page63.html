Che cosa succede quando il software che oggi vi procura lavoro diventa anche il sistema che può sostituirvi?

Non è più una domanda fantascientifica.

Per anni abbiamo raccontato le piattaforme di ride-hailing come un'innovazione capace di mettere in contatto passeggeri e autisti. Un'applicazione, un algoritmo di assegnazione, un sistema di pagamento e una commissione.

Un modello apparentemente semplice.

Ma dietro quell'interfaccia esiste un'architettura economica molto più importante: quella che determina chi controlla la domanda, i prezzi e la relazione con il cliente.

Una piattaforma può organizzare milioni di spostamenti senza possedere ogni automobile e senza assumere direttamente ogni conducente.

Il suo vantaggio competitivo è un altro: controlla il punto di accesso del passeggero al servizio.

Naturalmente, i modelli differiscono tra operatori e mercati. Non tutte le piattaforme determinano il prezzo nello stesso modo.

Ma il principio economico resta:.

Chi controlla la domanda possiede un potere negoziale enorme su chi fornisce il servizio.

Tecnicamente, una piattaforma di mobilità dispone già di alcuni componenti fondamentali per gestire un servizio automatizzato:.

Nel ride-hailing tradizionale, il servizio fisico è fornito da un conducente.

In uno scenario robotaxi, parte di quel lavoro viene affidata a un veicolo autonomo, mentre restano necessari gestione della flotta, manutenzione, assistenza, supervisione e altre attività operative.

Il cambiamento più significativo è che l'interfaccia del passeggero può rimanere esattamente la stessa.

Il cliente apre l'applicazione, indica la destinazione, visualizza un prezzo e prenota.

Quello che cambia è chi, o che cosa, esegue la corsa.

Le leggi disciplinano il trasporto, le autorizzazioni, la sicurezza e le condizioni di esercizio.

Un mercato nel quale il passeggero appartiene commercialmente alla piattaforma rende il fornitore fisico del servizio più facilmente sostituibile.

Oggi quel fornitore è un tassista, un conducente NCC o una flotta.

Domani, nei mercati dove tecnologia e normative lo permetteranno, potrà essere anche un veicolo autonomo.

La tecnologia non è necessariamente il nemico del professionista.

Un sistema moderno può gestire richieste, pagamenti, disponibilità, localizzazione, tariffe e dispatching senza sottrarre all'operatore ogni decisione economica.

Per realizzare un modello di questo tipo servono alcuni principi architetturali.

Il conducente deve poter definire le condizioni economiche del proprio servizio nei limiti delle disposizioni applicabili.

Prima di accettare una corsa, il professionista dovrebbe conoscere il compenso previsto e le commissioni.

Non crediamo che il progresso tecnologico debba essere fermato.

Crediamo però che sia necessario distinguere tra digitalizzare un settore e trasferirne il controllo economico a pochi intermediari.

I robotaxi continueranno a evolversi.

Ma per i professionisti del trasporto esiste una decisione che non può aspettare il 2030.

Continuare a utilizzare la tecnologia come strumento della propria impresa, oppure lasciare che sia la piattaforma a determinare il futuro della propria impresa.

Q: My 16-year-old doesn’t seem to be motivated to do anything beyond hanging out with friends and playing video games. When I bring up the prospect of college, he says he doesn’t care. What should I do?

According to recent reports, A: As parents, we want to set our high schoolers up for success. Sources indicate that “While it’s not appropriate for parents to be too directive, they can help their teenager reflect on what they liked and didn’t like and what felt meaningful and not so meaningful about their various experiences,” states Bronk. “Having someone to debrief with can help them deepen their reflection and more quickly identify a purposeful direction.”.

According to recent reports, research suggests that when teens are guided by their own interests, free of external pressures, they’re more likely to take charge of their own career development. But when parents are overly involved in their adolescents’ lives, says Bronk, young people don’t have an opportunity to cultivate a sense of agency, and this can make career development planning a substantial challenge.

Instead of trying to manage their path, says Bronk, parents should simply “be curious.” Ask your child: What interests you? What feels satisfying for you?

Sources indicate that “This kind of questioning can scaffold and deepen their young person’s reflections, and reflection is a key component of figuring out what kind of path a young person wants to follow,” she indicates. “Usually, what holds their interest is something they’re good at and care about, and that allows them to make a meaningful difference in the broader world,” she explains.

Encourage your teen to ask people he knows about their jobs, what they entail, and what they studied to get there.

Finally, don’t assume that your child’s lack of direction is apathy. Whatever their interest is, “work with it, not against it,” says Bronk. “At the same time, be patient. People who scored higher on extraversion tended to have lower C-reactive protein, and much of that association came from how active they remarked they were.

In fact, being active was more strongly associated with lower C-reactive protein than any other characteristic the researchers examined.

Being talkative indicated a small association in the opposite direction, while being lively was associated with lower levels of both inflammatory markers.

In several of the studies, the association with being active held up even after the researchers took other aspects of extraversion into account.

Being active and hardworking may both reflect how much energy people have for everyday tasks.

In earlier research, people who scored higher on the activity aspect of extraversion or the achievement-striving aspect of conscientiousness tended to have better aerobic capacity during a brisk walking test (Terracciano et al., 2013).

Regular physical activity could help explain part of the association with inflammation.

The relationship could run in the other direction, too, if people with higher inflammation feel less energetic and come to see themselves as less active or lively.

Industry observers note that when a major artist dies, the tributes that come pouring in often gloss over personal shortcomings, whether the failure to complete educational degrees or the abandonment of wives and children. But the memorial to Betye Saar this week at the Getty Center did just the opposite, setting into relief how powerfully she managed to balance—even integrate—acts of mothering and artmaking. (She also managed to earn her bachelor’s degree at the University of California, Los Angeles in 1949, a time when there were few Black students.).

Industry observers note that earlier this week the Getty filled its largest, 450-seat auditorium with fellow artists, curators, friends and family members of the artist, who died last July only four days short of her 100th birthday. The tone was sombre and celebratory, ending with her brother-in-law Bennie Maupin on saxophone and the band Bayou Bump leading a procession, as Saar had wanted.

Sources indicate that one of the most powerful tributes came from her eldest nephew, Channing Johnson, who described her as a “true” if untraditional matriarch. “She was as impactful and skilful as a family matriarch as she was a gifted artist. She gathered us together whether it was in a back yard or public park. She kept our family history in the forefront of our minds and in the public eye—you can literally see our family in her artwork,” he noted, referring to assemblages where she incorporated family snapshots or memorabilia.

Johnson also celebrated Saar’s sense of humour and adventure, noting that she learned to belly dance early on and highlighted off her dance moves in June when she was honoured by the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA) at its Party in the Garden gala.

His tribute followed one by MoMA director Christophe Cherix, who recalled Saar’s studio in Laurel Canyon being “unlike any other studio I had ever seen…overflowing with treasures, almost archaeological, with objects patiently waiting sometimes years or even decades to be gathered, recognised [and] assembled into works of art”.

Betye Saar, Girl Children, 1964 © Betye Saar, courtesy of the artist and Roberts Projects, Los Angeles.

Saar’s longtime home and studio in Laurel Canyon is where she developed her deeply spiritualist, Black-centred, female-empowered vision through printmaking and assemblage, most famously using a windowpane to organise the esoteric, celestial images that make up the 1969 work Black Girl’s Window. It is also where she raised her three daughters: the sculptor Alison Saar, the mixed media artist Lezley Saar and the writer Tracye Saar. Early on they liked to pretend the wheel of their mother’s printing press was the helm of a pirate ship, and they have said in interviews they are happy they survived without losing a finger or hand.

The veteran Los Angeles collector Joy Simmons, who recalled bringing her week-old daughter Naima to a Saar opening at the Jan Baum gallery in 1981 (“I was not going to miss this opportunity to celebrate a Black woman opening up at a white woman’s gallery”), described the Saar creative lineage, which extends to grandchildren who work in the arts today.

Industry observers note that “To Alison, Lezley, Tracye and the whole family: I’ve watched three generations of Saar women make this city’s art history something special. Betye’s work belongs to museums now,” Simmons said, “but I know her proudest moment and her proudest accomplishment is all of you.”.

When we first imagined Connecting on Campus, we wanted to find places where students were actually practicing the skills and qualities that make connection across differences possible, and to make those moments visible.

As I spent time with these students and their stories, and as we shaped the episodes in the edit, I found myself thinking more and more about choice. Across the series, you see students staying curious when they could shut down, listening to understand someone else’s experience, sharing their own perspective while making space for someone else’s, or finding the courage to remain in a difficult conversation.

In our Dickinson College episode, that choice highlights up in a moment that could have easily passed. Ryan Thies recounts meeting Matthew Craig at a Halloween party during his freshman year. Matthew was dressed as Donald Trump. Ryan could have seen that costume, made an assumption about who Matthew was, and walked away. Instead, he leaned in and got to know him. An unlikely connection became a friendship, and eventually the two chose to become roommates. Ryan’s choice changed what became possible between them. That choice reflects the kind of empathy we talk about in our Bridging Differences work: the deliberate, and often difficult, choice to engage with someone else’s experience even when it feels unfamiliar or challenges our own worldview.

The choices look different in our Bowie State University and University of Maryland episodes—and the stakes are obviously very different, too. Here, courage looks like choosing to show up and engage when discomfort, conflict, and fear might make it easier—and completely understandable—to withdraw. It’s the decision to say: This matters. I’ll attend. I’ll represent. I’ll listen. I’ll do my part.

It has been revealed that I came away thinking that “bridging” is sometimes misunderstood as compromise. Across all four videos, something more interesting emerges: You can build a relationship across a difference without necessarily resolving the difference itself.

At Iliff School of Theology, students imagine very different versions of America without being asked to arrive at one shared vision. At Dickinson, Republicans and Democrats engage seriously with each other without abandoning their political beliefs. And at Bowie State University and the University of Maryland, students move beyond assumptions, allow themselves to be vulnerable, ask difficult questions, and really listen to people whose backgrounds and experiences differ from their own. The circumstances are very different, but the lesson is consistent: Bridging isn’t about erasing what separates us. It’s about developing the curiosity, trust, and courage to stay connected across those differences.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that some forms of distress at work are easy to recognize because they interrupt someone’s daily performance and the work of those around them: For example, a person on your team becomes visibly exhausted, they are irritable, or they’re frequently absent. But other forms of distress are harder to see precisely because the work continues uninterrupted.

It has been revealed that it’s common to talk about burnout at work and to recognize it in others and in ourselves. But a fresh paper of mine with David S. Smith examines a less visible and less understood form of disengagement at work: rustout.

In a recent development, rustout, as we studied it, often involves being underutilized, intellectually stagnating, and feeling a deep-seated sense of unfulfillment. It can arise when people feel their work no longer offers enough meaning or challenge to keep them intellectually and creatively engaged. But because it’s different from burnout, the solutions are also different—both for individuals and for organizations.

I’ve presented this research at numerous conferences, and each time I have been approached by professionals who say, “That’s what I’ve been going through, but I never had a name for it until now.”.

These people, young and older, are committed to their work, they are hardworking, and they haven’t stopped caring. It is often the case that they care very much, and they meet expectations set before them. They are often the ones who are called upon because they are reliable and highly conscientious.

Industry observers note that if you look around your workplace, you probably know many people who fit this bill. The problem is not a loss of effort on the part of the individual; it is a breakdown in the relationship between effort and meaningful work. From my study of this topic, rustout is really about the decline in professional vitality, when a person’s capacities are not used in ways that feel meaningful to them.

It has been revealed that in our research, participants described experiences shaped by administrative overload, reduced autonomy, and a misalignment between their professional aspirations and assigned tasks. Many people highlighted feeling professionally stuck: “Sadly, the elements I love about the job . . . are often overshadowed by the other administrative aspects of the role,” “I feel like I’m feeding the machine,” and “I’m a square peg in a round hole.”.

It has been revealed that they highlighted a sense that the work they were doing no longer drew sufficiently on their talents: “The better you are at it, the more of the administrative side of the job you get. It’s lacking opportunities to be creative.”.

Rustout can be quite psychologically disorienting. From the outside, a person may appear successful and competent. There is no question about their productivity; they meet set targets, but could do so “with [their] eyes closed.” However, they may feel increasingly detached from the work that once animated them. Their distress isn’t dramatic; it’s a slow process of psychological erosion.

Recent findings suggest that in many ways, rustout overlaps with burnout, but they are not the same. Burnout involves prolonged negative stress that isn’t adequately managed. It’s about too much—working too much, caring too much, too much pressure, and too little time to recuperate. In contrast, rustout is associated with too little positive stress or challenge over time, too little stimulation, and limited opportunities for individual growth.

However, while burnout is part of organizations’ mental health literacy, rustout rarely gets a mention. And this is problematic—both for the individual and the organization. “I often feel I have produced nothing at the end of the week, and there is no sense of development,” one person stated.

Sources indicate that some will stay in their roles out of a sense of duty (and for other practical reasons like salary or seniority), but others will choose to leave. Another noted, “I am functioning at my job, but I’m not thriving, and I am looking elsewhere.”.

Recent findings suggest that rustout often hides behind competence, and the reliable person is often given more of the same work. The person who does not complain is assumed to be coping and doing OK. The person who stabilizes others and is a good team player is rarely asked whether they themselves feel developed or challenged.

Recent findings suggest that one person said, “I have no experience of ever having a conversation with anyone around it. No line manager ever named it, it has never been mentioned in a communal setting or in a one-to-one setting. . . . No one has ever asked, ‘Do you feel fulfilled or do you feel you have opportunities to use your skills and talents in a meaningful way?’”.

In a recent development, it’s easy to see how doing overtime and being dependable can become a trap. Eventually, the employee becomes valued less for what they might become and more for what they can continue to absorb—they won’t “rock the boat” by opening the can of worms that is their dissatisfaction. “You put up, and shut up,” one person remarked.

Industry observers note that meanwhile, many workplaces are organized to detect individual or team shortcomings (or failure) more readily than stagnation. If someone stops performing, the problem becomes visible. However, if someone continues to perform while becoming inwardly disengaged, the organization may see no problem at all. And if anyone notices their dissatisfaction, they may not openly discuss it, as another of my interviewees reported. “It suits everyone not to talk about it; when everyone is working and doing their jobs—then don’t disturb anything. Everything is cosy.”.

Participants experiencing rustout described barriers that limited their ability to use their strengths or pursue professional growth, which are particularly notable for having a sense of meaning and fulfillment at work.

For many people, their working life gives them a sense of identity and purpose, but when work no longer offers that sense of meaning, their relationship with the job can become fractured or feel “off.” In some cases, they can’t put their finger on what is happening, and that might be why I often hear after presentations, “I never had a name for it.”.

Naming rustout won’t solve the concern, but it can help the person to understand why they feel underutilized or dissatisfied. Naming rustout can help people understand this isn’t a personal failure but an experience that is shaped by the design or conditions of work (much like burnout). It is also shaped by the culture of work, particularly when there is a resistance to change or a failure to see the value in helping people to flourish.

According to recent reports, from my discussions with participants and colleagues about rustout, it is clear that it isn’t a pre-retirement phenomenon. It seems to arise for those who are experienced, perhaps mid-career, and those who have been in their role for a long time.

One participant explained, “I have been doing [this role] for years. There must be better ways to organize things to ensure the expertise of experienced staff is used in more effective ways.” Others said, “It’s the seven-, eight-, nine-year mark when there isn’t an opportunity to be stimulated,” and “It’s the seven-year itch, you’ve been doing something creative and then you reach a point where you are doing the same thing on repeat.”.

It has been revealed that in such ways, we can’t dismiss rustout as a consequence of aging in the workplace. It can happen whenever people are pigeonholed into a system that no longer offers them meaningful development. Although some repetition and routine are an expected part of every job, there should also be a sense that your abilities and talents matter in your role. That there is a future and something exciting to work toward.

Sources indicate that the language of burnout has helped many people name distress that had previously been normalized or dismissed. It has also helped organizations take workplace well-being more seriously. But any dominant mental health vocabulary can become limiting when it starts to stand in for the whole field of experience. If burnout is the only category available for occupational distress, then recovery is often imagined in terms of rest and resilience.

They may be necessary, but they are not always sufficient. A person experiencing rustout may not simply need less work; they may need different work: work that restores their sense of intellectual engagement and curiosity. As Maya Angelou once mentioned, “We aren’t designed to be static”—that could apply to all aspects of life, including the workplace.

If you are experiencing rustout, it would be good to do an inventory of what is happening for you. Do you feel fulfilled in your role? Do you have opportunities to use your talents? Do you gain some sense of meaning from your role? Are there ways to seek renewal in your work? What might this look like, and can you have this conversation with your line manager or boss?

In a recent development, for organizations, the questions are also direct. Look around your team. Are you giving your most dependable employees opportunities for development? Do you continually lean on their “safe pair of hands” because it is easier than asking someone else to learn the ropes? Are you willing to put the conversation about professional dissatisfaction on the table? How can you help your team to flourish?

Recent findings suggest that that is the unsettling truth at the heart of the rustout. A person can be conscientious yet disengaged, reliable yet underused, competent but unfulfilled. They can be good at their job and still feel themselves receding from it. The silence around rustout allows it to thrive, which is why I often hear the same response after conference presentations: “I thought it was just me.” I think there is a particular loneliness in that sentence, the loneliness of continuing to do the work, while privately wondering why it no longer feels like yours.

He’s flawed and sometimes lashes out as any male chimpanzee might do, but throughout the seven years we’ve studied him, no challenges have managed to displace him. Doug looks out for others, and in turn they look out for him. Over my time studying many groups of chimpanzees, it seems the most tolerant social environments emerge from the leadership of strong, stable, and generally fair alphas who allow others access to resources and who keep tight bonds with their closest allies.

Research across five European cultures found that the area of the body you’ll let someone touch maps almost directly onto the strength of your emotional bond with them. There is cultural variation. Touch conveys intimacy and trust. Yet holding someone’s hand or shoulder can also be used to intimidate or harass. We could learn from primates to stop withholding touch where it would help most and back down from reaching for it where it shouldn’t be used at all.

One key area where more touch helps is sports. A study of all 30 NBA teams during the 2008–09 season found that the teams who touched more early in the season, through fist bumps, chest bumps, hugs, and high fives, went on to perform better and play more cooperatively later, even after controlling for player salaries and pre-season expectations. We are a part of the natural world like all other animals, needing to balance our cooperative and competitive urges. And the evidence suggests we sit at the better end of that range far more often than we might think.

When researchers simply told participants that most people worldwide see climate change as a threat, belief in others’ willingness to cooperate ticked up, and actual cooperation followed it, slightly, but in the right direction.

Building on the lessons of primate relationships is easier than it seems.

They may groom, or play, or even sit in contact. More often than not they just like to hang out near their close partners. Just being close to one another is meaningful.

Reconciliation and comforting are commonplace in humans and primates, but it’s much easier to prevent escalation than it is to smooth things over after a fight.
